## The question in plain language

Make numbers by selecting **distinct factorials** and adding them. For example,

$$
1!+3!+4!=1+6+24=31.
$$

Let $A$ contain every finite such subset sum. [Erdős Problem #1108](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1108) asks whether $A$ has only finitely many perfect $k$th powers for each fixed $k\ge2$, and whether it contains only finitely many *powerful* numbers (integers whose prime divisors all occur squared).

These are different problems. A factorial sum can be powerful without being a perfect power.

## A small example

Take factorials

$$
1!,2!,\ldots,7!=1,2,6,24,120,720,5040.
$$

They have $2^7=128$ distinct subset sums, including the empty sum $0$.

Exact integer-square tests find the following positive squares among those 128 values:

$$
1,9,25,121,144,729,841,5041,5184.
$$

Exact prime factorization finds **14 positive powerful** values, including these squares and nonsquare examples such as $8$ and $27$.

These findings show that the finite factorial-sum set has repeated-power structure. They do **not** decide whether there are finitely or infinitely many such values when arbitrarily large factorial indices are allowed.

## What is known

The original 1988 question is still **open**, including the special problem of whether infinitely many squares have the form $1+n!$.

The catalogue reports an important partial result of Brindza and Erdős (1991): if the number of factorial summands is a **fixed number $r$**, then a powerful sum of $r$ distinct factorials has a bounded largest factorial index, where the bound can depend on $r$.

That does not settle the full problem, because the set $A$ permits an *unbounded* number of summands as the largest factorial index grows.

Erdős also discussed a related problem of Mahler involving sums of distinct powers $k^j$ for a fixed base $k$. The example

$$
1+7+7^2+7^3=400=20^2
$$

belongs to that different setting and must not be confused with factorial sums.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2379](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2379#issuecomment-6168369685) records the fixed-summand versus unrestricted-summand distinction and reproduces the exact finite-square and powerful-number tables.

The 1991 published proof and precise treatment of $0!$, $1!$, and the empty sum have not been independently audited. In the bounded check, the factorial indices are $1,\ldots,7$, with $0$ allowed only as an empty subset sum and excluded when counting *positive* powerful numbers.

## What remains

Read Brindza and Erdős's 1991 theorem in full, confirm its indexing conventions and how the bound depends on the number of summands, and assess the still-open unrestricted problem. Larger finite searches may test hypotheses but cannot settle finiteness of the infinite intersection.
