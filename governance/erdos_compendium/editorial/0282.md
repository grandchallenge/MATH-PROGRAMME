## The question in plain language

An Egyptian fraction writes a rational number as a sum of unit fractions, fractions whose numerator is $1$. Problem 282 asks about a very specific greedy way to make such a sum. Start with a reduced fraction $x=a/b$ satisfying $0<a<b$ and with odd denominator $b$. At a stage where the positive remainder is $r$, choose the smallest odd positive integer $n$ such that

$$
\frac{1}{n}\le r,
$$

but impose the crucial rule that a denominator already used may not be chosen again. Subtract $1/n$ and repeat. The question is whether this process always reaches remainder $0$ after finitely many steps. In symbols, the proposed conclusion is that for every reduced $a/b$ with $0<a<b$ and $b$ odd, the odd greedy expansion terminates.

The word “greedy” matters twice. The next denominator is the least eligible one, not merely any odd denominator that works, and “eligible” means unused as well as large enough. A finite sum of distinct odd-denominator unit fractions is therefore not automatically the output of this algorithm. The problem concerns the behavior of one deterministic procedure for every allowed starting fraction.

This distinction also explains why the problem is subtle. The remainder decreases whenever a positive unit fraction is subtracted, but a decreasing sequence of positive rational remainders need not hit zero in finitely many steps. The denominators can grow, and the question is about exact finite termination rather than convergence toward zero.

## A small example

Take $x=2/3$. The first eligible odd denominator is $3$, giving a remainder of $1/3$. The denominator $3$ is now unavailable, so the next choice is $5$, not $3$ again. The successive choices are $3$, $5$, $9$, and $45$:

$$
\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{3},
\qquad
\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{5}=\frac{2}{15},
$$

$$
\frac{2}{15}-\frac{1}{9}=\frac{1}{45},
\qquad
\frac{1}{45}-\frac{1}{45}=0.
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{2}{3}=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{5}+\frac{1}{9}+\frac{1}{45}.
$$

Every denominator is odd and every denominator occurs once. This example is also a warning about the wording. If reuse were allowed, one could write $2/3=1/3+1/3$, but that is not the odd greedy algorithm in the source problem. The exact choice at each line can be checked by comparing the reciprocal with the current remainder: after $1/3$, the unused odd denominator $5$ is the first one with reciprocal at most $1/3$; after $1/5$, the first such unused odd denominator is $9$; and after $1/9$, it is $45$.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham’s *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* gives the source formulation on p. 30, including the “not yet used” convention, attribution to Stein, and related questions about arithmetic progressions and square denominators. The broad subject has many positive representation theorems, but those theorems do not settle the greedy process.

For example, Graham’s *On Finite Sums of Unit Fractions* gives a criterion for representing a rational number as a finite sum of distinct unit fractions whose denominators lie in an arithmetic progression; the relevant concluding result is on pp. 206–207. Graham’s *On Finite Sums of Reciprocals of Distinct nth Powers* gives the general criterion in Theorem 4 and the square-denominator consequence in Corollary 1, p. 91. These results say when a representation exists. They do not say that repeatedly taking the least available denominator will find one.

The ordinary greedy algorithm with all positive denominators is much better behaved: the classical Fibonacci result gives finite termination in that unrestricted setting. Restricting the allowed denominators to the odd integers changes the problem. Pihko’s “Remarks on the ‘Greedy Odd’ Egyptian Fraction Algorithm” defines the odd procedure in (1.1), gives its expansion in (1.2), and records the corresponding uniform termination question as Open Problem 1.1 in *The Fibonacci Quarterly* 39 (2001), 221–227. Pihko’s 2010 sequel, p. 202 and §3, including Corollary 3.6, gives structural families with prescribed increasing numerator runs; that is meaningful progress about the process, not a proof of universal termination. Louwsma and Martino, *Rational Numbers with Two-Term Odd Greedy Expansion*, *Integers* 25 (2025), A46, classify the two-term case while leaving general termination open.

The authenticated RESULT/1 also reports a finite exact computation: 2,006 reduced fractions with odd denominator less than $100$ were tested, and all terminated in that tested range. The reported maximum was 18 terms, for $84/85$, with a largest denominator having 3,122 decimal digits. Such a computation is useful evidence and a source of examples, but no finite search settles a statement quantified over all odd denominators.

## What remains

The central unresolved task is a theorem proving or refuting finite termination for every reduced $a/b$ with $b$ odd under the exact unused-odd greedy rule. A bounded successor computation can still be valuable: enumerate all such fractions with $b\le199$, record each exact denominator sequence and remainder, and independently check minimality, oddness, distinctness, and the final identity. It should stop at the first counterexample or at the prescribed bound. Its acceptance test must be exact, and its conclusion must remain “verified through the bound,” never “proved for all inputs.”

The next mathematical step would need a mechanism that controls the changing remainder and the rapidly growing unused denominators, or a rigorously certified counterexample. Existing representation criteria do not supply that mechanism. Any stronger status would require source-semantic review and a genuine proof record, not an extrapolation from the computation or from the formal interface.

For orientation only, Problem 279 remains OPEN despite an unreviewed GPT-generated 2026 proof claim; that claim is not treated as a proof and must not be imported into the status of 282. A separate source issue also remains material: Problem 286 has an EXACT_BLOCKER involving a width coefficient stated as $(e-1)k$ in Erdős–Graham versus a different width formulation in Croot. That source-semantic conflict must be investigated, not silently erased or reused as evidence for 282.
