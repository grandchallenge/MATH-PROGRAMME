## The question in plain language

Can there be infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that **every** number of the form $n-2x^2$ is prime whenever $x$ is an integer and $2x^2<n$?

[Erdős Problem #1140](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1140) has a negative answer: only finitely many such integers exist, according to published results of Epure and Gică and earlier work.

The phrase **every integer $x$** includes $x=0$. Therefore the condition requires $n=n-2\cdot0^2$ itself to be prime. Omitting zero changes the problem.

## A small example

Checking every integer $x$ with $2x^2<n$ for each $2\le n\le2000$ yields exactly

$$
2,\ 5,\ 7,\ 13,\ 31,\ 61,\ 181,\ 199.
$$

These agree with the known list reported by the catalogue.

For example, $n=4$ might appear to work if we check only *positive* $x$, because $4-2=2$. But $x=0$ gives $4$, which is not prime. Similarly the misleading positive-only test admits some other composite candidates.

A finite search cannot rule out any exceptional integer larger than its cutoff.

## What is known

**Epure and Gică (2010, Theorem 4.1)** established that the only examples congruent to $1\bmod4$ are $5,13,61,181$.

For the class $n\equiv3\bmod4$, their arguments combined with **Mollin and Williams (1989)** imply that the known examples are $7,31,199$, with **at most one additional exception**. The exceptional possibility has not been excluded in the maintained account.

The existence of at most one extra integer is already enough to **disprove infinitude**, but it is not a proof that the eight known integers form the complete list.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated audit #2411](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2411#issuecomment-6181193050) verifies the list through 2,000 and explicitly corrects the $x=0$ convention. The general number-theoretic theorem remains externally sourced, not independently replayed.

## What remains

Review the complete Epure–Gică and Mollin–Williams arguments, identify the exceptional-case conditions precisely, and avoid asserting that the possible ninth example cannot exist without an additional proof.
