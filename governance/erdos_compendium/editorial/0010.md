## The question in plain language

[Erdős Problem #10](https://www.erdosproblems.com/10) asks whether **one fixed bound** on the number of powers of two suffices to express every sufficiently large integer as a prime plus those powers. Precisely: are there integers $K,N_0$ such that

$$
\forall n\ge N_0\quad
\exists p\text{ prime},\ t\le K,\ e_1,\ldots,e_t\in\mathbb N:
\quad n=p+\sum_{i=1}^{t}2^{e_i}\;?
$$

Exactly one prime is used. The number $t$ can be zero, exponent zero is permitted ($2^0=1$), and exponents may repeat. The bound $K$ concerns the **number of summands**, not how large an exponent may be. The global existence question remains open in the October 2026 reviewed source record.

## A small example

For a given prime $p\le n$, the minimum number of powers of two adding to $n-p$ equals the number of ones in its binary expansion, its *popcount*. Two copies of $2^e$ can be carried into a single $2^{e+1}$; repeated carrying reaches the unique binary expansion. Conversely that expansion is already a representation. Therefore

$$
n\text{ is representable with at most }K\text{ powers}
\iff \exists p\le n\text{ prime}:
\mathrm{popcount}(n-p)\le K.
$$

For example,

$$
50=11+32+4+2+1.
$$

A more stringent verified finite test from the [GCL #10 audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1279#issuecomment-6103628767) is

$$
1{,}117{,}175{,}146
=1{,}117{,}175{,}107+1+2+4+32.
$$

The audit reports deterministic verification that the first summand is prime, and a complete check of 462 candidate offsets showing this integer is **not** representable with three or fewer powers. It therefore needs exactly four under the stated rules. A single finite example does not constrain all sufficiently large integers.

## What is known

A density theorem associated with P. X. Gallagher, *Primes and powers of 2*, *Inventiones Mathematicae* **29** (1975), 125–142, says that for every $\varepsilon>0$ some $K(\varepsilon)$ permits representations for integers of lower density at least $1-\varepsilon$. The allowed number of powers depends on $\varepsilon$; even density arbitrarily close to one does not imply that **every sufficiently large integer** has a representation. This theorem interface is reported in the public registry; an exact original-text proof replay was not undertaken for this article.

Crocker's 1971 covering-congruence techniques give nonrepresentability results for restricted numbers of powers. An external 2026 formal-verification report concerns an infinite family of even integers not representable with **at most three** powers. Its reported Lean kernel result is for that narrower statement, and its publication review was marked draft: it is not a GCL MATHCERT certificate. Even a fully verified obstruction at three powers does not rule out an eventual fixed bound of four or more.

Related small-bound claims, including parity-sensitive conjectures, remain **conjectural**, not established universal representations.

## GCL's current state

The [GCL source audit #1279](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1279#issuecomment-6103628767) checks the pinned Formal Conjectures definition: a finite multiset of powers (including repetitions and exponent zero) plus a prime. The frozen headline uses **all integers $n\ge2$**, while later upstream uses **all sufficiently large integers**. Although worded differently, these two *existence of some bound* assertions are equivalent.

Indeed, suppose some $K$ works beyond $N_0$. Each of the finitely many integers $2\le n<N_0$ has *some* representation: use prime $2$ for even $n$, prime $3$ for odd $n\ge3$, and express the nonnegative remainder in binary. Take the maximum of $K$ and the finite number of powers required across this prefix. That larger bound represents every $n\ge2$. This is an elementary normalization, not a proof that such an eventual $K$ exists.

## What remains

A positive result needs **one** universal $K$ beyond a threshold. A negative result requires proving that **every** candidate $K$ fails for arbitrarily large $n$. Neither high-density results nor any fixed finite computation establishes either conclusion.

This article reports source-based mathematical exposition and finite checks, not a GCL-certified solution or independent replay of external Lean proofs. Editorial acceptance remains distinct from mathematical certification.
