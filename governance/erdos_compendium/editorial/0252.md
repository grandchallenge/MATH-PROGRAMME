## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, let $\sigma_k(n)$ be the sum of the powers indexed by $k$ of the positive divisors of $n$. Thus $\sigma_k(1)=1$ and $\sigma_k(6)=1^k+2^k+3^k+6^k$. The problem asks whether the rapidly convergent real number

$$
\alpha_k=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_k(n)}{n!}
$$

is irrational for every integer $k\geq 1$. The quantifier is important: this is one assertion for each positive integer $k$, not merely a question about one fixed numerical series. The factorial makes the tail small, but it does not make irrationality automatic; a convergent series with rational terms can still have a rational sum.

The normalized source statement above follows the pinned catalogue packet and the papers. The current maintained page writes the displayed sum without an explicit lower limit. Its surrounding discussion and cited literature make the intended range $n\geq1$ clear, but the missing bound is a source-fidelity defect worth recording rather than silently repairing.

## A small example

For $k=4$, direct divisor enumeration gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sigma_4(1),\ldots,\sigma_4(7))
  &=(1,17,82,273,626,1394,2402),\\
\sum_{n=1}^{7}\frac{\sigma_4(n)}{n!}
  &=\frac{106271}{2520}
   \mathrel{\approx}42.171031746.
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, $\sigma_4(6)=1+16+81+1296=1394$. The values and the fraction were independently reproduced with exact integer arithmetic:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import factorial

def sigma(k, n):
    return sum(d**k for d in range(1, n + 1) if n % d == 0)

values = [sigma(4, n) for n in range(1, 8)]
partial = sum((Fraction(sigma(4, n), factorial(n))
               for n in range(1, 8)), Fraction())
assert values == [1, 17, 82, 273, 626, 1394, 2402]
assert partial == Fraction(106271, 2520)
```

This is a finite check, not an irrationality proof. It neither controls the infinite tail nor distinguishes a rational sum from an irrational one. It is useful only as a reproducible sanity check on the normalization and the arithmetic used in the source arguments.

## What is known

The classical unconditional result is now exactly the finite range $1\leq k\leq4$.

- For $k=1$, Erdős and Straus's 1971 theorem on series with divisor-function numerators applies to the factorial specialization: their Theorem 2.26, p. 642, assumes a monotone integer sequence $a_n$ with $a_n\geq n^{1/2}$ eventually; taking $a_n=n$ gives the denominator $1\cdot2\cdots n=n!$. Their later 1974 paper, Theorem 1.1, p. 85, restates the earlier growth criterion in a broader rationality framework. These are borrowed published results, not a new proof here.
- For $k=2$, the historical record is Problem 4518 of Erdős and Kac, *American Mathematical Monthly* 60 (1953), p. 47, followed by Breusch's solution in volume 61 (1954), pp. 264–265. The bibliography in the pinned formal file conflates the problem and solution metadata by citing “61 (1954), Problem 4518”; the separate records are the safer source boundary.
- For $k=3$, Schlage-Puchta's theorem in *The Irrationality of a Number Theoretical Series* (2006), p. 455, proves irrationality unconditionally and also proves the implication for every positive integer $k$ from Schinzel's Hypothesis H. Friedlander, Luca, and Stoiciu independently prove the unconditional $k=3$ case in Theorem 1, p. 2, and their Theorem 2, p. 3, proves the analogous implication from the conjecture asserting simultaneous primality of $k$ affine forms.
- Pratt proves $\alpha_4$ irrational in “The Irrationality of a Divisor Function Series of Erdős and Kac,” arXiv:2209.11124v1 (2022), Theorem 1, p. 2. Pratt explicitly says that proving the cases $k\geq5$ appears to require new ideas.

Thus the unconditional open remainder can be stated precisely as

$$
\forall k\in\mathbb Z,\quad k\geq5\Longrightarrow\alpha_k\notin\mathbb Q.
$$

The conditional statements are different claims. Schinzel's Hypothesis H implies irrationality for every positive integer $k$ in Schlage-Puchta's theorem. The prime-tuple conjecture implies the same conclusion in Friedlander–Luca–Stoiciu. A conditional theorem is not a classical unconditional solution, and neither source is a formal proof in the provider snapshot.

## What remains

Mathematically, the central open task is to prove or refute irrationality for every fixed $k\geq5$, with the first unresolved case $k=5$. The published proofs use a rationality assumption, factorial denominator clearing, and carefully selected prime or almost-prime patterns. For $k=4$, Pratt's argument illustrates the depth: sieve estimates and exponential-sum input are combined to contradict the near-integrality forced by rationality. That method is a named published lemma chain, not an original argument supplied by this article.

For formalization, the smallest immediate obligation is semantic rather than heroic: prove that the source series and the provider's natural-number `tsum` denote the same real number, including the $n=0$ summand. A second obligation is to state the prime-tuple and Schinzel hypotheses in a form demonstrably equivalent to the papers' integer-polynomial formulations. Until those checks and an actual kernel replay are complete, the provider status should remain “statement scaffold with unproved declarations.”
