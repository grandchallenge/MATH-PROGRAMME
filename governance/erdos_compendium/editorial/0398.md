## The question in plain language

[Erdős Problem 398](https://www.erdosproblems.com/398) is the Brocard–Ramanujan problem. It asks whether the positive-integer solutions of

$$
n!+1=m^2
$$

are exactly $(n,m)=(4,5),(5,11),(7,71)$. Equivalently, it asks whether $n!=m^2-1$ has any other solution. The sign of $m$ is immaterial, so one may take $m$ to be a nonnegative integer; the factorial variable is a positive integer in the usual statement. The pinned Lean statement instead uses natural numbers for both variables and expresses the same intended solution set as

$$
\{n\mid \exists m,\ n!+1=m^2\}=\{4,5,7\}.
$$

That normalization is useful, but it is a statement of the conjecture, not a proof of it.

The catalogue calls the problem **falsifiable**. In the maintained catalogue's terminology, this means that the assertion is open but a counterexample would be finite data: one pair $(n,m)$ with $n!+1=m^2$ and $n\notin\{4,5,7\}$. It does not mean that the problem is decidable, disproved, or independent of a foundation such as Peano arithmetic or ZFC.

## A small example

The three familiar solutions can be checked exactly, without floating-point approximations:

$$
4!+1=24+1=25=5^2,
$$

$$
5!+1=120+1=121=11^2,
$$

and

$$
7!+1=5040+1=5041=71^2.
$$

The following short computation repeats the test with integer square roots for every $n$ from $0$ through $20$:

```python
from math import factorial, isqrt

hits = []
for n in range(0, 21):
    value = factorial(n) + 1
    root = isqrt(value)
    if root * root == value:
        hits.append((n, root, value))
print(hits)
```

Its exact output is `[(4, 5, 25), (5, 11, 121), (7, 71, 5041)]`. This verifies the three examples and excludes additional natural-number solutions only in that finite range. It cannot establish the universal assertion, because a possible counterexample could occur above $20$; nor does it reproduce the much larger published search discussed below.

## What is known

The live problem page describes the conjecture as unsolved and reports no other solutions below $10^9$, referring to Berndt and Galway's computation. The historical source listed there is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 77, where the problem is recorded. The page also records two kinds of conditional or restricted progress.

Overholt's one-page 1993 paper, *The Diophantine Equation $n!+1=m^2$*, in the *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* 25, p. 104, gives a finiteness result under a weak form of the Szpiro/ABC conjecture. “Only finitely many” is weaker than “only three”: even assuming that hypothesis, the cited result does not identify the complete solution set.

Naciri's 2025 paper gives unconditional finiteness in broad restricted families. Theorem 1(i), on p. 2 of the published PDF, says that for fixed $k\geq2$ there are finitely many solutions when $m-1$ or $m+1$ has all prime exponents below $k$; for the case $k=7$, the only possible pairs are the three known ones, using the Berndt–Galway computation below $10^9$. Theorem 1(ii) gives an analogous finite result when $m\pm1$ has fewer than a fixed number of distinct prime divisors, and states that the prime-power case leaves only $(4,5)$. These are meaningful restrictions on the equation, not a solution of the unrestricted problem.

## What remains

The central unresolved task is to prove that no further positive integer $n$ makes $n!+1$ a square, or to exhibit a new pair. A finite computation, even one reaching $10^9$, supplies a falsification test and valuable evidence but cannot close an unbounded universal claim. Conditional ABC/Szpiro arguments and Naciri's restricted-family theorems likewise leave the unrestricted boundary intact.

A bounded successor should therefore preserve the exact equation and first audit the implications of each restriction, rather than relabeling a conditional or finite result as a solution. A useful deliverable would be a source-linked comparison table for the unrestricted statement, the ABC/Szpiro finiteness theorem, the 7-free theorem, and the prime-power theorem, with every hypothesis and conclusion written explicitly. Acceptance should require the cited theorem/page and a reproducible finite check; falsification would be a counterexample or a demonstrated mismatch in a claimed reduction. The task stops when that comparison is complete or when a required primary text cannot be read.
