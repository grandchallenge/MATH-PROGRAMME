## The question in plain language



An Egyptian-fraction representation of $1$ writes one as a sum of distinct unit fractions, meaning reciprocals of positive integers. Erdős Problem #286 asks how tightly such a representation can be packed when it has exactly $k$ terms. If

$$
1=\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k},
$$

with $n_1<\cdots<n_k$, its width is the distance from the smallest to the largest denominator, $n_k-n_1$. The original Erdős–Graham source asks, in the notation of its set of representations with $n$ terms, whether the smallest possible width is

$$
(e-1)n+o(n)
$$

as $n$ tends to infinity. Here $o(n)$ means an error whose ratio to $n$ tends to zero; it is an asymptotic assertion, not an exact formula for small values.

The modern wording packages the same-looking idea as an interval question: for every sufficiently large $k$, can one find an interval of width $(e-1+o(1))k$ containing $k$ distinct positive integers whose reciprocals sum to $1$? The quantifier matters. “For every sufficiently large $k$” is stronger than “for infinitely many $k$,” and a change in the coefficient or in what the parameter measures changes the problem.

## A small example

For $k=3$, take the distinct denominators $2$, $3$, and $6$. Direct arithmetic gives

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16=\frac36+\frac26+\frac16=1.
$$

They lie in the interval $[2,6]$, whose width is $6-2=4$. A finite exact-fraction enumeration of triples drawn from $\{2,3,\ldots,200\}$ also returned width $4$ with the triple $(2,3,6)$. This is a checked illustration of the definition, not evidence for an asymptotic coefficient. In particular, comparing $4$ with $(e-1)\cdot3$ would say nothing reliable about the limit as $k$ grows.

The example also shows why “distinct” must be stated. Repeating a denominator would turn the problem into a different additive question, while the source definition uses a set of distinct denominators. Likewise, an interval may contain many integers, but the assertion only asks that the selected $k$ denominators be distinct members of it.

## What is known

The pinned source audit checked P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), printed p. 33. That source defines the family of distinct-denominator representations and records the minimum-width expression with coefficient $e-1$. The scan is the UCSD copy with SHA-256 `0cbf0c32f0ab1e1c71db5121a88bac905bf976c4a6ab6bb6d7d9cf9ddd184ed3`.

Croot’s primary paper, E. S. Croot III, “On unit fractions with denominators in short intervals,” *Acta Arithmetica* 99 (2001), 99–114, defines the same kind of set on p. 99. Its introduction states two Erdős–Graham questions: one about the largest possible smallest denominator, and one about minimum width asymptotic to $k$; it also says that the earlier questions were misstated. Those are not merely typographical details: the paper’s displayed parameterization is materially different from the printed Erdős–Graham coefficient recorded above.

Croot’s Main Theorem, pp. 100–101, says that for every fixed rational $r>0$ and every $N>1$, there are distinct integers $N<x_1<\cdots<x_k$ whose reciprocal sum is $r$ and whose largest denominator is at most

$$
\left(e^r+O_r\left(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\right)\right)N.
$$

The paper proves this using Propositions 1 and 2 on pp. 103–104, with the construction completed on p. 105. It explicitly describes the result as solving the Erdős–Graham questions for infinitely many $k$. That is a genuine published theorem, but it does not by itself establish the current formulation for all sufficiently large $k$, and the source audit did not resolve whether its corrected width statement is semantically identical to the original $(e-1)k$ statement.

## What remains

The first remaining task is source adjudication, not a new slogan about the problem being solved. A careful comparison must align Erdős–Graham’s $n$, Croot’s $k$ and $N$, the smallest and largest denominators, and the definition of interval width. It must decide whether Croot’s “misstated” correction is a correction of the coefficient, of the parameterization, or of both. Until that comparison is settled from the primary text, the catalogue’s `proved` label cannot be used to erase the exact blocker.

The second task is a bounded formalization review. If the intended target is the original theorem, the formal statement must be shown to match its source-level quantifiers and coefficient. If the intended target is Croot’s corrected result, it should be encoded as that result, with the “infinitely many $k$” consequence kept distinct from a claim for all sufficiently large $k$. Either route needs a checked semantic bridge before it can support a proof-status statement.

The published Croot theorem remains valuable evidence and may support a reusable lemma about representing a fixed rational in a short multiplicative interval. It is not, without the missing comparison, a certificate for the exact registered #286 statement. The stopping condition for this documentary pass is therefore precise: resolve the coefficient and quantifier comparison from primary sources, or retain `EXACT_BLOCKER` and identify the missing source argument.
