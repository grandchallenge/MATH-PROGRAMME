## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $m$, let $\omega(m)$ be the number of *different* primes dividing $m$. Thus $\omega(12)=2$, because only $2$ and $3$ occur. Let $\Omega(m)$ count prime factors with multiplicity: $\Omega(12)=3$, since $12=2^2\cdot3$. The original problem asks whether there is one absolute constant $C>0$ and infinitely many positive integers $n$ for which every positive shift $k$ obeys

$$
\omega(n+k)\leq Ck.
$$

The quantifiers matter. The same $C$ must work for every $k\geq1$ and for all members of one infinite set of $n$; it is not a constant chosen afresh for each shift or each $n$. The notation $\omega(n+k)\ll k$ is shorthand for precisely this kind of absolute-constant bound.

The printed source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 61, where the notation for the prime-factor count is not defined in the local problem sentence. The maintained page [Erdős Problem 248](https://www.erdosproblems.com/248) also cites P. Erdős, “Remarks on some problems in number theory” (1974), pp. 197–202, as `[Er74b]`. The authenticated GCL source audit found the exact printed formulation on Erdős–Graham p. 61 and did not find this exact statement in the cited 1974 scan. That is a provenance conflict, not a mathematical contradiction.

## A small example

The finite calculation below is an illustration, not evidence for infinitude. Take $n=1$, $C=1$, and inspect $1\leq k\leq8$. The values of $n+k$ are $2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9$, and their distinct-prime counts are $1,1,1,1,2,1,1,1$. Each is at most $k$, so this finite prefix passes the required inequality; the largest observed ratio $\omega(n+k)/k$ is $1$.

```python
from collections import Counter

def factor_counts(m):
    f, p = Counter(), 2
    while p * p <= m:
        while m % p == 0:
            f[p] += 1
            m //= p
        p += 1
    if m > 1:
        f[m] += 1
    return f

def omega(m): return len(factor_counts(m))
def Omega(m): return sum(factor_counts(m).values())

rows = [(k, omega(1+k), Omega(1+k), omega(1+k) <= k)
        for k in range(1, 9)]
print(rows)
print(all(row[-1] for row in rows), max(w/k for k, w, _, _ in rows))
print(omega(12), Omega(12))
```

Running this gives `[(1,1,1,True), (2,1,1,True), (3,1,2,True), (4,1,1,True), (5,2,2,True), (6,1,1,True), (7,1,3,True), (8,1,2,True)]`, followed by `True 1.0` and `2 3`. This independently checks both the example arithmetic and the elementary inequality $\omega(m)\leq\Omega(m)$ on the displayed inputs. It does not test the unbounded quantifier or establish a second suitable $n$.

## What is known

The original question is a solved literature problem, not an open conjecture. T. Tao and J. Teräväinen, “Quantitative correlations and some problems on prime factors of consecutive integers,” arXiv:2512.01739v2 (25 April 2026), prove the stronger statement in Theorem 1.1, printed p. 2:

$$
\exists C>0\;\text{such that}\;\left\{n\in\mathbb N_{>0}:\forall k\in\mathbb N_{>0},\ \omega(n+k)\leq\Omega(n+k)\leq Ck\right\}\text{ is infinite}.
$$

Here $\Omega$ is the multiplicity-counting function. The first inequality is elementary, while the second is the deep sieve theorem. In §2, printed pp. 11–24, Tao and Teräväinen construct a random $n$ in a large interval and arrange a tail estimate of the form

$$
\mathbb P\bigl(\Omega(\mathbf n+k)>Ck\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{10k^{3/2}}.
$$

Because $\sum_{k\geq1}(10k^{3/2})^{-1}<1$, the union bound leaves positive probability that all positive shifts satisfy the bound simultaneously. The construction uses a growing-dimensional Maynard-type/Selberg sieve and moment estimates; those are borrowed results from the named paper, not a proof supplied by this article. The implication to Erdős Problem 248 is therefore exact once their theorem is accepted, but I have not independently reproduced their full sieve argument.

There is a later improvement in C. F. Lau, “On the Number of Prime Factors of Consecutive Integers,” arXiv:2604.15042v2 (24 June 2026), Theorem 1.1, printed p. 3: infinitely many positive $n$ satisfy $\omega(n+k)\leq\Omega(n+k)\leq C\log k$ for every $k\geq2$. The missing $k=1$ is why this should be reported as a related stronger bound, not silently substituted for Tao–Teräväinen’s exact all-positive-shifts theorem. The official page reports both results.

## What remains

For the original mathematical claim, the remaining work is documentary and formalization-sensitive rather than an open conjecture: preserve the exact Tao–Teräväinen source lock, verify any replay against the precise quantified proposition, and record whether the external development compiles without hidden axioms or `sorry` dependencies. The elementary bridge $\omega(m)\leq\Omega(m)$ is straightforward, but it does not reconstruct the weighted-sieve proof.

There are also nearby open or only partially improved questions. Lau’s paper discusses bounds for backward shifts, sharpness heuristics, and other Erdős problems; those must not be folded into Problem 248. In particular, an $O(\log k)$ result for $k\geq2$ does not assert the best possible constant, nor does a finite search establish infinitely many $n$. The finite check and the bridge $\omega\leq\Omega$ are source-limited partial deductions, not a fresh solution. The official page’s “resolved” label, the catalogue’s `proved (Lean)` label, Tao–Teräväinen’s published/preprint theorem, and an independently replayed formal proof are four different claim types.
