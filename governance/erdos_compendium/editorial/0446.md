## The question in plain language

Fix a positive integer $n$. What fraction of all positive integers are divisible by at least one integer lying strictly between $n$ and $2n$? Erdős Problem 446 calls this natural density $\delta(n)$ and asks how fast it decreases when $n$ grows. It also asks about the density $\delta_1(n)$ of integers having **exactly one divisor** in that interval, conjecturing that $\delta_1(n)$ might be negligible compared with $\delta(n)$.

The strict interval $(n,2n)$ matters: a divisor equal to $n$ or $2n$ does not count. For each fixed $n$, the divisibility condition involves only finitely many possible divisors, so the natural densities exist and may be calculated using inclusion–exclusion. The difficult question concerns the asymptotic behavior of these densities as the interval of allowed divisors moves to larger values.

The two questions have different answers. The exact order of magnitude of $\delta(n)$ is known, and the assertion $\delta_1(n)=o(\delta(n))$ is **false**. Indeed, integers with exactly one eligible divisor account for a positive proportion of those with at least one, uniformly in the asymptotic sense proved by Ford.

## A small example

Take $n=3$. The only integers strictly between $3$ and $6$ are $4$ and $5$. Therefore an integer is counted by $\delta(3)$ exactly when it is divisible by $4$ or by $5$. Inclusion–exclusion gives

$$
\delta(3)=\frac14+\frac15-\frac1{20}=\frac25.
$$

To have exactly one eligible divisor, an integer must be divisible by **exactly one** of $4$ and $5$. The density is therefore

$$
\delta_1(3)=\left(\frac14-\frac1{20}\right)+\left(\frac15-\frac1{20}\right)=\frac7{20}.
$$

The conditional proportion is $\delta_1(3)/\delta(3)=7/8$. This is an exact finite-modulus calculation, not a proof that the corresponding proportion remains large for arbitrary growing $n$.

The computation generalizes to any fixed $n$: all divisibility indicators are periodic modulo the least common multiple of the integers in $(n,2n)$, so a finite residue-class enumeration can compute each $\delta_r(n)$ exactly. That period quickly becomes enormous, making direct enumeration impractical for large $n$, and motivating the analytic estimates.

## What is known

Besicovitch (1934) showed that the lower limit of $\delta(n)$ is zero; Erdős (1935) proved the stronger conclusion $\delta(n)\to0$. Erdős (1960) established a logarithmic decay exponent:

$$
\delta(n)=(\log n)^{-\alpha+o(1)},
\qquad
\alpha=1-\frac{1+\log\log2}{\log2}=0.08607\ldots.
$$

Tenenbaum (1984) refined the estimates, and Kevin Ford (2008) obtained the correct order of magnitude,

$$
\delta(n)\asymp
\frac{1}{(\log n)^\alpha(\log\log n)^{3/2}}.
$$

This is an order-of-magnitude equivalence with implicit positive constants, not a claim that the quotient converges to one.

Ford also disproved the second conjecture. More generally, if $\delta_r(n)$ counts integers with **exactly $r$ divisors** in $(n,2n)$, then for each fixed positive $r$ he proved $\delta_r(n)\gg_r\delta(n)$. Taking $r=1$ shows that $\delta_1(n)/\delta(n)$ cannot tend to zero. The maintained [Erdős Problems #446](https://www.erdosproblems.com/446) page records both conclusions and distinguishes the earlier Erdős and Tenenbaum stages.
