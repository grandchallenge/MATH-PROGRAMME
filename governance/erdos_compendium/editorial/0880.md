## The question in plain language

An infinite set $A$ is an **additive basis of order $h$** if every sufficiently large integer is a sum of **at most $h$** elements of $A$, allowing repetitions. In contrast, a *restricted* representation uses **distinct** summands. Burr and Erdős asked: if $A$ is an ordinary basis of order $h$, must the set $B_h(A)$ of sums of at most $h$ distinct members have eventually bounded gaps?

This question depends on the exact distinction between *at most* and *exactly* $h$ terms. It also depends on whether selecting the same element twice is allowed. For example, $2a=a+a$ is an ordinary representation but is not a two-element restricted representation.

## A small example

For an order-two basis, every sufficiently large odd integer $m$ has a representation with at most two summands from $A$. If it has two terms $m=a+b$, those terms must be **distinct**, since $a=a$ would make $m=2a$ even; a single-term representation is already restricted. Therefore all sufficiently large odd integers lie in $B_2(A)$, so the consecutive gaps are eventually at most 2. This short parity proof settles order two affirmatively.

For $h=3$ and $h=4$, explicit finite stages of the published construction reveal why the same reasoning fails. The ordinary sums cover initial target intervals, but the restricted sums omit long tails:

| Order | Ordinary target interval | Missing restricted values |
|---:|---|---|
| 3 | from 3 through 53 | 45 through 53 |
| 4 | from 4 through 191 | 144 through 191 |

These initial holes are finite examples only. The published construction recursively produces arbitrarily large such gaps.

## What is known

Hegyvári, Hennecart and Plagne proved the complete order-dependent answer in [*Answer to a Question by Burr and Erdős on Restricted Addition, and Related Results*](https://www.cmls.polytechnique.fr/perso/plagne/Erdos-Burr.pdf), *Combinatorics, Probability and Computing* **16** (2007), pp.747–756, DOI 10.1017/S0963548306008224.

Their Theorem 1 has **two contrasting clauses**. For every ordinary additive basis of order 2, the restricted sums of at most two elements have eventually bounded gaps, and the gaps are at most **2**. For every order $h\ge3$, there exists an ordinary basis of order $h$ for which the restricted sums of at most $h$ members have **unbounded** gaps. The negative examples are built recursively rather than arising from a single finite example.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #880](https://www.erdosproblems.com/880) reports the problem as **proved** in the sense that its answer is known, not in the sense that the universal bounded-gap conjecture is true for every $h$.

## GCL's current state

The order-two result admits an independent direct proof. An odd integer cannot equal the sum of two copies of one integer. Since the ordinary order-two basis property covers every sufficiently large odd target, the restricted set must contain all sufficiently large odd targets. This forces its eventual maximum gap to be two.

A finite regression script checks the parity argument for all **512** subsets of $[1,9]$. It also instantiates the **first stage** of the explicit Hegyvári–Hennecart–Plagne counterexample for orders three and four. It verifies ordinary coverage and the indicated restricted gaps using independent bounded representation enumeration.

These computations do **not** reproduce all stages of the published infinite proof. The recursive separation estimates are imported from the 2007 source; GCL has not independently compiled them in Lean or produced an axiom-replay certificate.

## What remains

The bounded next task is to formalize the **odd-target order-two lemma**. Inputs: a set $A$, the ordinary at-most-two representation predicate, and the restricted at-most-two representation predicate. Target: prove that every odd number with an ordinary representation of length at most two has a restricted representation of the same permitted length. Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem, literal axiom output, and exact verification against the 512 finite cases.

The separate infinite negative theorem can be audited by proving the recursive growth of omitted restricted intervals for each fixed $h\ge3$. A failed interval-coverage or separation inequality falsifies that attempted proof. Stop short of asserting independent certification until the full induction is reproduced and checked.
