## The question in plain language

An Egyptian fraction is a finite sum of reciprocals of distinct positive integers. Erdős and Graham ask which integers can occur as the largest denominator in a representation of $1$. In their printed p. 35, the question is phrased for an increasing list $x_1<\cdots<x_k$ whose reciprocal sum is $1$, and asks whether the largest entry assumes almost all integer values.

The source-faithful set is

$$
A=\left\{n\in\mathbb N:\exists S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\},\ n\in S,\ \sum_{m\in S}\frac{1}{m}=1\right\}.
$$

The quantifiers matter. For a particular $n$, membership in $A$ asks for one finite witness $S$: every denominator lies between $1$ and $n$, the denominators are distinct because $S$ is a set, and $n$ is present, hence is the largest denominator. The question is not whether every set of denominators works, nor whether one fixed representation works for infinitely many $n$.

“Almost all” means natural density one:

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,x]|}{x}=1.
$$

Equivalently, the exceptional set $B=\mathbb N\setminus A$ has density zero. This does not mean that every sufficiently large integer belongs to $A$; a density-zero exceptional set may still be infinite. On the evidence inspected here, the mathematical problem is **proved** by a published theorem of Greg Martin. The GCL formal/documentary state remains a `STATUS_CONFLICT`, described below.

## A small example

The integer $6$ is in $A$, witnessed by the exact identity

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16
=\frac6{12}+\frac4{12}+\frac2{12}=1.
$$

The largest denominator is $6$, and the denominators are distinct. Splitting the term $1/3$ is not needed here; another direct witness is $12$:

$$
\frac12+\frac14+\frac16+\frac1{12}
=\frac6{12}+\frac3{12}+\frac2{12}+\frac1{12}=1.
$$

These calculations can be independently reproduced with exact rational arithmetic. The following finite search tests increasing tuples and never uses floating point:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations

def first_by_max(k, bound):
    for largest in range(k, bound + 1):
        for earlier in combinations(range(1, largest), k - 1):
            ns = earlier + (largest,)
            if sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in ns), Fraction(0)) == 1:
                return ns
    return None

assert first_by_max(3, 20) == (2, 3, 6)
assert first_by_max(4, 100) == (2, 4, 6, 12)
print(first_by_max(3, 20), first_by_max(4, 100))
```

The output is `(2, 3, 6) (2, 4, 6, 12)`. This verifies finite membership, and minimality for those fixed term counts within the stated search. It is not evidence for the density theorem. The same two identities and the finite-check requirement are recorded in the authenticated source audit [RESULT/1](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1562#issuecomment-6135764273).

## What is known

Greg Martin’s “Denser Egyptian Fractions,” *Acta Arithmetica* 95 (2000), 231–260, DOI [10.4064/aa-95-3-231-260](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-95-3-231-260), directly addresses the Erdős–Graham question. I inspected the accessible arXiv paper, which identifies the journal reference and gives the definitions and theorem statements. In the introduction, Martin defines $L_1(r)$ to be the integers that cannot be the largest denominator in an Egyptian-fraction representation of a positive rational $r$. His Theorem 4, equation (6), says that $L_1(r)$ has density zero and, for $x\geq3$, has order of growth $x\log\log x/\log x$, with constants depending on $r$.

For $r=1$, the complement of $A$ differs from Martin’s $L_1(1)$ only by the harmless initial convention at $1$. Thus Martin’s theorem implies

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,x]|}{x}=1.
$$

The result is stronger than density one: the exceptional count is bounded above and below by constant multiples of $x\log\log x/\log x$. It is not an asymptotic equivalence with a certified leading constant. Martin’s paper also discusses exceptional integers related to prime powers, but that discussion must not be inflated into a complete classification of $B$.

This is a published external proof of the mathematical statement, not a proof newly established by this manuscript. The original Erdős–Graham scan is historical source evidence for the question; Martin’s theorem is the resolving primary result inspected for the status claim.
