**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #507, **Heilbronn's triangle problem**, remains **OPEN**. It asks for the largest minimum triangle area achievable by placing $n$ points in a unit disk. The strongest currently cited lower bound is due to Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi; the upper bound was improved by Cohen, Pohoata and Zakharov in a 2025 *Inventiones mathematicae* paper.

## The question in plain language

Place $n\ge3$ **distinct points** in the Euclidean unit disk. Every triple forms a triangle, possibly of area zero if the points are collinear. Measure the **smallest** area among all those triangles, then vary the configuration to make this smallest area as **large** as possible.

Let

$$
D=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x^2+y^2\le1\},
\qquad
T(P)=\min_{\{a,b,c\}\subset P}\mathrm{Area}(a,b,c).
$$

The extremal function is

$$
\boxed{\alpha(n)=\sup_{P\subseteq D,\ |P|=n}T(P).}
$$

Here the minimum is **within each configuration** and the supremum is **over all configurations**. Reversing those operations changes the problem. The original Erdős paper uses a unit *circle* in the older geometric sense of the disk of points inside the circle.

The more commonly studied version places points in the unit **square**. The two versions have comparable orders of magnitude, because a translated unit square lies inside a unit disk and a unit disk lies inside a square of side two; rescaling areas changes them by constant factors. Exact numerical values in the square and disk need not agree.

## A small example

For three points, the largest possible area in a unit disk is the area of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the boundary circle:

$$
\alpha(3)=\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}.
$$

For four or more points, maximizing the *minimum* area is a global placement problem: improving one small triangle can create a different small triangle elsewhere. Collinear triples have zero area and thus are never helpful for maximizing the minimum.

An especially useful **exact arithmetic construction** uses a prime number $p\ge3$. For each $i=0,\ldots,p-1$, put

$$
P_i=\left(\frac{i}{p}-\frac12,\,
\frac{i^2\bmod p}{p}-\frac12\right).
$$

Every coordinate lies between $-1/2$ and $1/2$, so all $p$ points lie in the unit disk. For three distinct indices $i,j,k$, the determinant giving **twice** the oriented triangle area is an integer divided by $p^2$. Modulo $p$, its numerator is the Vandermonde product

$$
(j-i)(k-i)(k-j)\not\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

Consequently no three points are collinear and each triangle's area is **at least**

$$
\frac{1}{2p^2}.
$$

For any $n\ge3$, Bertrand's postulate provides a prime with $n\le p<2n$. Take any $n$ points from the $p$‑point set; removing points cannot decrease the minimum triangle area. We obtain the entirely elementary lower bound

$$
\alpha(n)>\frac{1}{8n^2}.
$$

This gives the correct order for Erdős's original elementary $n^{-2}$ bound, **not** the later stronger logarithmic bound. The construction's finite primes and all triangle determinants are testable using exact integer and rational arithmetic.

## What is known

### Erdős's original question

P. Erdős, *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed **p.246, Problem III.6**, [original scan](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=26), was visually inspected. Erdős defines the maximum, over configurations, of the **minimum** triangle area, records an easy $O(1/n)$ upper bound and his own $c/n^2$ lower construction, and cites K. F. Roth's 1951 improvements. Those historical rates were superseded; they are not today's strongest bounds.

Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi, *A lower bound for Heilbronn's problem*, *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* (2) **25** (1982), 13–24, proved the stronger existence bound

$$
\alpha(n)\ge c\frac{\log n}{n^2}
$$

for an absolute constant $c>0$ and sufficiently large $n$. Their **complete original proof was not inspected** in this audit. The result is cited as established literature, not derived from the modular construction above.

### The best cited upper bound

Alex Cohen, Cosmin Pohoata and Dmitrii Zakharov, *Lower bounds for incidences*, *Inventiones mathematicae* **240** (2025), 1045–1118, [open-access paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00222-025-01331-2), established a new upper bound. Their **Theorem 1.8** asserts that for every $\varepsilon>0$, every set of $n$ points in the unit square contains three whose triangle area is at most

$$
C_{\varepsilon}\,n^{-7/6+\varepsilon}.
$$

Equivalently, the upper bound is $n^{-7/6+o(1)}$. This is **not** a bound of exactly $Cn^{-7/6}$ with a single absolute constant: the $\varepsilon$‑dependent loss matters.

The published proof of Theorem 1.8 is short once its main incidence result is available. Among $n$ square points, find many disjoint pairs separated by $O(n^{-1/2})$. Draw the line through each pair. A deep **incidence-distance corollary** finds another selected point at distance at most $n^{-2/3+o(1)}$ from one of these lines. The area of the resulting triangle is at most half its base length times that distance:

$$
O(n^{-1/2})\,n^{-2/3+o(1)}
=n^{-7/6+o(1)}.
$$

The *full peer-reviewed primary article* and the statement and proof of Theorem 1.8 were inspected on the publisher's accessible text. The underlying incidence theorem is a substantial result, not something GCL's bounded finite tests reproduce.

Thus the established gap is

$$
\boxed{
c\frac{\log n}{n^2}
\le\alpha(n)\le n^{-7/6+o(1)}
}
$$

up to appropriate constants and comparable disk/square normalization. The true asymptotic order is still unknown. The maintained [Erdős #507 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/507), edited December 2025, records the same bounds and OPEN classification.

## What remains

The research problem is to narrow the substantial exponent gap, for example by a genuinely stronger construction with uniformly large minimum triangle area or a new universal area upper bound.

The next bounded mathematical work package is entirely reproducible: construct the prime-field point sets for small odd primes; independently compute all triangle determinants from exact rational coordinates; check the Vandermonde residue; and verify that no triangle area is below $1/(2p^2)$. A zero determinant, a residue inconsistency or an incorrect disk inclusion would falsify the implementation. A finite success would **not** prove any improvement to the general asymptotic.

The 1982 primary proof and the deeper 2025 incidence corollary remain separate formalization obligations. The current article remains an unaccepted editorial draft.
