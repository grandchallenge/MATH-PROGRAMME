## The question in plain language

Let $k$ be a nonnegative integer. Erdős Problem 396 asks whether one can always find a positive integer $n$ for which every integer in the descending block $n,n-1,\ldots,n-k$ contributes to a single divisor of the central binomial coefficient. In formulas, the intended question is whether, for every $k$, there is an $n>k$ such that

$$
\prod_{i=0}^{k}(n-i) \mid \binom{2n}{n}.
$$

The condition $n>k$ is the natural normalization: it keeps every factor positive and makes the product an ordinary positive integer. The right side counts the ways to choose $n$ objects from a set of $2n$ objects. The problem asks whether that count can absorb the whole consecutive product, not merely each factor separately. The issue-bound statement is [Erdős Problem 396](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1666).

The product is deliberately consecutive, so shared prime factors matter. Knowing that each factor divides the binomial coefficient does not automatically show that their product does: the same prime power may be needed by several factors. This is why the exact quantifiers and the joint divisibility condition must be kept visible.

## A small example

The case $k=2$ has a nontrivial finite witness at $n=2480$. Direct exact arithmetic gives

$$
2480\cdot2479\cdot2478=15\,234\,545\,760,
$$

and

$$
\binom{4960}{2480}\bmod 15\,234\,545\,760=0.
$$

This can be reproduced without floating-point arithmetic by running:

```python
from math import comb

k, n = 2, 2480
d = n * (n - 1) * (n - 2)
assert comb(2 * n, n) % d == 0
print(d, comb(2 * n, n) % d)
```

The calculation verifies one finite instance only. It does not prove the universal assertion in $k$, establish that $2480$ is the smallest witness, or explain why witnesses should exist for every larger $k$. It is a useful regression check for the normalization and for any future computational certificate, not a substitute for a proof.

## What is known

The maintained [live problem entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/396), accessed 2026-10-13, gives the same question and records the problem as open. It points to Carl Pomerance’s paper, “Divisors of the Middle Binomial Coefficient,” *The American Mathematical Monthly* 122 (2015), 636–644, [primary PDF](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/amm2015.pdf) and [DOI record](https://doi.org/10.4169/amer.math.monthly.122.7.636). The site’s short key `Po14` and bibliography year “2014” do not match the paper’s journal volume, page footer, and PDF presentation, which identify the published article as 2015; that bibliographic drift is retained rather than silently repaired. The original attribution is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), cited by the live page as `ErGr80`.

Pomerance’s Theorem 2, on pp. 638–640, proves that for every fixed positive $k$, almost all positive integers $n$ satisfy

$$
(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+k)\mid\binom{2n}{n}.
$$

That is a forward product and is not the falling product in Problem 396. His Theorem 3, on p. 640, proves that for every fixed $k\geq0$, infinitely many $n>k$ satisfy $n-k\mid\binom{2n}{n}$, while those $n$ have upper asymptotic density less than $1/3$. This is a strong one-factor result, but it does not combine the factors $n,n-1,\ldots,n-k$ into one divisor. The proof uses p-adic valuations and Kummer’s carry criterion, a promising interface for a bounded successor task.

## What remains

The clean mathematical gap is simultaneous control of the prime-power valuations in the whole block $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k)$. A bounded investigation could fix one small $k$, compute exact witnesses and non-witnesses, factor the divisor, and compare each required valuation with the valuation of $\binom{2n}{n}$ as computed from factorial valuations or carries. A useful first target is a checked lemma for $k=2$ or $k=3$ under an explicit congruence or prime-factor hypothesis, with a finite range large enough to catch normalization errors. Any claimed generalization must separately address overlapping prime factors and cannot be inferred from Pomerance’s forward-product density theorem.
