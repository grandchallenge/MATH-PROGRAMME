## The question in plain language

A set $A$ of positive integers is an *additive basis of order $k$* if every sufficiently large integer can be made from a bounded number of elements of $A$. For [Erdős Problem #870](https://www.erdosproblems.com/870), the maintained formulation allows **at most** $k$ summands and asks whether sufficiently many representations force $A$ to contain a minimal subbasis of the same order.

For each $k\ge3$, is there a constant $c(k)>0$ such that a lower bound

$$
r_{A,k}(n)\ge c(k)\log n
$$

for all sufficiently large $n$ guarantees a minimal order $k$ subbasis? Here a representation is a nondecreasing list of one through $k$ positive summands, repetitions allowed. Minimality means that removing any member destroys the ability to represent all sufficiently large integers.

The intuition is that a large supply of alternative representations should make it possible to prune a basis to a smallest indispensable core. The question asks whether a logarithmic supply is enough. It is closely related to #868 and #869, but its higher-order quantifiers and summand conventions matter.

## A small example

There are two natural ways to count representations: exactly $k$ summands or between one and $k$ summands. They are not interchangeable without a proof. For example, let

$$
A=\{n\in\mathbb N:n\equiv1\pmod3\}.
$$

Every positive integer is a sum of **at most three** members of $A$: integers congruent to $1$, $2$, or $0$ modulo $3$ use respectively one, two, or three summands. However, every sum of **exactly three** members is congruent to $0$ modulo $3$. Thus $A$ is an at-most-three basis but not an exact-three basis.

This distinction is material because [Erdős and Nathanson's 1988 paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1988-25.pdf), Part 2 and Part 3, defines an asymptotic basis of order $h$ using exactly $h$ summands, whereas the maintained #870 wording explicitly says **at most** $k$. A result formalized for the modern convention must not be silently presented as a theorem about the older convention.

An exact finite fixture compares two independent ways to count nondecreasing representations: direct enumeration of combinations with repetition and a coin-change dynamic program indexed by sum and number of terms. They agree for $A=\{1,2,3,5,8,13\}$, $k=2,3,4$, and every $1\le n\le30$. For instance, $n=10$ has respectively $2,4,8$ representations with at most $2,3,4$ summands. The residue-class example was also checked directly through $n=100$.

## What is known

The original higher-order question is in [Erdős and Nathanson (1988), Part 3, Problem 3](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1988-25.pdf). Their earlier positive result for order two requires a logarithmic coefficient strictly above a particular threshold. They observed that the method did not straightforwardly extend to $h>2$.

The maintained #870 record remains classified **open** in the community database as of 13 October 2026. There is, however, a substantial later source that must be evaluated rather than ignored. David Corneliu Turturean's [public proof repository](https://github.com/davidturturean/erdos-870), commit `a732dee4fbbef41e8a300aef264a3bc1bf505862` dated 26 June 2026, contains a manuscript, pinned Lean 4 project, and a claimed **negative answer for every $k\ge3$ and every $C>0$**.

Its argument begins with the Larsen–Larsen order-two construction. For $k\ge4$ it introduces finite residue-class fillers that amplify representation counts while preserving the absence of minimal subbases. The $k=3$ case requires a different clustered-canary construction to control finite shifts. The manuscript explicitly adopts the at-most $k$ convention.

The Lean entry point is `Erdos870.erdos_870 : Erdos870Target`. The target quantifies over every $k\ge3$ and $C>0$, producing a set $E$ with `BasisAtMost E k`, `LogRepLowerBound E k C`, and `ContainsNoMinimalSubbasis E k`. `RAtMost` counts multisets of positive elements, so the source-level representation convention matches the maintained at-most $k$ statement. This is a meaningful semantic match, not merely a similarly named theorem.
