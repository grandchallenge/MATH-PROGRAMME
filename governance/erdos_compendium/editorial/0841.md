## The question in plain language

The search distance t_n measures how far above n one must go to find integers whose product with n is a square. Its distribution is controlled by the largest prime factor but the two quantities need not be pointwise equal.

For n a positive integer, define t_n least t>=0 for which there is a subset S⊂{n+1,...,n+t} with n·prod(S) a square, with t_n=0 for square n. A classical problem estimates t_n and how often it is nearly n; the 2024 paper solves DISTRIBUTIONAL agreement with largest prime factor P(n), not a pointwise formula for all n.

## A small example

For n=6, the selected {8,12}⊂{7,...,12} yields 6·8·12=24², and exhaustive all earlier intervals t<=5 admit no such subset. Therefore t_6=6, whereas largest prime factor P(6)=3. For n=4 square, t_4=0 and P(4)=2, showing why lower t_n>=P(n) needs nonsquare scope. Exact parity-of-prime-exponents certificate fits finite testing.

This finite calculation is not an asymptotic or all-parameter proof.

## What is known

Maintained /latex/841 read 2026-10-14: Selfridge proved t_n=P(n) if P(n)>sqrt(2n)+1, and t_n<<sqrt(n) otherwise; Bui–Pratt–Zaharescu [BPZ24], *A problem of Erdős-Graham-Granville-Selfridge on integral points on hyperelliptic curves*, Math Proc Camb Phil Soc (2024) 309–323, proved equality of limiting distribution fractions for t_n<=n^c versus P(n)<=n^c (0<c<=1), plus bounds on exceptional small t and all nonsquares. Strict t_n>=P(n) applies to nonsquares; squares have t=0 by convention. Their theorem does NOT give pointwise t_n=P(n) always.

A subset product makes $n$ a square exactly when the parity of every prime exponent in the product becomes even. At $n=6$, selecting 8 and 12 first becomes possible with $t=6$, since

$$

6\cdot8\cdot12=24^2.

$$

For square $n$, the empty subset yields $t_n=0$. Thus $t_n\geq P(n)$ requires a non-square restriction. The Bui–Pratt–Zaharescu 2024 theorem equates limiting DISTRIBUTIONS of $t_n\leq n^c$ and $P(n)\leq n^c$, not the values for each $n$.
