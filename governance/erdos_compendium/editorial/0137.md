## The question in plain language

A positive integer is **powerful** when every prime dividing it occurs with exponent at least two. Erdős and Selfridge asked: for any integer $k\ge3$, can a product of $k$ consecutive positive integers ever be powerful?

$$
P(n,k)=(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+k),\qquad n\ge0.
$$

An affirmative instance would have $v_p(P(n,k))\ge2$ for every prime $p\mid P(n,k)$. The conjecture is that no such instance exists. A **perfect power**, $a^m$ with $m\ge2$, is always powerful when $a>1$, but a powerful integer need not be a perfect power.

## A small example

The integer $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ is powerful but not a square or cube. It also equals $8\cdot9$, illustrating that **two** consecutive integers can have a powerful product. The product $2\cdot3\cdot4=24=2^3\cdot3$ is not powerful because the prime $3$ occurs only once. This example is not a proof for all lengths or starting values.

## What is known

**OPEN.** Erdős and Selfridge proved in *Illinois Journal of Mathematics* 19 (1975), 292–301, that the product of two or more consecutive positive integers is never a nontrivial perfect power. That theorem is weaker than excluding all powerful products. The maintained [Erdős #137](https://www.erdosproblems.com/137) also records a stronger, separate question about how many primes occur to exponent exactly one in a longer product. Neither result is promoted to a proof of the stated powerful-number conjecture.

## What remains

Factor $P(n,k)$ exactly for $1\le n\le200$ and $3\le k\le12$, recording one witness prime with valuation one in every tested case or a finite counterexample. Acceptance: verify the powerful predicate by exact prime valuations, reject incorrect inferences from the perfect-power test, and report the finite tested domain. A finite search cannot establish the universal conjecture.
