## The question in plain language

Start with any finite set of positive integers $A=\{a_1<\cdots<a_k\}$. After the seed has been prescribed, repeatedly choose the least integer larger than the current last term that is not a sum $a_i+a_j$ of two already chosen terms, where repeated indices are allowed. This is the Dickson version of the greedy sum-avoiding construction. The question is whether, for every finite seed, the successive gaps

$$
a_{m+1}-a_m
$$

eventually repeat with some fixed period.

“Eventually” matters: the first part of a sequence may look irregular for a long time, while the tail could repeat forever. Green’s open-problems list distinguishes this rule from Queneau’s variant, which forbids $i=j$. Problem 341 concerns the rule allowing $i=j$.

The wording on the maintained [Erdős Problems page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/341) is an individual-seed formulation, but its mathematical question is naturally read universally over finite positive seeds. It also records the historical example $\{1,4,9,16,25\}$ and says that periodicity can take thousands of terms to appear. That remark is a motivation, not a theorem about all seeds.

## A small example

Take the page’s seed $A=\{1,4,9,16,25\}$. I recomputed the greedy rule using exact integers: at each stage I formed every sum of two terms already present, including equal pairs, then scanned upward from the last term. The calculation gives

$$
(a_{87},\ldots,a_{92})=(440,443,445,448,453,471),
$$

and

$$
(a_{311},\ldots,a_{316})=(1616,1619,1621,1624,1629,1647).
$$

The second six-term block is the first block translated by $1176$. More importantly, an exact finite certificate checks the proposed tail through $2\cdot426+1176-2=2026$. Below $426$ there are $86$ selected values; from $426$ onward there are $224$ selected residues modulo $1176$. If $R$ is that residue set and $E$ is the union of $R$ with the residues of the pre-tail values, the direct residue calculation gives $|E|=233$ and

$$
E+R=(\mathbb Z/1176\mathbb Z)\setminus R,
$$

with both sides having $952$ residues. Together with the exact recurrence check up to $2026$, this is a finite all-future certificate for this seed: $a_{n+224}=a_n+1176$ for every $n\ge87$. It proves one instance, not the universal assertion. It also does not validate unrelated computations or settle whether every seed eventually behaves this way.

## What is known

The primary historical reference named by the live page is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, p. 53. Ben Green’s current [100 Open Problems](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/greenbj/papers/open-problems.pdf), Problem 7 and its comments, explicitly describes both the Dickson rule and the distinct-index variant, and says that eventual periodicity appears open for both. Green points readers to references and computations, not to a resolution. This is the clearest dated primary source located in the inquiry for the status boundary.

The live page was edited 20 January 2026 and still displays open status, zero proof expositions, one discussion comment, and a formalised-statement link. Its July 2026 comment reports a period length of $224$ terms and a translation of $1176$, but the forum warns that proof claims and comments are unverified. The exact calculation above independently reproduces the stated finite certificate; it is not a proof of the general problem.

## What remains

The central task is to prove or refute eventual periodicity for every finite positive seed under the repeated-index rule. A useful bounded successor is to formalize and independently check the finite-certificate lemma used above: a proposed tail onset $N$, period modulus $q$, and residue set $R$ should be accepted only after exact recurrence checks through $2N+q-2$ and a residue identity. This would turn individual computational discoveries into auditable theorems without pretending that a finite search proves the universal claim.

The smallest semantic prerequisite is a precise definition of the greedy extension, including the seed boundary, positivity, strict ordering, and whether equal summands are permitted. Only after that normalization should the Lean statement be repaired and proved or falsified. Extensive searches for apparent nonperiodicity remain finite evidence unless coupled to a proof of aperiodicity.
