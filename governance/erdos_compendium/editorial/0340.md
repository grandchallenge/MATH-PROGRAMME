## The question in plain language

Start with $1$. Having chosen a finite set of integers, append the smallest larger integer that keeps all sums $a+b$, with $a\leq b$, different. The resulting infinite sequence is the greedy Sidon sequence, also called the Mian–Chowla sequence:

$$
1,2,4,8,13,21,31,45,66,81,97,\ldots
$$

Problem 340 asks how quickly this particular sequence accumulates. If $A(N)$ denotes the number of its terms at most $N$, the principal question is whether, for every fixed positive $\varepsilon$, there is a constant depending on $\varepsilon$ such that $A(N)$ is at least that constant times $N^{1/2-\varepsilon}$ for all sufficiently large $N$. The wording “what is the order of growth?” is broader than this displayed lower-bound question: it invites a matching description, not merely one inequality.

This is a question about one deterministic sequence, not about the largest possible Sidon subset of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$. A Sidon set has no repeated pair sums, including diagonal sums such as $2a$. That convention matters when translating between the sequence definition, counting functions, and formal statements.

The original task is recorded in [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1610](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1610). The issue’s requested source-result citation remains **[authenticated original RESULT/1](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1610#issuecomment-6140008151)**; no result comment existed during this audit.

## A small example

Take the first five terms $A_5=\{1,2,4,8,13\}$. The unordered sums with repetition allowed are

$$
2,3,5,9,14,4,6,10,15,8,12,17,16,21,26.
$$

They are fifteen distinct integers, exactly the number of pairs $a\leq b$ from five elements, so the finite set is Sidon. To test the greedy next term, exact integer enumeration gives the following first collision for each candidate: $14$ gives $1+14=2+13$, $15$ gives $2+15=4+13$, $16$ gives $1+16=4+13$, $17$ gives $4+17=8+13$, $18$ gives $8+18=13+13$, $19$ gives $2+19=8+13$, and $20$ gives $1+20=8+13$. Candidate $21$ produces no repeated sum, so the next term is $21$.

This is a reproducible finite check over integers, not a numerical approximation. It verifies the construction through the fifth step and illustrates why the greedy rule is local but not obviously amenable to a simple recurrence. It does not prove any eventual estimate for $A(N)$, nor does it decide the problem’s quantifier over every $\varepsilon>0$.

## What is known

The primary historical reference for the initial computation is A. M. Mian and S. Chowla, “On the $B_2$ sequences of Sidon,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of India*, Section A 14 (1944), pages 3–4, bibliographic record [MR0014114](https://mathscinet.ams.org/mathscinet-getitem?mr=0014114). The first terms are independently transcribed in Kevin O’Bryant’s [annotated bibliography of Sidon-sequence work](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/DS11/pdf/0), page 3 of the PDF. Those sources support the name, definition, and initial terms; they are not evidence of a solution to the modern asymptotic formulation.

The same bibliography reports Alfred Stöhr’s treatment in “Gelöste und ungelöste Fragen über Basen der natürlichen Zahlenreihe,” *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* 194 (1955), 111–140, especially the Sidon discussion on pages 129–135 ([primary journal record](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1955.194.111)). For the greedy sequence it records the upper estimate that the term at index $k$ is less than $(k-1)^3+1$. In counting-function language this yields the familiar cubic-root-scale lower bound, up to the usual indexing and constant translation. It is far weaker than the target exponent $1/2-\varepsilon$.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-locked statement audit followed by a bounded exact computation. First, fix one definition of $A(N)$, one indexing convention, and the equivalence between “all pairwise sums with $i\leq j$ are distinct” and the formal `IsSidon` predicate. Then generate a machine-readable prefix through a declared bound $N_0$, checking every pair sum exactly and recording the first rejected candidate at each step. Acceptance should require a replayable certificate: the prefix, its pair-sum collision table, and a proof or independently checkable script that each selected term is the least admissible integer.

This can validate the implementation and expose indexing errors, but it cannot establish an asymptotic exponent. A separate mathematical task would need a proved upper bound on the greedy terms strong enough to imply the desired counting lower bound, or a counterexample sequence of certified prefixes plus a theorem showing that the proposed asymptotic assertion fails. Stopping is reached when the source normalization is settled and the bounded certificate either replays exactly or produces a concrete discrepancy; unresolved asymptotic theory should then be handed to mathematical review rather than inferred from data.
