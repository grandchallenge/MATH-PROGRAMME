## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 399 asks whether a factorial can ever be made from two like powers. In the notation of the problem, are there natural numbers $ n,x,y,k $ with $ xy>1 $ and $ k>2 $ such that either

$$
n! = x^k+y^k
$$

or

$$
n! = x^k-y^k?
$$

The second form can be written with a nonnegative right-hand side as $ n!+y^k=x^k $. The condition $ xy>1 $ excludes the uninteresting cases in which one of the two bases is zero or the product is too small. The exponent is at least $ 3 $. Thus the question is a universal nonexistence claim: one single valid quadruple $ (n,x,y,k) $ would disprove it.

The exact formal encoding pinned for this audit uses natural numbers and the disjunction
$( n! = x^k+y^k ) \mathbin{\lor} ( n!+y^k=x^k )$, together with $ 1<x y $ and $ 2<k $. This is the right normalization for the reported example, but it should not be confused with a classification of every possible solution.

## A small example

The reported counterexample is small enough to verify without approximation:

$$
10! = 3,628,800,
$$

and

$$
48^4-36^4=5,308,416-1,679,616=3,628,800=10!.
$$

Here $ xy=48\cdot36=1,728>1 $, $ k=4>2 $, and the two bases are not coprime: $ gcd(48,36)=12 $. The same identity may be written as $ 10!+36^4=48^4 $. I checked each displayed integer calculation with exact integer arithmetic; no floating-point or search heuristic is involved.

This calculation is decisive for the yes-or-no question as normalized: it supplies an admissible witness to the existential negation of “there are no solutions.” It does not show that this is the only witness, does not solve the coprime subproblems, and does not establish any general theorem about factorials and powers. In particular, the common divisor $ 12 $ is mathematically important because several older results impose $ gcd(x,y)=1 $.

## What is known

The primary source inspected here is P. Erdős and R. Obláth, “Über diophantische Gleichungen der Form $ n!=x^p+y^p $ und $ n!\pm m!=x^p $,” *Acta litterarum ac scientiarum Regiae Universitatis Hungaricae Francisco-Josephinae*, volume 8 (1937), pages 241–255. The authors explicitly restrict the main investigation to coprime $ x $ and $ y $. Their Theorem 1, on page 249, rules out factorial sums or differences of powers with exponent $ p $ in the stated non-power-of-two range, apart from the elementary exceptional solution for the square-sum case. Their fourth-power difference argument is different: Theorem 3, on page 254, gives nonexistence only for sufficiently large $ n $ when the two integers are coprime. These are substantial partial theorems, not a proof of the unrestricted Problem 399.

The maintained problem page also records two narrower results: Erdős and Obláth’s coprime result for $ k\ne4 $, and Pollack–Shapiro’s result that $ n!=x^4-1 $ has no solutions. It further records a congruence observation for the coprime equation $ n!=x^4+y^4 $. Those statements concern restricted families. They do not invalidate the non-coprime witness above.

The exact source of the Barfield attribution remains less clear. The live page says that Jonas Barfield found the displayed solution, while the formal source says that the example is discussed in problem D2 of Guy’s 2004 collection. I found no primary publication by Barfield in the source packet, and therefore treat the attribution as a reported provenance claim. The identity itself is independently verifiable, so the mathematical disproof does not depend on trusting a numerical transcription; the historical credit does.

## What remains

The useful next step is a bounded provenance-and-interface check, not an exhaustive Lean build. Its input should be the two frozen blobs above, the live Problem 399 page, the 1937 scan, and the bibliographic trail for the Barfield example. Its target should be a one-page ledger that states whether a primary document containing the exact $ 10!,48,36,4 $ counterexample can be identified, and separately records the hypotheses of each reusable coprime lemma.

Acceptance should require an author, date, stable document link, page or theorem location, and a line-by-line comparison with the normalized quantifiers. Falsification would be either a source showing a different equation or a hidden coprimality/positivity assumption that makes the cited result inapplicable. Stop after one bounded literature pass, or immediately when the primary provenance is located or confirmed unavailable; do not infer a stronger status from the catalogue label. A later formalization may target the finite witness and the elementary coprime interface, but any theorem still containing `sorry` must remain visibly incomplete.
