## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #310 asks whether a dense set of small positive integers must contain a small-denominator rational made from distinct unit fractions. A unit fraction is a reciprocal such as $1/6$. The source question starts with $\alpha>0$, an integer $N\geq1$, and a set $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ satisfying $|A|\geq\alpha N$. It asks for a subset $S\subseteq A$ and positive integers $a,b$ such that

$$
\sum_{n\in S}\frac{1}{n}=\frac{a}{b},\qquad 1\leq a\leq b,
$$

with $b=O_\alpha(1)$. The last notation means that, after fixing the density $\alpha$, one bound for $b$ works independently of $N$ and of the particular set $A$. The positivity condition matters: the empty subset would otherwise give the uninformative sum $0$.

The live [Erdős Problems entry for #310](https://www.erdosproblems.com/310) gives this formulation and identifies the historical reference as Erdős and Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980). It also records the later quantitative result described below. The original [GCL issue #1580](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1580) binds this source inquiry to assignment `ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0310-S01`.

## A small example

Take $N=6$ and $A=\{2,3,6\}$. Then $|A|=3=(1/2)N$, so this is a concrete set of density $\alpha=1/2$. Choose $S=A$. Exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{3}{6}+\frac{2}{6}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{6}{6}=1=\frac{1}{1}.
$$

The calculation was checked with Python's `fractions.Fraction`, which returned `sum = 1` and `verified = True`; no floating-point approximation is involved. It illustrates the target shape and shows that the denominator can be $1$. It does not establish the theorem: one finite example says nothing about every dense $A$, about sufficiently large $N$, or about a bound uniform over all such sets.

## What is known

Thomas F. Bloom's paper, [*On a density conjecture about unit fractions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.03726), states in Theorem 2 of the introduction that every set of positive upper density in the positive integers contains a finite subset whose reciprocal sum is exactly $1$. Its more quantitative Theorem 3 gives a finite-interval criterion in terms of the reciprocal mass $\sum_{n\in A}1/n$. Liu and Sawhney explain that Bloom's result already implies the original Problem #310 conclusion with $b=O_\alpha(1)$.

The sharper source is Yang P. Liu and Mehtaab Sawhney, [*On further questions regarding unit fractions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07113), arXiv version 1 dated 2024-04-10. Proposition 1.4 in the introduction states that, for every $\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $N$ depending on $\varepsilon$, if

$$
(\log N)^{-1/7+\varepsilon}\leq\alpha\leq\frac12,\qquad A\subseteq[1,N],\qquad |A|\geq\alpha N,
$$

then there are integers $1\leq s\leq t\leq\exp(C/\alpha)$ and a subset $B\subseteq A$ with $\sum_{n\in B}1/n=s/t$. The paper explicitly says that applying Bloom's Proposition 1 with standard estimates gives the original $t=O_\alpha(1)$ conclusion, while Proposition 1.4 records the stronger quantitative range and the exponential denominator bound. It also explains why that exponential dependence is essentially sharp. These are external published or posted mathematical results; this article does not independently reprove them.

## What remains

The external mathematical status is resolved by Bloom and Liu–Sawhney, but a careful GCL record still needs to preserve the exact semantic chain. In particular, the original statement uses a constant depending only on fixed $\alpha$, while Proposition 1.4 adds an admissible range for $\alpha$ that depends on $N$, together with the explicit bound $t\leq\exp(C/\alpha)$. A formalization review should check positive numerators, nonempty subsets, natural-versus-real coercions, the interval convention $[1,N]$, and the meaning of “sufficiently large.”
