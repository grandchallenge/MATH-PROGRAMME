## The question in plain language

A shape's **topological dimension** measures the number of independent local directions in a sense preserved by homeomorphisms. An interval has dimension one, an ordinary square dimension two, and the product of two ordinary squares dimension four.

Erdős asked whether this familiar rule can fail maximally. For every integer $n\ge2$, is there a topological space $S$ such that

$$
\dim S=n,\qquad \dim(S\times S)=n?
$$

This is [Erdős Problem #909](https://www.erdosproblems.com/909), **proved** by Richard D. Anderson and J. E. Keisler in 1967. They construct more than requested: the same space can have dimension $n$ in **every finite Cartesian power and even its countable Cartesian power**.

The space $S$ must be understood as a potentially unusual **noncompact subspace**, not an ordinary cube of dimension $n$ or manifold. The dimension in the original theorem is the standard inductive topological dimension for separable metric spaces, agreeing with covering dimension in the relevant setting. It is not linear-algebraic vector-space dimension.

## A small example

Take a finite set $D$ of isolated points. Its topological dimension is zero: every open cover is refined by the cover of individual singleton points, where no point belongs to more than one set. Its square $D\times D$ is also a finite discrete set, and hence has dimension zero.

This demonstrates the algebraic equality $\dim(D^2)=\dim D$, but **only in dimension zero**. It does not solve Erdős's positive-dimensional question.

At the opposite extreme, for an ordinary interval $I=[0,1]$,

$$
\dim I=1,\qquad \dim(I\times I)=2.
$$

Likewise, the standard cube $[0,1]^n$ has dimension $n$, while its square is homeomorphic to $[0,1]^{2n}$ and has dimension $2n$. An elementary Cartesian product of familiar manifolds is therefore the **wrong** candidate for #909.

Erdős had already shown that the space of rational-coordinate points in Hilbert space has topological dimension one and motivated the higher-dimensional search. The Anderson–Keisler construction supplies the affirmative answer for every requested positive integer dimension.

## What is known

Richard D. Anderson and J. E. Keisler, [*An Example in Dimension Theory*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1967-0215288-0), *Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society* **18** (1967), no.4, pp.709–713, supply the source construction. Their **Theorem 1** states that for any positive integers $q$ and $s$, a subspace $K\subseteq\mathbb R^q$ exists with both $K$ and its power of exponent $s$ of dimension $q-1$.

Their stronger **Theorem 2** constructs **one** such $K\subseteq\mathbb R^q$ whose every finite power and countable power also have dimension $q-1$. This is not a sequence of different $K$ chosen for each exponent: a single space works for all powers.

To answer Erdős, substitute $q=n+1$ and $s=2$. The published theorem gives

$$
K\subseteq\mathbb R^{n+1},\qquad
\dim K=\dim(K^2)=n.
$$

The construction involves transfinite choices meeting every nondegenerate continuum while arranging products to avoid carefully chosen countable families of spheres. Its dimension lower bound uses intersections with continua; its upper bound exploits sphere avoidance. This is a genuine infinite construction rather than a finite-dimensional coordinate trick.

The [maintained Erdős #909](https://www.erdosproblems.com/909) lists the problem as **PROVED**, citing Anderson–Keisler [AnKe67] and [Er82e]. A [page-specific scholarly source review](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/library/analysis/anderson_1967_example_dimension_theory) records that the original paper's Theorems 1 and 2 were read against scanned pages 709 and 712, while the full proof was not reconstructed. The [journal issue table of contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/i335948) independently confirms the article, authors and printed pp.709–713.

## GCL's current state

There is an exact logical reduction from the strong published theorem to the original problem. If for each positive $q$ there exists one nonempty $K\subseteq\mathbb R^q$ such that

$$
\dim(K^s)=q-1
\quad\text{for all positive finite integers }s,
$$

then fixing $q=n+1$ and selecting $s=1,2$ yields $\dim K=\dim(K^2)=n$. No special topology is changed between these powers. This deduction is elementary, although the existence theorem for $K$ is imported.

The executable fixture governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_908_909.py verifies this integer parameter substitution at 54 pairs of target dimension and finite exponent. It also enumerates every open cover on discrete finite sets of 1–4 points, showing that singleton refinements have order one and both the finite spaces and their squares have dimension zero. These are exact sanity checks, not a numerical certificate for the unusual Anderson–Keisler spaces: **topological dimension of an uncountable, noncompact construction cannot be inferred from a finite point sample**.

The classical topological fact that a subspace embeds in its own product via $x\mapsto(x,x_0)$ shows that dimension of a metric space cannot decrease under taking a nonempty square, when the covering-dimension monotonicity conditions hold. Anderson–Keisler attain **equality** rather than a decrease.

## What remains

A useful bounded mathematical task is the **parameter-substitution and product-embedding interface**. Fix the original Anderson–Keisler quantifiers so that the same $K$ works for all powers, and prove the direct implication to Erdős's square question in dimension $n$. Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or a no-placeholder, pinned Lean formalization of the implication, with literal axiom output, plus the finite-cover regression for all discrete sets tested.

The substantial obligation is an independent reconstruction of the 1967 proof, starting with its four dimension-theory lemmas, their use of nondegenerate continua and sphere-avoidance families, the transfinite selection principle, and the argument extending all finite powers to countable products. Source review must distinguish Theorem 1 (one specified power) from Theorem 2 (all powers at once) and check the full dimension hypotheses. The 54 integer substitutions do not certify any of those topological existence steps.
