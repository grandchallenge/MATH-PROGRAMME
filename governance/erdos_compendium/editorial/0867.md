## The question in plain language

Choose a set of distinct positive integers from $1$ to $N$, and write them in increasing order,

$$
A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_t\}.
$$

Call the set *admissible* if **no sum of two or more consecutive entries** equals another entry of the set. The phrase *two or more* is essential: a one-term sum would equal its own entry and make every nonempty set inadmissible. Erdős Problem [#867](https://www.erdosproblems.com/867) asked whether every admissible set satisfies

$$
|A|\leq \frac N2+O(1).
$$

The $O(1)$ means an additive constant independent of $N$. The proposed density $1/2$ is **false**: admissible sets can occupy a larger fixed proportion of the integers, even as $N$ grows. This is a finite extremal question, distinct from the related question about the density of a *single infinite* admissible sequence ([#839](https://www.erdosproblems.com/839)).

## A small example

Take

$$
A=\{4,5,6,7,8,10,12,14,16,17,19,20\}\subseteq[1,20].
$$

It has twelve members, already exceeding $20/2+1$. A direct check of every consecutive block of length at least two finds no block sum in $A$. An independent exhaustive search checks all

$$
\binom{20}{13}=77\,520
$$

thirteen-element subsets and finds that none is admissible. Hence the **exact finite optimum** is $f(20)=12$. The same two-method computation gives $f(10)=6$ and $f(15)=9$. These small cases illustrate the phenomenon but do not establish a density larger than one-half for arbitrarily large $N$.

## What is known

In the original 1992 discussion, Erdős proposed the half-density upper bound (printed page 43 of *Some of My Forgotten Problems in Number Theory*). Róbert Freud later constructed admissible sequences with asymptotic density at least $19/36>1/2$; thus the proposal fails. Don Coppersmith and Steven Phillips improved the **finite** construction and the general upper bound in their 1996 paper, *On a Question of Erdös on Subsequence Sums*, *SIAM Journal on Discrete Mathematics* **9**(2), 173–177:

$$
\frac{13}{24}N-O(1)\le f(N)
\le \left(\frac23-\frac1{512}\right)N+\log N.
$$

The publisher's primary abstract states both coefficients. They show that the correct extremal density lies strictly above $1/2$ and below $2/3$. They do **not** establish an exact limiting density or resolve the related infinite-sequence problem. This article reports the published results; it does not claim an independent proof audit of every step of the 1996 paper.

## GCL's current state

Here is a useful elementary upper-bound mechanism. Fix an integer $x>0$ and let $t$ be the number of selected elements in $(x,2x]$. There are $t-1$ sums of **consecutive pairs among those elements**. These sums are strictly increasing, lie in $(2x,4x]$, and cannot belong to $A$. That interval contains $2x$ integers, so at most $2x-(t-1)$ of them can be selected. Therefore

$$
|A\cap(x,4x]|\le t+2x-(t-1)=2x+1.
$$

Apply this estimate to disjoint geometric bands $(N/4,N]$, $(N/16,N/4]$, and so on, adjusting integer endpoints. The geometric series gives

$$
|A|\le \frac23 N+O(\log N).
$$

This is weaker than the published improved upper coefficient, but it is transparent and independently checkable. The improvement from $2/3$ to $2/3-1/512$ requires additional combinatorial arguments not reproduced here.
