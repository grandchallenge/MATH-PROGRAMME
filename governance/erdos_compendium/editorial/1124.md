## The question in plain language

Can a **square** be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into a **circle of the same area**?

[Erdős Problem #1124](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1124) is Tarski's circle-squaring problem, admired by Erdős. The surprising answer is **yes**, even when the allowed motions are **translations only**.

The word “pieces” is essential. The existence theorem does not say the square can be cut along a finite collection of straight lines or with ordinary physical scissors. The pieces in the original result are highly nonconstructive.

## A small example

A unit square has area one. A disk with the same area must have radius

$$
r=\frac1{\sqrt{\pi}},
$$

because its area is $\pi r^2=1$.

This exact calculation establishes the necessary area normalization. It does not produce even one of the pieces required to translate the square into the disk.

Translations preserve the geometry of every piece. The hard part is choosing a finite partition that matches the two shapes globally despite their different boundaries.

## What is known

**Miklós Laczkovich (1990)** established Tarski's conjecture in the affirmative, proving a finite **translation** equidecomposition between a disk and a square of equal area. It is a striking application of discrepancy estimates and combinatorics to geometric dissection.

Later work, including Marks and Unger, examined versions with additional regularity of the pieces, notably Borel equidecompositions. Such refinements should be treated as distinct theorems with their own assumptions, not as automatic consequences of the 1990 construction.

The original pieces need not be individually Lebesgue measurable, so a naive measure argument about each piece is not a proof of the existence theorem.

## What remains

Read the original theorem and proof carefully, including the number and regularity of pieces, whether translations alone suffice, and subsequent Borel-strengthened results. An illustrative grid approximation would remain only an approximation.
