## The question in plain language

Suppose we choose two integers $a,b\geq 2$ having no common factor greater than $1$. We are allowed to use the numbers

$$
a^k b^\ell,\qquad k,\ell\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.
$$

For example, with $a=3$ and $b=5$, the permitted numbers begin with $1,3,5,9,15,25,27,45,75,81,\ldots$. In a sum, **each permitted number may be used at most once**. Does there exist a threshold $N(a,b)$ such that *every* integer greater than that threshold is a sum of these distinct permitted numbers?

Yes. B. J. Birch proved this in 1959. A set with this eventual representation property is called *additively complete*. It is not required to represent every small number, and it is not enough merely to represent infinitely many large numbers: the assertion covers **every** integer beyond a fixed, base-dependent threshold.

The assumptions $a,b\geq2$ matter. The shortened wording “$\gcd(a,b)=1$” alone, found in some catalogue descriptions, would wrongly allow $a=1,b=3$: then only powers of $3$ are available, and numbers congruent to $2$ modulo $3$ cannot be expressed as sums of distinct such powers.

## A small example

For $a=3$ and $b=5$, the permitted numbers include $3^0 5^0=1$, $3$, $5$, $9$, $15$, $25$, $27$ and $45$. Here are four distinct-summand representations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
23 &= 15+5+3,\\
37 &= 25+9+3,\\
41 &= 25+15+1,\\
100 &= 45+25+15+9+5+1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Birch states $N(3,5)=22$ on p. 370 of the original paper. We independently checked the finite interval $1\leq n\leq500$ by enumerating all distinct permitted numbers at most $500$, and updating the reachable subset sums once for each permitted number. The *only* unreachable positive integers in that interval are

$$
2,\quad 7,\quad 11,\quad 22.
$$

Thus the explicit computation verifies coverage for $23$ through $500$ and matches Birch's reported threshold in that range. **A finite computation cannot establish that every integer above $500$ is representable.** The general assertion is supplied by Birch's mathematical proof, not by this experiment.

For reproducibility, the source-audit return provides a small Python subset-sum program and its exact expected result.

## What is known

**Classically proved.** Birch, *Note on a problem of Erdős* (1959), pp. 370–373, establishes eventual completeness for coprime bases greater than $1$. The paper begins with the main theorem (p. 370), then develops preliminary lemmas and the progression/gap argument in the subsequent pages. We have inspected the primary paper's statement and proof structure, but have not independently checked every proof step.

Birch records Davenport's observation that the exponent of one base can be bounded independently of the represented integer. The paper explicitly says it omits the details of that strengthening. Later studies obtain quantitative bounds; the maintained catalogue mentions Hegyvári (2000), Fang and Chen (2017), and Yu (2024). Those quantitative refinements have **not** been independently verified for this article and are not used here to prove the main claim.

Cassels (1960), pp. 111–124, gives a more general theory of complete sets of distinct summands. The maintained catalogue reports that it implies Birch's result. We have verified the bibliographic identity and location, but not replayed the special-case deduction.

**Important wording issue.** On p. 370, the scan of Birch's theorem appears to say that the exponent pairs are *positive* integers. Taken literally, that would make all summands divisible by $ab$ and contradict the claimed representation of arbitrary large integers. The rest of the discussion, including the binary-base observation, requires zero exponents. The pinned FormalConjectures declaration explicitly uses $k,\ell\in\mathbb N$ and $a,b\geq2$. This ambiguity is recorded as a source-normalization exception, not silently repaired.
