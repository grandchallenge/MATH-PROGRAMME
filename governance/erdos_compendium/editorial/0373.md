## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 373 asks about products of factorials. Find positive integers $n$ and a finite nonincreasing list $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ such that

$$
n! = a_1!a_2!\cdots a_k!,
$$

where every $a_i$ is at least $2$ and $a_1<n-1$. The question is whether only finitely many such lists exist. “Finitely many” concerns the complete data $(n,a_1,\ldots,a_k)$: it is stronger than saying that a search has found no examples up to a large bound.

The strict inequality is essential. If the largest factor were allowed to be $(n-1)!$, then any factorisation of $n$ into factorials could give a solution after multiplying by $(n-1)!$. The problem deliberately removes this trivial source. The ordering is only a normalization, so the same factors are not counted again in different orders.

The live statement and the pinned formal statement agree on this core shape. The formal provider represents a solution by a natural number paired with a list, requires the list to be pairwise descending, and uses `headI < n - 1` together with $1<a$ for every list member. That encoding is a formal interface, not itself a proof.

## A small example

The largest solution listed in the source material is an exact identity:

$$
16! = 14!\,5!\,2! = 20\,922\,789\,888\,000.
$$

It satisfies $15>14\geq5\geq2\geq2$. A second familiar identity is

$$
10! = 7!\,6! = 3\,628\,800.
$$

Both calculations use ordinary integer multiplication, so they can be reproduced without floating-point arithmetic. For example, this exact check succeeds:

```python
from math import factorial, prod
n, factors = 16, (14, 5, 2)
assert factorial(n) == prod(factorial(a) for a in factors)
assert n - 1 > factors[0] >= factors[1] >= factors[2] >= 2
```

I also ran a recursive exact-integer enumeration for $2\leq n\leq16$, allowing nonincreasing factors from $2$ through $n-2$ and pruning only when the partial product exceeded $n!$. It returned precisely

$$
9!=7!\,3!\,3!\,2!,\qquad 10!=7!\,6!,\qquad 10!=7!\,5!\,3!,\qquad 16!=14!\,5!\,2!.
$$

This finite check confirms the displayed examples and gives no evidence about larger $n$. It cannot establish finiteness, because a finite search cannot rule out a later solution.

## What is known

The primary source in the packet is P. Erdős, “Problems and results on number theoretic properties of consecutive integers and related questions,” *Proceedings of the Fifth Manitoba Conference on Numerical Mathematics* (1976), pp. 25–44. On printed p. 28, Theorem 2 says that if

$$
P(n(n-1))>4\log n,
$$

then, for sufficiently large $n$, the factorial equation has only the paper’s “trivial” solutions. Here $P(m)$ means the largest prime factor of $m$. Since Problem 373 excludes those trivial solutions, an eventual proof of this prime-factor inequality would imply the requested finiteness. Erdős states this as a conditional route; the paper does not prove the inequality and therefore does not prove Problem 373 unconditionally.

The maintained page reports a second conditional route involving $P(n(n+1))/\log n\to\infty$, as well as later conditional and quantitative work. Those reports are useful leads, but they do not change the status of the exact problem without a source-level argument matching its quantifiers and hypotheses. The live entry also records conjectured small solutions, including the four identities reproduced above. A conjectured complete list is not a proof that the list is complete.
