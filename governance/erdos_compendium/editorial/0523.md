**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #523 is **PROVED**. It asks for the asymptotically exact largest magnitude of a random polynomial with independent fair signs on the **complex unit circle**. Salem and Zygmund obtained the order $\sqrt{n\log n}$ in 1954; Gábor Halász established the precise almost-sure coefficient **one** in 1973.

## The question in plain language

Choose one infinite sequence of independent random signs $\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_1,\dots$, each taking $-1$ or $+1$ with equal probability. Form the degree‑$n$ Littlewood polynomial

$$
P_n(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon_kz^k.
$$

Define its **maximum modulus on the unit circle**

$$
M_n=\max_{|z|=1}|P_n(z)|.
$$

Is there a deterministic positive $C$, independent of the random sign sequence, such that

$$
\boxed{
M_n=(C+o(1))\sqrt{n\log n}
\quad\text{almost surely}?
}
$$

The answer is affirmative with $C=1$. Here the logarithm is natural, $n$ goes to infinity and the error term tends to zero for almost every fixed infinite sign sequence.

The circle includes all complex numbers $z=e^{it}$. Evaluating only the endpoints $z=\pm1$ is a different problem: there are many angular opportunities for a large trigonometric sum, and this maximization is where the extra factor $\sqrt{\log n}$ emerges.

## A small example

For the particular deterministic sign choice $\varepsilon_k=1$ for all $k$, the polynomial is

$$
P_n(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^n.
$$

At $z=1$ it has magnitude $n+1$. By the triangle inequality no point on the unit circle has larger magnitude, so $M_n=n+1$. This is much greater than $\sqrt{n\log n}$ for large $n$.

This does **not** contradict the theorem. The assertion is *almost surely* for the random sequence; the deterministic all-positive configuration is an exceptional sequence of probability zero in the infinite product space.

Now use an exact identity valid for **every** sign choice. Orthogonality of complex exponentials gives

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
|P_n(e^{it})|^2\,dt
=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\varepsilon_k|^2=n+1.
$$

Since an average cannot exceed a maximum, and a sum cannot exceed the sum of the absolute values,

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{n+1}\le M_n\le n+1.}
$$

These elementary bounds are useful checks but leave a vast gap. Even their lower bound is missing the $\sqrt{\log n}$ factor of the random supremum theorem. The hard mathematics comes from probabilistic control **uniformly over the entire circle**.

## What is known

### The original 1961 formulation

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed [p.253](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=33), Section V.3, Erdős records the Salem–Zygmund result that there exist constants $c_1,c_2>0$ such that for almost all sign sequences and all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
c_1\sqrt{n\log n}<M_n<c_2\sqrt{n\log n}.
$$

The original page was inspected directly. Erdős conjectures a fixed asymptotic constant $C$ and also proposes a weaker, fixed-degree statement in which all but a negligible fraction of finite sign vectors have maximum close to $C\sqrt{n\log n}$.

The original source is R. Salem and A. Zygmund, *Some properties of trigonometric series whose terms have random signs*, *Acta Mathematica* **91** (1954), 245–301, [DOI 10.1007/BF02393433](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02393433). Its primary bibliographic record was checked. The full original proof was not independently reproduced.

### Halász's exact almost-sure result

Gábor Halász, *On a result of Salem and Zygmund concerning random polynomials*, *Studia Scientiarum Mathematicarum Hungarica* **8** (1973), 369–377, established the conjecture with

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_n}{\sqrt{n\log n}}=1
\quad\text{almost surely}.
}
$$

The exact historical reference and normalization are recorded in the maintained [Erdős #523 LaTeX source](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/523). The [problem page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/523), last edited February 1, 2026, labels this result **PROVED**.

Halász's complete 1973 primary proof was not retrieved in this tranche. Therefore the result is correctly attributed to its historical author and established status, but no independent rederivation of the probability-one asymptotic or its constants is claimed.

### A rigorous reduction from the circle to a finite grid

A useful computational and pedagogical bridge is **deterministic discretization**. Write $Q_n(t)=P_n(e^{it})$. Differentiation gives

$$
|Q_n'(t)|\le\sum_{k=1}^{n}k
=\frac{n(n+1)}2=:L_n.
$$

Select $m$ equally spaced angles $t_j=2\pi j/m$, $j=0,\ldots,m-1$. Every angle is within distance at most $\pi/m$ of a grid angle, so the mean-value inequality yields

$$
\boxed{
0\le M_n-\max_{0\le j<m}|Q_n(t_j)|
\le\frac{\pi n(n+1)}{2m}.
}
$$

Taking, for instance, $m=n^4+1$ makes this error much smaller than $\sqrt{n\log n}$. A sufficiently precise **probabilistic theorem for the grid maximum** could therefore transfer to the continuous circle. The deterministic grid bound is not that probabilistic theorem.

There is also an **exact discrete Parseval identity** when $m>n$. With $\omega_m=e^{2\pi i/m}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}|P_n(\omega_m^j)|^2
&=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{n}
\varepsilon_k\varepsilon_\ell
\frac1m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\omega_m^{j(k-\ell)}\\
&=n+1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The inner root-of-unity average is one when $k=\ell$ and zero otherwise, since $|k-\ell|<m$. This identity can be checked **without any floating-point approximation**, using integer exponent differences modulo $m$.
