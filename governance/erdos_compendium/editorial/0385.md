## The question in plain language

For an integer $m>1$, let $p(m)$ be its least prime divisor. Problem 385 studies the largest value of $m+p(m)$ obtained from a composite integer below $n$:

$$
F(n)=\max\{m+p(m):1<m<n,\ m\text{ composite}\}.
$$

The first question asks whether $F(n)>n$ for every sufficiently large $n$. In words, once $n$ is large enough, must there always be a composite $m<n$ whose least prime factor reaches far enough to make $m+p(m)$ pass $n$? The second asks whether the excess $F(n)-n$ eventually becomes arbitrarily large. The maintained page also records a stronger-looking scale question: might the excess be nearly as large as $\sqrt n$?

Erdős’s original 1979 wording has the opposite logical polarity: is $F(n)\le n$ true for infinitely many $n$? This is equivalent to asking whether the first maintained-page question has a negative answer. The quantifiers matter: a long finite computation cannot establish either an eventual statement or its negation.

## A small example

Take $n=10$. The composite integers below $10$ are $4,6,8,9$. Their least prime divisors and resulting sums are

$$
(m,p(m),m+p(m))=(4,2,6),(6,2,8),(8,2,10),(9,3,12).
$$

Therefore $F(10)=12$, so $F(10)>10$. This can be reproduced with exact integer arithmetic:

```python
from math import isqrt

n = 10
rows = []
for m in range(1, n):
    divisors = [d for d in range(2, isqrt(m) + 1) if m % d == 0]
    if divisors:
        p = min(divisors)
        rows.append((m, p, m + p))
assert rows == [(4, 2, 6), (6, 2, 8), (8, 2, 10), (9, 3, 12)]
assert max(value for _, _, value in rows) == 12
```

The calculation verifies one finite instance and checks the definition directly. It says nothing about how often the inequality holds later, and it cannot certify a threshold that works for all sufficiently large $n$.

## What is known

The primary source is Paul Erdős, “Some unconventional problems in number theory,” *Acta Mathematica Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae* 33 (1979), 71–80, printed p. 73. There Erdős defines the same $F(n)$ using composite $m$ with $1<m<n$, asks whether $F(n)\le n$ has infinitely many solutions, and says that plausible conjectures on primes would imply only finitely many. The source presents this as a problem, not as a theorem resolving it.

The maintained [Problem 385 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/385) records two comments dated 3 September and 23 September 2026. They report segmented-sieve computations finding no further exceptions in ranges reaching $10^{11}$ and approximately $1.0011\times10^{12}$, together with reported lower bounds for the excess on subranges. The page itself warns that comments are user responsibility and are not verified for correctness. They are useful leads, not established external proofs or GCL-certified computation.

Terence Tao’s [2024 discussion](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2024/08/19/erdos-problem-385-the-parity-problem-and-siegel-zeroes/) explains why the unresolved part is connected to covering intervals by residue classes and to the difficulty of controlling semiprimes at intermediate scales. That exposition is evidence about the analytic shape of the obstacle, not a proof of either question. The live page reports zero proof expositions and zero proof claims.

## What remains

The smallest clean successor is a bounded, source-reproducible finite audit rather than an attempted solution. Fix a numerical bound $B$, implement the recurrence

$$
F(n)=\max\bigl(F(n-1),(n-1)+p(n-1)\bigr),
$$

including the second term only when $n-1$ is composite, using an exact segmented least-prime-factor sieve. Publish the bound, source revision, output hash, and the complete exception list for $F(n)\le n$. Acceptance is byte-for-byte agreement with an independently rerun implementation and direct checks at every reported exception; a mismatch falsifies the certificate and stops the task. The stopping rule is the fixed bound and two agreeing runs, not an extrapolation to infinity.

The global missing ingredient is not finite enumeration. It is a theorem controlling the relevant composite or semiprime survivors uniformly over the intermediate interval scales that can defeat $m+p(m)>n$. No such theorem was established in this audit.
