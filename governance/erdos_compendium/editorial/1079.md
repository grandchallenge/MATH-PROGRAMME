## The question in plain language

Turán's theorem gives the maximum number of edges in a graph without a complete graph of prescribed order. [Erdős Problem #1079](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1079) asks whether enough edges also force a dense **neighborhood**.

Fix $r\ge4$. When a graph $G$ has at least $\mathrm{ex}(n,K_r)$ edges, then either it is the extremal Turán graph $T_{r-1}(n)$, or there is a vertex $v$ of degree $d$ proportional to $n$ whose neighbors span more edges than a $K_{r-1}$‑free graph could contain:

$$
e(G[N(v)])\ge\mathrm{ex}(d,K_{r-1})+1.
$$

The extremal exception is essential.

## A small example

For $r=4$, take $G=K_4$. It has six edges, while $\mathrm{ex}(4,K_4)=5$. Every vertex has three neighbors, which themselves form $K_3$ with three edges. But $\mathrm{ex}(3,K_3)=2$. Thus there is exactly one extra neighborhood edge.

By contrast, the balanced tripartite $T_3(4)$ has five edges and is the allowed exception. These are elementary instances, not the general theorem.

## What is known

**Bollobás and Thomason proved the conjecture** in [*Dense neighbourhoods and Turán's theorem*](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/4508/), *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series B* 31 (1981), 111–114. Their own public abstract confirms the high-degree neighborhood conclusion and the exact Turán exception. Bondy (1983) subsequently showed that above the strict Turán edge threshold a maximum-degree vertex can be chosen.

Notation is easy to confuse. The authors write $t_s(n)$ for the $s$‑partite Turán edge count, so $t_{r-1}(n)=\mathrm{ex}(n,K_r)$.
