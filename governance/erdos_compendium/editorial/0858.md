## The question in plain language

A set of positive integers is *primitive* when no member divides another. Erdős [Problem #858](https://www.erdosproblems.com/858) asks about a weaker restriction: two members $a<b$ are forbidden together only if $b=at$ and **every prime factor of $t$ is larger than $a$**. Write $p_{\min}(t)$ for the least prime factor of $t\ge2$. The rule is

$$
\text{no }a,b\in A,\quad a<b,\quad b=at,\quad p_{\min}(t)>a.
$$

For each upper limit $N$, choose a set $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ satisfying the rule and make its reciprocal sum as large as possible:

$$
M(N)=\max_{A\text{ admissible}}\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a.
$$

The original question asks how $M(N)/\log N$ behaves as $N$ grows. The crucial point is that the optimizing set **may change with $N$**. This is not the same as fixing one infinite admissible set and studying its partial sums.

## A small example

For $N=6$, the set $\{2,3,4,5\}$ is admissible. Although $2$ divides $4$, the quotient is $2$, whose smallest prime factor is not greater than $2$. The pair $2,6$ is forbidden, however, because $6=2\cdot3$ and $3>2$.

The reciprocal weight of $\{2,3,4,5\}$ is

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15=\frac{77}{60}.
$$

An independent exhaustive search of all subsets of $\{1,\ldots,6\}$ confirms that $77/60$ is the largest possible weight. A second method, based on weighted antichains in a tree, produces the same answer. Further exact checks give:

| $N$ | Maximum reciprocal sum $M(N)$ |
|---:|---:|
| 4 | $13/12$ |
| 5 | $77/60$ |
| 6 | $77/60$ |
| 7 | $599/420$ |
| 10 | $4189/2520$ |

These are **finite calculations**, not an experimental proof of an asymptotic constant.

There is also a simple construction that works for every $N$: take all integers strictly larger than $\sqrt N$. If $a>\sqrt N$ and $p_{\min}(t)>a$, then $t>a$, so $at>a^2>N$. Thus no forbidden pair fits inside the interval. The sum of reciprocals in this terminal interval is

$$
\sum_{\sqrt N<a\le N}\frac1a=\frac12\log N+O(1).
$$

Therefore the normalized optimum cannot approach zero. This does not contradict the earlier theorem that **each fixed infinite admissible set** has reciprocal partial sums $o(\log N)$: taking a new optimizing set at every $N$ changes the quantifiers.

## What is known

Erdős stated the problem in [1970, printed page 128](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1970-21.pdf). He distinguished the maximum over a finite set from results of Alexander and Erdős–Sárközi–Szemerédi for a single infinite sequence. The modern [Erdős Problems catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/858) now marks #858 **solved**. Its April 24, 2026 revision attributes the result to Przemek Chojecki working with GPT-5.4 Pro and reports

$$
M(N)=(c+o(1))\log N,\qquad c\approx0.618\ldots.
$$

The reported numerical value is not a GCL-certified theorem. A later, independently authored primary manuscript by Paul Pajo, [*Erdős Problem #858: A Flow-Network Proof*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404185689_Erdos_Problem_858_A_Flow-Network_Proof), dated April 25, 2026, states a more precise value $c\approx0.6187712111$. It describes a tree-and-flow proof using a threshold function and Mertens-type prime estimates. We have inspected its finite structural lemmas and the statement of its analytic argument, but have not independently checked the complete proof. The original Chojecki proof artifact and exact version remain to be recovered from the catalogue discussion.

The result therefore has three distinct evidence levels: the catalogue's solved status, a publicly accessible mathematical proof manuscript, and GCL's own limited finite verification. Only the third is independently checked here.

## GCL's current state

A useful reduction appears in Pajo's manuscript. For $b>1$, define its *parent* to be the largest proper divisor $a$ such that the quotient $b/a$ has no prime factor at most $a$:

$$
\\mathrm{parent}(b)=
\max\{a:a\mid b,\ a<b,\ p_{\min}(b/a)>a\}.
$$

The parent is always defined because $a=1$ qualifies. Following parents produces a rooted tree, and the forbidden-pair relation corresponds to ancestry in that tree. In ordinary language, a valid set is a selection of vertices with **no chosen vertex above another chosen vertex**. Such a selection is called an *antichain*.

For a finite tree, a maximum-weight antichain can be computed recursively. At a vertex $a$, either choose $a$ itself, worth $1/a$, or reject it and combine the best antichains of all its children. The recursion is

$$
F(a)=\max\left\{\frac1a,\ \sum_{b:\\mathrm{parent}(b)=a}F(b)\right\}.
$$

We independently implemented this recurrence and checked that it agrees with **literal exhaustive subset search** for every $N\le15$. We also compared ancestry with the original divisibility predicate for every pair $1\le a<b\le100$. Both tests passed. The exact rational fixture is saved as `governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_858.py` on the cohort working branch, pending authenticated source return and branch admission.

The finite recursion is not a proof of the published asymptotic. Passing from exact trees to the constant $0.618\ldots$ requires uniform estimates for reciprocal sums over primes and control of the transition between favorable and unfavorable regions of the tree. Those are separate analytical obligations.

## What remains

The first bounded mathematical task is to turn the tree correspondence and weighted-antichain identity into a short, fully checked lemma, including the exceptional cases $a=1$, repeated prime factors in $t$, and the undefined least-prime-factor expression at $t=1$. A Lean proof or a line-by-line mathematical verification would be appropriate. An explicit pair $(a,b)$ contradicting the ancestry equivalence would falsify the proposed reduction.

The next task is a primary-source audit of the Chojecki/GPT-5.4 Pro proof itself: locate the dated artifact, identify its theorem and constant, and compare every hypothesis with the original 1970 statement. Only then should the analytic argument of that proof and the later flow-network manuscript be reviewed, particularly the uniformity of the prime-sum estimates and the treatment of threshold regions. Neither task should relabel the problem as open or claim independent certification prematurely.
