## The question in plain language

A graph needs infinitely many colours if no finite collection of colours can assign different colours to adjacent vertices. Such a graph must contain odd cycles, since a graph without any odd cycle is bipartite and two-colourable. But how **many different odd lengths** must occur?

[Erdős Problem #55](https://www.erdosproblems.com/55), attributed to Erdős and Hajnal, asks whether the reciprocal sum of the distinct odd cycle lengths in every graph with infinite chromatic number must diverge:

$$
\sum_{\ell\in C_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)}\frac1\ell=\infty.
$$

The answer is **yes**, proved by Hong Liu and Richard Montgomery.

## A small example

In the complete graph on seven vertices, cycles occur at odd lengths 3, 5 and 7. The sum of the reciprocals of those **distinct lengths** is

$$
\frac13+\frac15+\frac17=\frac{71}{105}.
$$

If a graph contains 500 triangles, the contribution from length three is still only $1/3$: the theorem counts lengths, not individual cycles.

## What is known

Liu and Montgomery, *A solution to Erdős and Hajnal's odd cycle problem*, *Journal of the American Mathematical Society* **36** (2023), 1191–1234, prove the stronger quantitative estimate

$$
\sum_{\ell\in C_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)}\frac1\ell
\ge\left(\frac12-o_k(1)\right)\log k
$$

for graphs of chromatic number $k$ as $k$ increases. One ingredient is their Theorem 1.4, which ensures that every odd integer in a long multiplicative interval occurs as a cycle length. Since about half the integers in a long interval are odd, its reciprocal sum grows with leading coefficient **one-half**, not one.

An infinite-chromatic graph contains finite subgraphs with arbitrarily large chromatic number, by the compactness theorem for finite graph colouring. Those finite subgraphs inherit their cycle lengths from the original graph, so the quantitative bound forces an unbounded reciprocal sum.

The mathematical problem is settled in the published literature; the proof has not been independently re-created or kernel-certified by GCL.

## What remains

The original divergence problem is resolved. Further work can formalize the compactness and reciprocal-sum deductions from a precisely source-pinned finite-chromatic theorem. That is a limited formalization target, not a new answer to Erdős and Hajnal.
