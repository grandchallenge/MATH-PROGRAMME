## The question in plain language

Take a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers

$$
n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots.
$$

For each $n_i$, choose one residue $a_i$ modulo $n_i$ to forbid. The integers that escape every forbidden class are

$$
A(a)=\{m\in\mathbb Z: m\not\equiv a_i\pmod {n_i}\text{ for every }i\}.
$$

The hypothesis is a strong one: for every possible infinite choice of residues, $A(a)$ has natural density zero. In plain language, every way of placing one forbidden congruence class at each modulus eventually covers almost all integers.

The question asks whether this covering can always be witnessed by a finite prefix, uniformly over the placements. Formally, is it true that

$$
\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists k\ \forall(a_i),\quad
\mathrm{dens}\{m\in\mathbb Z:m\not\equiv a_i\pmod {n_i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k\}<\epsilon?
$$

The order of the quantifiers matters. One $k$ must work for every residue choice; it is not enough that each choice has its own good prefix. The original statement appears in Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 29 of the scan.

The pinned Lean statement uses zero-based functions, `StrictMono n`, positive moduli, and two-sided integer density through `Set.HasIntDensity`. Its prefix condition is `i < k`. Those conventions are close to the printed problem, but the source audit correctly treats the passage between the printed density convention and the formal one as a semantic comparison to be checked, not as an automatic equivalence.

## A small example

For a finite prefix with moduli $(2,3,4,5,7)$, every residue choice is periodic with period

$$
L=\mathrm{lcm}(2,3,4,5,7)=420.
$$

There are $2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot5\cdot7=840$ choices. The following exact enumeration counts the integers in one period that avoid all five forbidden classes.

```python
from itertools import product
from math import lcm

q = (2, 3, 4, 5, 7)
L = lcm(*q)
counts = []
for residues in product(*(range(n) for n in q)):
    count = sum(all(x % n != a for n, a in zip(q, residues))
                for x in range(L))
    counts.append((count, residues))

maximum = max(count for count, _ in counts)
zero_count = next(count for count, a in counts
                  if a == (0, 0, 0, 0, 0))
print(L, len(counts), maximum, zero_count)
```

It prints `420 840 96 96`. Thus the largest uncovered density in this finite experiment is $96/420=8/35$, and the all-zero residues attain that maximum. They are not the only maximizers: the calculation finds 420 tied choices. This is a checked instance of the finite sieving interface, not a proof for every collection of moduli.

## What is known

The standard affirmative strategy has two ingredients. First choose all residues to be zero. The uncovered set is then the set of integers divisible by none of $n_1,n_2,\ldots$. The hypothesis says its density is zero, so the union of the multiples has density one.

Davenport and Erdős prove the needed finite-stage density statement in “On sequences of positive integers,” *Acta Arithmetica* 2 (1936), 147–151, DOI [10.4064/aa-2-1-147-151](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-2-1-147-151). Their Theorem 1(a,b), on pp. 149–150, identifies the relevant limiting density of the union of multiples with the increasing limit of the finite unions. Consequently, for some finite $k$, the all-zero prefix leaves density below any prescribed $epsilon$.

The second ingredient is the finite sieving assertion usually attributed to Rogers: for fixed positive moduli, the uncovered density is largest when every forbidden residue is zero. If this inequality is available in exactly the required form, then every arbitrary residue choice on the same prefix has uncovered density no larger than the all-zero choice. The two ingredients give the desired uniform quantifier order.

This is the classical proof interface reported by the source audit. The original Erdős–Graham page is a problem statement, not the proof of the resolution. The exact Halberstam and Roth, *Sequences*, Vol. I (1966), Chapter V.3, pp. 242–244, pages cited for Rogers' result were not obtained in this audit. Tao's exposition points to the same result, but it is secondary evidence and does not repair the missing primary-source fidelity check. The full affirmative resolution therefore remains an externally reported argument whose final source bridge is not independently verified here.
