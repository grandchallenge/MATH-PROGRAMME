## The question in plain language

Assign every positive integer a sign $f(n)\in\{-1,1\}$. For a fixed positive common difference $d$, consider **every finite arithmetic progression** with that difference, of any length and any starting point. Its discrepancy is the absolute sum of the signs on its members.

Erdős asked for the smallest function $h(d)$ such that **one single infinite signing** can keep the discrepancy of *every* progression of difference $d$ at most $h(d)$, simultaneously for all $d\ge1$. The quantifiers matter: choosing a different signing for each $d$ would be much easier. The finite progressions need not start at one or have a prescribed length.

## A small example

For $d=1$, the alternating signing $f(n)=(-1)^n$ keeps the sum on every interval between $-1$ and $1$. For $d=2$, however, that same signing is constant along each even or odd progression, so its discrepancy grows with the progression length. Thus an argument for one difference cannot be reused indiscriminately for all differences.

For any progression $a,a+d,\ldots,a+(m-1)d$, the sum is a difference of two prefix sums along the residue class $a\bmod d$. Uniform boundedness of those residue-class prefix sums implies bounded discrepancy for the corresponding difference $d$.

## What is known

The optimal growth of $h(d)$ remains **OPEN**. Cantor, Erdős, Schreiber and Straus established a signing for which $h(d)\ll d!$. Van der Waerden's theorem forces $h(d)$ to be unbounded as $d$ grows. K. F. Roth's 1964 discrepancy theorem supplies the quantitative lower estimate $h(d)\gg d^{1/2}$.

József Beck, in *A discrepancy problem: balancing infinite dimensional vectors* (2017), improved the existence upper bound to $h(d)\le C_\varepsilon d^{8+\varepsilon}$ for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$. This polynomial upper bound does **not** prove that exponent $8$ is optimal or that a square-root estimate can be attained. The catalogue references to [Be17] and [Ro64] are primary-source trails; the full proofs have not been independently replayed.

## What remains

A bounded successor exhaustively searches sign assignments on $[1,12]$ and computes maximum discrepancies for each difference $d\le4$ and all possible starts and lengths, then compares an independent residue-prefix implementation. Acceptance: the alternating signing has discrepancy at most one for $d=1$ and unbounded two-step discrepancies as its domain grows. Finite witnesses are **not** a construction of the required infinite signing.
