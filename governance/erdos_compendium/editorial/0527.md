**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #527 is **PROVED**. It asks whether a random power series with coefficients that decay slightly faster than $n^{-1/2}$ must converge **somewhere on the unit circle**, for almost every random choice of signs. Marcus Michelen and Mehtaab Sawhney answered yes in 2025 and proved a stronger statement: the set of convergence points almost surely has **Hausdorff dimension one**.

## The question in plain language

Let $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ be deterministic complex numbers. Independently choose random signs $\varepsilon_n\in\{-1,+1\}$ with equal probabilities, and consider the series

$$
P(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_na_nz^n,
\qquad |z|=1.
$$

Assume that

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n\,|a_n|\longrightarrow0.}
$$

Is there, with probability one over the signs, **at least one** point $z$ of the unit circle at which the series converges?

The point $z$ may depend on the entire infinite sign sequence. This is crucial: the theorem asserts that almost every random realization has **some** suitable point. It does not identify a fixed deterministic $z$ that works for almost every realization.

The original problem also emphasizes the interesting case

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n|^2=\infty.
$$

If the squares are summable, convergence already occurs almost surely at any fixed point of the circle by standard independent-series theorems. When the squares diverge, the series almost surely diverges at **each fixed point** and at **Lebesgue-almost-every point** for almost every sign realization. The surprise is that exceptional convergence points may nevertheless exist.

## A small example

Consider coefficients

$$
a_n=\frac1{\sqrt{n(1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor)}}.
$$

They satisfy the crucial decay condition because

$$
\sqrt n\,a_n
=\frac1{\sqrt{1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor}}\longrightarrow0.
$$

But their squares are **not summable**. Group the terms into dyadic blocks $2^j\le n<2^{j+1}$. On that block,

$$
|a_n|^2=\frac1{n(j+1)}
\ge\frac1{2^{j+1}(j+1)}.
$$

There are $2^j$ terms, so each block contributes at least

$$
\sum_{n=2^j}^{2^{j+1}-1}|a_n|^2
\ge\frac1{2(j+1)}.
$$

The sum of these lower bounds diverges, since it is half the harmonic series. Thus this sequence lies in the **nontrivial** regime: smaller than $1/\sqrt n$ asymptotically, yet with infinite total coefficient-square mass.

At any fixed point $|z|=1$, independence and zero mean give

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E\left|
\sum_{n=m}^{N}\varepsilon_na_nz^n
\right|^2
=\sum_{n=m}^{N}|a_n|^2.
}
$$

The equality is exact: cross terms vanish because $\mathbb E(\varepsilon_j\varepsilon_k)=0$ for distinct $j,k$. This demonstrates that **no single fixed point** gets square-summable tails in this example.

Still, the 2025 theorem implies that for almost every random sign realization there exists a **realization-dependent** set of convergent points on the circle of full Hausdorff dimension one.

This does not contradict divergence at almost every point in arc-length measure. A set can have Hausdorff dimension one while having **Lebesgue measure zero**.

## What is known

### Erdős's original conjecture and the sharp threshold

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.254](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=34), Erdős records joint work with Aryeh Dvoretzky, *Divergence of random power series*, *Michigan Mathematical Journal* **6** (1959), 343–347. The original 1961 page was directly inspected.

Dvoretzky and Erdős proved that if the coefficient magnitudes are bounded **below** by a positive constant times $n^{-1/2}$, then the random series almost surely **diverges at every point** of the unit circle. Erdős asks if their threshold is sharp in the converse direction: does the much weaker assumption $|a_n|=o(n^{-1/2})$ guarantee a convergent point, almost surely?

An earlier example satisfying a monotonicity condition $|a_{n+1}|\le|a_n|$ was discussed in the original paper. Erdős's question does **not** impose that monotonicity condition on every coefficient sequence, and neither does the modern solution.

### Michelen and Sawhney's 2025 affirmative theorem

Marcus Michelen and Mehtaab Sawhney, *Convergent points for random power series on the unit circle*, [arXiv:2509.02729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.02729), September 2, 2025, prove two results.

**Theorem 1.1.** For any deterministic complex coefficients with $\sqrt n|a_n|\to0$, the random series converges at **some** point of the unit circle with probability one.

**Theorem 1.2.** Under the same assumption, the set of unit-circle points where the series converges has **Hausdorff dimension exactly one**, almost surely:

$$
\boxed{
\dim_{\mathrm H}
\left\{z:|z|=1,\ 
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_na_nz^n
\text{ converges}\right\}=1
\quad\text{a.s.}
}
$$

The original complete fourteen-page PDF was retrieved and its first two pages, including both numbered theorems and the proof outline, read directly. Unlike Erdős's original interesting setup, **neither theorem requires** $\sum|a_n|^2=\infty$. If the squares converge, the existence assertion is already straightforward. The substantial novelty of the theorem lies in its validity without that assumption, including the sharp nonsummable regime.

The proof uses a **multiscale branching construction**. Roughly, one retains candidate points that behave well over increasingly large blocks of coefficients, and then finds descendants at finer angular scales that also behave well. A carefully chosen sparse sequence of scales permits both enough candidate points to survive and enough control of intermediate partial sums to establish genuine **Cauchy convergence**.

The paper's Lemma 2.3 controls the final passage from good points at every scale to convergence. Lemma 2.5 compares the random sign model with Gaussian coefficients via a Lindeberg argument, enabling Gaussian correlation inequalities. These are substantive probabilistic tools. Neither a finite variance identity nor the dyadic example above replaces their proof.

### Why full dimension is compatible with measure zero

For a fixed $z$ with $|z|=1$, the independent random series converges almost surely if and only if $\sum|a_n|^2<\infty$. In the nonsummable case, the probability of convergence at that **fixed** $z$ is zero.

Fubini's theorem then implies that, almost surely, the set of convergent $z$ has **zero arc-length measure**. Michelen and Sawhney show that this exceptional set can nevertheless have Hausdorff dimension one, which is the largest possible dimension for a subset of the circle.

This contrasts three distinct assertions:

- A fixed $z$ works almost surely: **false** in the nonsummable regime.
- Almost every $z$ works for almost every sign realization: **false** in that regime.
- Some $z$ works for almost every sign realization, indeed a dimension-one set: **true** by the 2025 theorem.

That quantifier distinction is central to faithful exposition.

## What remains

An independent full proof audit would examine Michelen and Sawhney's multiscale branching argument, in particular the exact events ensuring convergence between sparse scales, the Lindeberg comparison, and the Hausdorff dimension lower bound. The 1959 Dvoretzky–Erdős original proof supplies the converse-side historical foundation.

This is a **proved** mathematical problem. The remaining work is an accurate educational explanation and reproducible elementary examples, not reopening it as an unsolved Solve campaign.
