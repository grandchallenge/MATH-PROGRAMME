## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, consider divisors just **above** its square root. Ruzsa's question, recorded by Erdős and Rosenfeld, is whether for every fixed real $\varepsilon>0$ there is a finite constant $K_\varepsilon$ so that all sufficiently large $n$ have at most $K_\varepsilon$ divisors in the **open** interval

$$
\left(\sqrt n,\ \sqrt n+n^{1/2-\varepsilon}\right).
$$

[Erdős Problem #886](https://www.erdosproblems.com/886) is still open. The bound may depend on the fixed exponent $\varepsilon$, but cannot depend on $n$. The condition applies to **all sufficiently large integers**, not only perfect squares or integers with selected factorizations.

This is a distinct question from [#887](https://www.erdosproblems.com/887), which asks for one absolute divisor-count bound valid for every fixed multiplier $C$ in the critical interval of width $C n^{1/4}$.

## A small example

For a perfect square $n=t^2$, the interval corresponding to $\varepsilon=1/4$ becomes

$$
\left(t,\ t+\sqrt t\right).
$$

In fact there is **no** divisor of $t^2$ in this interval, for any positive integer $t$. To see this, suppose $ab=t^2$ and $a<t<b$. Write $a=t-u$ and $b=t+v$ with positive integers $u,v$. Expanding the product gives

$$
t(v-u)=uv.
$$

The right-hand side is positive, so $v>u$. As $v-u\ge1$, it follows that $uv\ge t$, and therefore $v^2>uv\ge t$. Thus $v>\sqrt t$, excluding the proposed interval.

A reproducible exact search checks this for all squares $n=t^2$, $2\le t\le2000$. Enlarging the interval to width $C\sqrt t$, the same search finds at most 2 divisors for $C=2$ and at most 3 for $C=3$ within this finite range. Those observed maxima are not general bounds for arbitrary nonsquare integers.

## What is known

Erdős and Rosenfeld, [*The factor-difference set of integers*](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/acta-arithmetica/all/79/4/109554/the-factor-difference-set-of-integers), *Acta Arithmetica* **79** (1997), pp.353–359, proved that for every fixed $C>0$, all sufficiently large positive integers have at most $1+C^2$ divisors in the **closed** interval

$$
\left[\sqrt n,\ \sqrt n+C n^{1/4}\right].
$$

They also exhibited infinitely many $n$ with four divisors in the corresponding **open** interval with $C=16$. The factor-difference estimate behind the bound is in Proposition 4.1 of the original paper.

These results control intervals of width a fixed multiple of $n^{1/4}$, but do not answer #886 when $\varepsilon<1/4$, where the allowed interval grows substantially faster than $n^{1/4}$. A claim about $C n^{1/4}$ must not silently be upgraded to one about $n^{1/2-\varepsilon}$.

Tsz Ho Chan, [*Factors of a perfect square*](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa163-2-4), *Acta Arithmetica* **163** (2014), pp.141–143, proves that perfect squares have at most five divisors in a **symmetric** interval about $\sqrt n$ of width $n^{1/4}(\log n)^{1/7}$. His later 2015 work covers a further class of numbers close to squares. These are powerful special-family results, not a proof of the universal Ruzsa conjecture.

## GCL's current state

The elementary perfect-square exclusion above has a direct integer proof independent of asymptotic estimates. It is stronger for the **one-sided open** interval at $\varepsilon=1/4$ than the general $1+C^2$ statement, because perfect-square arithmetic gives the extra factor-pair identity $t(v-u)=uv$.

The source-locked finite fixture governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_885_886.py uses no floating-point comparisons. For $n=t^2$ and an integer $C\ge1$, membership of a divisor $d>t$ in the open interval of width $C n^{1/4}$ is **exactly** the integer inequality

$$
(d-t)^2<C^2t.
$$

It checks $t=2,\ldots,2000$ and $C=1,2,3$ using only integer division and squared differences. All $C=1$ counts vanish, exactly as proved. This finite fixture does not test the general fractional exponent on nonsquare $n$ and cannot resolve the quantifier over all $\varepsilon>0$.

The pinned Formal Conjectures source defines the interval with real powers and strict comparisons, and its theorem body is a placeholder; the existence of this statement is not a kernel-level solution.

## What remains

A useful bounded theorem is the **perfect-square gap lemma**: for every integer $t>0$, if $t^2=ab$ with positive integers $a<t<b$, then $b-t>\sqrt t$. Acceptance requires a complete paper proof or a pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem with literal axiom report, plus exact integer tests for all $2\le t\le2000$.

The next research obligation is to determine whether techniques beyond the $n^{1/4}$ scale yield a bound independent of $n$ for some fixed $\varepsilon<1/4$ in general nonsquare families. The existing Chan special-square theorem must be kept distinct from any such universal result. A family with unbounded counts in one fixed exponent interval would refute that exponent's universal claim; finite scans do not.
