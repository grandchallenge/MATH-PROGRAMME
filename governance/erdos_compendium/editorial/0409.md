## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 409 studies what happens when we repeatedly apply the function

$$
T(n)=\varphi(n)+1,
$$

where $\varphi(n)$ is Euler's totient function, the number of integers from 1
through $n$ that are coprime to $n$. Starting from a positive integer $n$, keep
applying $T$. If a prime is reached after $k$ applications, write $c(n)=k$.
Thus a prime has count zero, while a composite starting value may have a
positive count. The central question is how large $c(n)$ can be and what a
useful general upper bound should look like.

The source also asks two distribution questions. For a fixed prime $p$, can
infinitely many starting integers eventually land on $p$? If so, what is the
density of those starting integers? A related variant replaces $T$ by

$$
S(n)=\sigma(n)-1,
$$

where $\sigma(n)$ is the sum of the positive divisors of $n$: do these
iterations always reach a prime, and how quickly? These are related dynamical
questions, but they are not one theorem with one conclusion.

## A small example

Take $n=45$. Exact factorisation gives

$$
\varphi(45)=45\left(1-\frac13\right)\left(1-\frac15\right)=24,
$$

so the orbit is

$$
45\longmapsto25\longmapsto21\longmapsto13.
$$

The intermediate values are composite and 13 is prime, so direct arithmetic
verifies $c(45)=3$. This illustrates the definition, including the fact that
the first prime is what matters. For comparison, $\sigma(15)=1+3+5+15=24$, so
the sigma-minus-one orbit reaches the prime 23 in one step:

$$
15\longmapsto23.
$$

A bounded exact enumeration using divisor sums, totients, and trial-division
primality checks found a largest totient count of 7 among $1\le n\le1000$,
attained at $n=949$. This is a finite check, not an upper bound for all
integers, and it says nothing by itself about densities or asymptotic growth.

## What is known

The maintained problem page identifies the question as a problem of Finucane
and cites Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in
Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 81. That primary document
states the questions but does not provide a solution to them. The maintained
page also records the later interpretation that, if $F(n)$ denotes the number
of totient-plus-one iterations, the elementary facts $F(n)=o(n)$ and
$F(n)=1$ for infinitely many $n$ are not the intended endpoint; the likely
goal is a good upper bound. It points to Problem B41 in Guy's 2004 collection
for related discussion. These are source reports, not a new theorem proved by
this article.

There is one elementary termination fact for the totient map. At $n=1$ the
next value is 2. If $n>1$ is composite, then $\varphi(n)+1<n$; therefore a
non-prime orbit descends until it reaches a prime. This establishes existence
of $c(n)$ for positive $n$, but it does not determine its maximum order, the
size of a fixed-prime basin, or its density. The sigma map is different: it is
not governed by the same decreasing argument, so its termination question
requires separate evidence.

The formal quantifiers matter. The minimum-count theorem is stated for every
positive $n$; the fixed-prime question is encoded as an existential choice of
one prime whose basin is infinite; and the density statement assumes that the
relevant density already exists before asking for its value. The sigma family
uses the hypothesis $n>1$. These choices are useful interfaces for several
subquestions, but they should not be read as a single formalization of every
possible meaning of “how soon” or “density”.

## What remains

The meaningful unresolved work is to choose one precisely normalized target:
for example, a sharp bound for $c(n)$, the infinitude of the preimage of a
fixed prime, or the density of that preimage. A bounded successor should first
reproduce the primary wording, define whether iteration count starts at zero,
and separate the totient and sigma maps. It can then compute a finite table or
prove a local lemma with an explicit range and stopping rule. Such evidence
may expose a useful pattern, but it cannot establish an asymptotic, an
infinite-family statement, or sigma termination without a proof covering all
inputs.
