## The question in plain language

A binomial coefficient $\binom nk$ may have a small prime divisor. If it has **no** prime divisor at most $k$, [Erdős Problem #1093](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1093) assigns it a number called its *deficiency*.

For $n\ge2k$, examine the $k$ consecutive integers

$$
n,\ n-1,\ldots,n-k+1.
$$

The deficiency is the number among them whose prime factors are all at most $k$ (the $k$-*smooth* terms). But if any prime at most $k$ divides $\binom nk$, the deficiency is **undefined**, not zero.

The open questions ask whether infinitely many such binomial coefficients have deficiency exactly one and whether only finitely many have deficiency greater than one.

## A small example

Take $\binom73=35=5\cdot7$. Neither prime factor is at most $3$, so its deficiency is defined. Among $7,6,5$, only $6=2\cdot3$ is $3$-smooth, giving deficiency **one**.

Other exact examples show larger deficiencies: $\binom{47}{11}$ has deficiency **four** and $\binom{284}{28}$ has deficiency **nine**. These are isolated arithmetic examples, not infinite families.

## What is known

Erdős, Lacampagne and Selfridge introduced the problem in 1988. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1093) records their subsequent results, including a bound of the form $n\ll2^k\sqrt{k}$ when the deficiency is defined and positive.

It lists examples with deficiencies one, two, three, four and nine. An affirmative result for eventual absence of higher deficiencies has been discussed conditionally on other strong conjectures; such a conditional argument does **not** settle the unconditional question.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2364](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2364#issuecomment-6166419649) formalizes the undefined case and reproduces the deficiency values for all fourteen historical exceptional pairs associated with neighboring Problem #1094.

The program computes binomial coefficients as exact integers and tests smoothness by integer prime division. A finite search cannot prove either an infinite existence claim or a global finiteness claim.

## What remains

Check the 1988 and 1993 original definitions and proofs, independently validate the smoothness arithmetic using prime valuations, and clarify the relationship between positive deficiency and counterexamples to the least-prime-factor conjecture #1094.
