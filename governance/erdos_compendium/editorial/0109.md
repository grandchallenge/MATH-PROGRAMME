## The question in plain language

An infinite set $A$ of positive integers has positive **upper density** when its proportion inside some arbitrarily large initial intervals stays bounded away from zero:

$$
\overline d(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,N]|}{N}>0.
$$

The **Erdős sumset conjecture** asks whether any such $A$ contains all the pairwise sums

$$
B+C=\{b+c:b\in B,\ c\in C\}
$$

of **two infinite sets** $B,C\subseteq\mathbb N$. Both summand sets must be infinite, and their *entire* sumset must lie in $A$. Merely finding infinitely many individual representations $a=b+c$ is much weaker.

## A small example

Let $A=2\mathbb N$, the positive even integers. Taking $B=C=2\mathbb N$ gives $B+C\subseteq A$; both summand sets are infinite. This simple case benefits from exact periodic structure.

By contrast, positive density alone does not immediately supply one common pair of infinite sets: a greedy construction may make infinitely many acceptable **individual** sums while failing to control all cross-sums between selected elements. This is the obstacle the general theorem overcomes.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Joel Moreira, Florian Richter and Donald Robertson established the conjecture in [*A proof of a sumset conjecture of Erdős*](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2019.189.2.4), *Annals of Mathematics* 189 (2019), 605–652. The primary publisher abstract explicitly says that any positive-density subset of $\mathbb N$ contains $B+C$ for two infinite subsets. Their proof decomposes bounded sequences into structured and pseudorandom components in **two** different ways, combining tools from ergodic theory and additive combinatorics; their methods also extend to countable amenable groups.

A shorter proof by Bernard Host appeared subsequently and is noted on [Erdős #109](https://www.erdosproblems.com/109). The two proof routes should not be equated without auditing their different prerequisites. Neither proof is claimed here to be a direct consequence of elementary finite pigeonhole arguments.

The relevant limit is **upper** density, not a demand that the ordinary asymptotic density exist. The protected source statement must preserve this distinction.

## What remains

A bounded fixture is to prove the claim for periodic sets that contain a residue class stable under addition. For a fixed modulus $m$ and allowed residue set $R$, acceptance requires exhibiting two explicitly infinite arithmetic progressions $B,C$ with residues $b,c$ such that $b+c\bmod m$ belongs to $R$, and proving $B+C\subseteq A$. Reject examples where either progression is finite or only selected pairwise sums lie in $A$. This verifies the semantics, not the universal positive-upper-density theorem.
