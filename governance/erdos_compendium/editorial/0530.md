**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #530 is **OPEN** in its precise form: must every $N$-element set of real numbers contain a Sidon subset of size $(1+o(1))\sqrt N$? The **square-root exponent** was settled in 1975 by Komlós, Sulyok and Szemerédi. A May 2026 preprint by Bailleul and Riblet supplies the explicit uniform coefficient $1/(3\sqrt3)\approx0.19245$, but the conjectured coefficient one remains unknown.

## The question in plain language

Take an arbitrary finite set $A$ of $N$ **distinct real numbers**. We want to keep as many of its elements as possible while ensuring that no two *different unordered pairs* have the same sum.

Such a subset $S\subseteq A$ is a **Sidon set**, or a $B_2$ set. In precise form,

$$
a+b=c+d,\qquad a,b,c,d\in S
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\{a,b\}=\{c,d\}
$$

where braces mean **multisets**: repeating an element in a pair is allowed. For instance, $a+a=b+c$ is a forbidden relation when $a,b,c$ are distinct.

Let $s(A)$ be the maximum size of a Sidon subset of $A$. The quantity Erdős considers is

$$
\ell(N)=\min_{\substack{A\subset\mathbb R\\|A|=N}}s(A).
$$

Equivalently, $\ell(N)$ is the **largest integer guaranteed** for *every* set of $N$ real numbers. This worst-case quantifier is central. Asking for a large Sidon subset of the particular interval $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ is an easier, different problem.

Erdős asks whether

$$
\boxed{\ell(N)\sim\sqrt N,}
$$

meaning $\ell(N)/\sqrt N\to1$. We know that $\ell(N)$ has order $\sqrt N$, but not whether the coefficient one is universally achievable.

## A small example

The set $\{1,2,3\}$ is **not** Sidon, since $1+3=2+2$. However $\{1,2\}$ is Sidon: its three pair sums $2,3,4$ are distinct. Thus its largest Sidon subset has size two.

Now consider $S=\{1,2,4\}$. Its six unordered pair sums, including repeated elements, are

$$
2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8.
$$

They are all different, so $S$ is a Sidon set of size three. Notice that replacing the condition by one requiring **four distinct** indices would mistakenly accept $\{1,2,3\}$, because $1+3=2+2$ involves a repeated index.

There is an equivalent **difference criterion**. A finite set $S$ is Sidon if and only if all its *positive ordered differences* $b-a$ with $a<b$, $a,b\in S$, are distinct. Indeed,

$$
b-a=d-c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b+c=a+d.
$$

An equality of two different differences gives a nontrivial equality of pair sums, and conversely. With $m=|S|$, there are $m(m-1)/2$ such differences. This gives an efficient exact finite test.

Sidon status is invariant under any affine change of coordinates $a\mapsto ua+v$ with $u\ne0$, because $ua+v+ub+v=uc+v+ud+v$ exactly when $a+b=c+d$. This is why arithmetic progressions have the same extremal Sidon size as ordinary integer intervals.

## What is known

### Erdős's original question and the interval obstruction

The primary source is Erdős, *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, in *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), Chapter 12, [printed p.120](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=4). The **original scanned page was visually inspected**. Erdős attributes the question to J. Riddell (1969), permits **arbitrary real numbers**, and includes equal-index pairs when describing the Sidon condition.

At the time, the known inequalities were

$$
cN^{1/3}\le\ell(N)\le(1+o(1))N^{1/2}
$$

for a positive constant $c$. The upper bound follows by choosing the worst-case candidate $A=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. Classical results of Erdős, Turán, Chowla and Singer show that the largest Sidon subset of this interval has size $(1+o(1))\sqrt N$.

A simple but instructive proof gives the historical **cube-root** lower bound. Let $S\subseteq A$ be a maximal-by-inclusion Sidon set, with $m=|S|$. Every element $x\in A\setminus S$ would destroy the Sidon property if added. Examining a new nontrivial equality of two pair sums shows that either

$$
x=a+b-c\qquad\text{or}\qquad
x=\frac{a+b}{2}
$$

for suitable $a,b,c\in S$. Hence

$$
A\setminus S\ \subseteq\
(S+S-S)\ \cup\ \frac{S+S}{2}.
$$

Counting all possible ordered expressions, with some overcounting, gives

$$
N\le m+m^3+m^2.
$$

Therefore every sufficiently large $N$-element set contains a Sidon subset with $m\gg N^{1/3}$. The argument is elementary but cannot alone attain the correct square-root exponent.

### Komlós, Sulyok and Szemerédi proved the order

János Komlós, Miklós Sulyok and Endre Szemerédi, *Linear problems in combinatorial number theory*, *Acta Mathematica Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae* **26** (1975), 113–121, [DOI 10.1007/BF01895954](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01895954), proved a general comparison theorem for subsets avoiding fixed linear additive relations.

Specialized to the Sidon relation $a+b=c+d$, this gives

$$
\boxed{\ell(N)\ge c_0\sqrt N}
$$

for some universal $c_0>0$ and sufficiently large $N$. Together with the interval construction,

$$
\boxed{\ell(N)=\Theta(\sqrt N).}
$$

This settles the **exponent**, but not the conjectured *asymptotic constant one*. The original 1975 bibliographic record and theorem characterization were checked; the full original multi-lemma proof was not independently reconstructed.

The 1975 theorem is often phrased for integer sets. For a finite collection of reals, there is a standard finite additive-model reduction: the elements lie in a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb Q$. A generic rational linear functional separates the finitely many nonzero pair-sum differences. After clearing denominators, it maps the set into integers while preserving every equality and inequality of pair sums. This is enough to transfer a Sidon-subset guarantee **back** to the original real set.

### A 2026 explicit improvement

Alexandre Bailleul and Robin Riblet, *On the largest Sidon subset in a finite subset of* $\mathbb R^N$, [arXiv:2605.03181](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03181), submitted May 4, 2026, present a stronger numerical lower bound:

$$
\boxed{
\ell(N)\ge
\left(\frac{1}{3\sqrt3}+o(1)\right)\sqrt N.
}
$$

The coefficient is approximately $0.19245$. The authors prove the same estimate for arbitrary finite subsets of real vector spaces, **uniformly in the ambient dimension**. The **complete original eleven-page PDF** was retrieved and its abstract, introduction and stated constants checked directly.

Their method first compresses a general finite integer set by a **Freiman $2$-morphism** that preserves exactly the equalities relevant to Sidon subsets. It then uses Singer's constructions and a covering of a suitable cyclic group by Sidon sets. A projection and approximation argument handles real vector spaces. This refines earlier quantitative bounds attributed to Abbott.

The preprint improves the guaranteed constant but does **not** establish the limiting ratio one. Its complete proof has not been independently reconstructed by GCL, and its current status as a 2026 preprint is kept distinct from the published 1975 theorem.

Noga Alon and Paul Erdős, *An application of graph theory to additive number theory*, *European Journal of Combinatorics* **6** (1985), 201–203, also proposed that any $N$-point set might be partitionable into at most $(1+o(1))\sqrt N$ Sidon sets. This is a **stronger conjecture** than obtaining a single guaranteed large Sidon subset. Neither conjecture follows from the known order estimate.

The exact finite fixture checks Sidon pair-sum and positive-difference predicates, affine invariance, maximal-set obstruction and the largest Sidon subsets of small integer intervals. None of those tests proves the universal asymptotic constant.

There is no #530 statement in the admitted protected Formal Conjectures snapshot, and no GCL Lean proof is asserted.

## What remains

The research obligation is to improve the universal coefficient from $1/(3\sqrt3)$ toward one, or to construct sets of real numbers disproving $\ell(N)\sim\sqrt N$. A sharper statement about covering arbitrary sets by few Sidon subsets would be a different, stronger advance.

The bounded task is to validate small exact examples and the cube-root argument, which is useful for exposition and tests but not a substitute for Komlós–Sulyok–Szemerédi's or Bailleul–Riblet's methods.
