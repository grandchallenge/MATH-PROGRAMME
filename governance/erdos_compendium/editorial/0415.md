## The question in plain language

Let $\phi(n)$ be Euler's totient: the number of integers from $1$ through $n$ that are coprime to $n$. Erdős Problem 415 studies the order in which consecutive values $\phi(m+1),\ldots,\phi(m+k)$ can appear. For a strict interpretation, all values in the block must be different, and its order is one of the $k!$ permutations of $1,\ldots,k$. Define $F_{\mathrm{str}}(x)$ to be the largest $k$ for which every such permutation occurs in at least one block ending at or before $x$.

The maintained problem page asks three related questions. Is $F(x)$ asymptotic to a constant times $\log\log\log x$? Is the decreasing pattern the first one to disappear? Is the pattern suggested by $\phi(1),\ldots,\phi(k)$ the most likely? There is an essential ambiguity: “ordering pattern” might mean strict inequalities, or a weak order in which equal values are allowed. The first interpretation is a permutation question. The third cannot be purely a permutation question, since $\phi(1)=\phi(2)=1$ and $\phi(3)=\phi(4)=2$.

The source record therefore contains two problems joined together: a strict-order threshold and an equality-sensitive weak-order problem. They should not be silently treated as the same statement.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check using only integer arithmetic. Compute $\phi(t)$ from its definition $\phi(t)=\#\{a:1\leq a\leq t,\ \gcd(a,t)=1\}$, enumerate every starting point $m$ with $m+3\leq x$, discard blocks with ties, and record the rank pattern. The first cutoff found for which all six strict patterns of length three occur is $x=315$:

| pattern of ranks | $m$ | $(\phi(m+1),\phi(m+2),\phi(m+3))$ |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| $(1,2,3)$ | 104 | $(48,52,106)$ |
| $(1,3,2)$ | 5 | $(2,6,4)$ |
| $(2,1,3)$ | 4 | $(4,2,6)$ |
| $(2,3,1)$ | 15 | $(8,16,6)$ |
| $(3,1,2)$ | 12 | $(12,6,8)$ |
| $(3,2,1)$ | 312 | $(312,156,144)$ |

For example, $\phi(313)=312$, $\phi(314)=156$, and $\phi(315)=144$, so the last row is genuinely decreasing. This is a reproducible certificate for a finite, length-three assertion; it does not prove an asymptotic formula, prove that the next missing pattern is decreasing at every cutoff, or say anything about weak orders with ties.

## What is known

The relevant primary source is Pollack, Pomerance, and Treviño, “Sets of monotonicity for Euler’s totient function,” Ramanujan Journal 30 (2013), 379–398. Their Theorem 1.5, on page 3 of the author PDF, states that the largest consecutive block in $[1,x]$ on which $\phi$ is nonincreasing has size $\frac{\log_3 x}{\log_6 x}+(\alpha-\gamma+o(1))\frac{\log_3 x}{(\log_6 x)^2}$, and the same holds for nondecreasing blocks. Here $\log_j$ denotes the j-fold iterated natural logarithm, $\gamma$ is Euler's constant, and $\alpha$ is defined by $\exp(\alpha)=\prod_p(1-1/p)^{-1/p}$.

The paper's remark after the proof, on page 12, says its lower-bound construction also works for strictly decreasing and strictly increasing blocks. Thus, for the strict interpretation, the longest increasing and decreasing blocks have this same asymptotic scale. Since every universal collection of strict permutations must include both monotone permutations, $F_{\mathrm{str}}(x)$ is at most that quantity. In particular it cannot be asymptotic to a positive constant times $\log_3 x$; the extra divisor $\log_6 x$ matters.

That primary theorem does not establish that every strict permutation occurs up to the same threshold. A linked 2026 circulation sketch claims such a result by reindexing the paper's construction, and claims that every fixed strict permutation occurs infinitely often. This is an external, unreviewed report rather than a GCL-certified theorem; its status is recorded as a claim requiring source and semantic review. It also deliberately leaves the equality-allowing variant separate. The maintained page reports these facts and was last edited 28 May 2026, but a maintained webpage is not a substitute for checking the proof.
