## The question in plain language

A *sunflower* is a collection of sets whose pairwise intersections are all the same common set, called its *core*. For example, three different sets might all contain one central element and otherwise be disjoint.

[Erdős Problem #20](https://www.erdosproblems.com/20) is the famous **sunflower conjecture**. Fix a number of petals $k$. Is there a constant $c_k$ depending on $k$ but **not on the set size** $n$ such that every sufficiently large family of sets each of size $n$ must contain a sunflower with $k$ petals once it has roughly $c_k^n$ members? The fixed-petal-count conjecture remains open.

## A small example

The three sets

$$
\{1,2\},\qquad\{1,3\},\qquad\{1,4\}
$$

form a sunflower whose common core is $\{1\}$. By contrast, three sets $\{1,2\}$, $\{2,3\}$ and $\{1,3\}$ have three **different** pairwise intersections and hence do not form a sunflower.

For two petals every pair of different sets trivially forms a sunflower. The difficult part begins at fixed $k\ge3$ and growing set size $n$.

## What is known

The classical Erdős–Rado sunflower lemma gave an upper threshold comparable to $n^n$ (with constants depending on $k$). Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang, *Improved bounds for the sunflower lemma*, *Annals of Mathematics* **194** (2021), 795–815, improved this dramatically to a threshold of order $(\log n)^n$ for fixed $k$, up to appropriate fixed-parameter factors.

But $(\log n)^n$ cannot be bounded by $c_k^n$ using one fixed $c_k$ for all large $n$. Thus the celebrated improvement is not the conjectured exponential bound.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1289](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1289#issuecomment-6121165642) reads the frozen formal definition of the sunflower threshold, identifies the quantifier requiring $c_k$ independent of $n$, and checks trivial one- and two-petal examples. Both the main conjecture and the source's factorial-bound variant have unfilled proof bodies; a verified trivial test case is not a global solution.

## What remains

Prove a fixed-base exponential bound for each fixed petal count, or produce a family showing that no such bound can hold. The main obstacle is eliminating the growing $\log n$ factor from the base of the best published general estimates.
