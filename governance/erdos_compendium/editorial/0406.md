## The question in plain language

Write a positive integer in base $3$, so that its digits are $0$, $1$, or $2$. Problem 406 asks whether only finitely many powers $2^n$ avoid the digit $2$ altogether. Equivalently, is the set

$$
\{n\in\mathbb N: \text{every ternary digit of }2^n\text{ is }0\text{ or }1\}
$$

finite? The wording concerns the exponents, not merely the numerical size of the powers. The first examples are $2^0=1$, $2^2=4=(11)_3$, and $2^8=256=(100111)_3$. The conjectural stronger statement is that these are the only examples, but finiteness alone would still allow additional examples.

This is Erdős’s question about ternary expansions of powers of two. It is a digit-restriction problem with an elementary statement but difficult global behavior: increasing $n$ makes the number of ternary digits grow, while the digits are constrained by the multiplicative sequence $2^n$.

## A small example

The third listed example can be checked without approximation:

$$
1+3+3^2+3^5=1+3+9+243=256=2^8,
$$

so the ternary expansion is $(100111)_3$. I also ran this exact-integer check for every $n$ from $0$ through $16$:

```python
def ternary(n):
    digits = []
    while n:
        n, r = divmod(n, 3)
        digits.append(str(r))
    return ''.join(reversed(digits)) or '0'

good = [n for n in range(17)
        if set(ternary(2**n)) <= {'0', '1'}]
print(good)
```

The output was `[0, 2, 8]`; the same run printed $2^8=256$, `100111`, and verified the displayed sum. This is a reproducible finite calculation, not an argument about all exponents: it checks only the explicitly bounded range $0\leq n\leq16$ and cannot exclude a later example.

There is a related variant in which the allowed digits are $1$ and $2$. The exact check gives exponents $0,1,2,3,4,15$ through $16$, matching the reported candidate $2^{15}$ as the largest such power in the small range. The variant must not be confused with Problem 406, whose allowed digits are $0$ and $1$.

## What is known

The maintained Erdős Problems entry records the question as open. It reports that Gupta’s earlier search found no further $0$–$1$ ternary powers through exponent $4373$, and that later computation extended the range. Robert Saye’s primary paper, *On two conjectures concerning the ternary digits of powers of two* (Journal of Integer Sequences 25 (2022), Article 22.3.4, pp. 1–9), gives the clearest recent finite result. Its Lemma 1 describes the period $u_k=2\cdot3^{k-1}$ of powers of $2$ modulo $3^k$ and a recursion that controls the next ternary digit. Section 4 reports a computation testing the Erdős and Sloane digit conjectures for every exponent through

$$
2\cdot3^{45}=5908625413101667397286.
$$

No counterexample was found in that finite range. Saye’s result is computational evidence, not a proof that the set of exceptional exponents is finite.

There is also a genuine analytic counting result. Narkiewicz proved the bound $N(x)\leq1.62x^{\log_3 2}$ for the number $N(x)$ of exponents $n\leq x$ whose ternary expansion of $2^n$ omits $2$. Jeffrey Lagarias’s primary paper *Ternary expansions of powers of 2* (Journal of the London Mathematical Society 79 (2009), 562–588) states this history and proves a broader 3-adic analogue in Theorem 1.4. A sublinear upper bound still permits infinitely many such exponents, so it does not settle Problem 406.

## What remains

The central unresolved task is a proof or disproof of finiteness, not merely a larger search. A bounded successor could take an exact exponent ceiling $B$, reproduce Saye’s residue recursion modulo the required powers of $3$, and emit the complete list of candidate exponents up to $B$ together with independently checkable integer identities. Acceptance would require exact arithmetic, a stated convention for $n=0$, and a certificate that every recursion branch covering $0\leq n\leq B$ was visited. A found exponent outside $0,2,8$ would falsify the “only three examples” strengthening, but would not by itself disprove finiteness. Failure to find one would establish only the bounded claim.

Before any formal proof work, the natural prerequisite is a line-by-line semantic comparison of $Nat.digits 3 n \subseteq [0, 1]$ with the source definition of a base-three expansion, including the treatment of zero and the exponent convention. No Lean replay or mathematical certification is supplied here.
