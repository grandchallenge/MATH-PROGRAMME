**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #546 is **PROVED**. Erdős asked whether the two-color Ramsey number of *any* graph with $m$ edges and no isolated vertices is bounded by an exponential of order $\sqrt m$. Benny Sudakov proved the conjecture in 2011, with the explicit bound $R(G)\le2^{250\sqrt m}$. The substantially stronger comparison problem #545 remains open.

## The question in plain language

For a finite simple graph $G$, its two-color Ramsey number $R(G)$ is the smallest positive integer $N$ such that every red-blue coloring of the edges of the complete graph $K_N$ contains a **monochromatic copy** of $G$. This is ordinary, **not necessarily induced**, subgraph containment; the other edges among selected vertices may have either color.

Erdős asked whether there is an **absolute constant** $c>0$ such that for **every** graph $G$ with exactly $m\ge1$ edges and **no isolated vertices**,

$$
\boxed{R(G)\le 2^{c\sqrt m}.}
$$

The constant is independent of $G$, its number of vertices and its particular shape. The answer is **yes**.

The exclusion of isolated vertices is essential, not decorative. If a graph has one edge and $q$ additional isolated vertices, it still has $m=1$ but any monochromatic copy needs at least $q+2$ vertices. No constant bound depending only on $m$ could cover all such graphs.

By contrast, if every vertex of $G$ has degree at least one, the handshaking identity gives

$$
|V(G)|\le \sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)=2m.
$$

Thus the number of vertices is itself controlled by $m$. The conjecture asserts a **much stronger** control on the Ramsey number than merely bounding the vertices of $G$.

## A small example

The path $P_3$ has two edges and three vertices, and

$$
R(P_3)=3.
$$

Every red-blue coloring of the three edges of a triangle has two edges of the same color, which form a monochromatic $P_3$. The graph $2K_2$, two disjoint edges, also has $m=2$, but

$$
R(2K_2)=5.
$$

The latter exact value is proved in the adjacent #545 article: $K_4$ has a red-star/blue-triangle coloring with no monochromatic two-edge matching, while every coloring of $K_5$ contains one.

For the three-edge triangle $K_3$, the familiar classical result is $R(K_3)=6$. These small cases demonstrate that the Ramsey number depends on more than the **number of edges**, even though Sudakov's theorem gives a common upper bound depending on $m$ alone.

The proved general numerical bound is deliberately enormous in these examples. For $m=2$, it permits

$$
R(G)\le 2^{250\sqrt2},
$$

far above the exact values $3$ and $5$. Its achievement is the **uniform exponential order** across arbitrarily complicated graphs, not a sharp estimate for every small graph.

## What is known

### The original conjecture and its resolution

Erdős formulated the problem in *On some problems in graph theory, combinatorial analysis and combinatorial number theory*, *Graph Theory and Combinatorics* (Cambridge 1983; published 1984), p.10. The complete original Erdős text was not obtained in this tranche; the bibliographic locator is preserved as attributed by subsequent primary papers.

Benny Sudakov, *A conjecture of Erdős on graph Ramsey numbers*, *Advances in Mathematics* **227** (2011), 601–609, [DOI 10.1016/j.aim.2011.02.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aim.2011.02.004), resolved it. The complete [author-hosted published PDF](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~sudakovb/erdos-ramsey-problem.pdf) was retrieved and inspected. **Theorem 1.1, printed p.602**, explicitly states

$$
\boxed{R(G)\le 2^{250\sqrt m}}
$$

for **every** graph with $m$ edges and no isolated vertices. The proof appears in Section 3, printed pp.605–608. This mathematical status is PROVED, not merely a conjectural bound.

The numerical constant $250$ was not optimized. The strength lies in replacing the extra logarithmic factor that earlier general estimates required.

### What was known before 2011

For the complete graph $K_t$,

$$
m=\binom t2\asymp t^2.
$$

The classical Erdős–Szekeres upper bound gives $R(K_t)\le2^{2t}$, while Erdős's probabilistic lower bound gives an exponential lower bound of order $2^{t/2}$. Consequently, graphs with $m$ edges can genuinely have Ramsey number

$$
R(G)=2^{\Theta(\sqrt m)}
$$

for an appropriate family of complete graphs. Thus the exponent $\sqrt m$ is **best possible up to an absolute constant**.

Earlier work by Noga Alon, Michael Krivelevich and Benny Sudakov in 2003 established a general bound of the larger scale

$$
R(G)\le2^{O(\sqrt m\log m)}
$$

and obtained the conjectured smaller exponential scale for **bipartite** graphs. Sudakov's 2011 theorem supplied the missing general step, including graphs with many triangles or other odd cycles.

The 2011 argument develops tools for monochromatic pairs and structured subsets and assembles them through an iterative density argument. The original proof is accessible in full, but this editorial pass **does not claim an independent line-by-line mathematical reconstruction** or a formal proof in Lean.

### Why this does not solve the sharper problem #545

In #545, with $m=\binom n2+t$, Erdős and Graham ask whether a specific **concentrated graph** $H_{n,t}$ has maximal Ramsey number among all $m$-edge graphs without isolates.

Sudakov's theorem bounds all such Ramsey numbers by a shared value $2^{250\sqrt m}$. It does not show

$$
R(G)\le R(H_{n,t}),
$$

because the right-hand side is generally much smaller than Sudakov's broad upper bound. Sudakov's own introduction explicitly presents the Erdős–Graham extremal question as separate and much harder.

Likewise, the analogous uniform **three-or-more-color** Ramsey upper bound with exponent of order $\sqrt m$ was not established by this two-color theorem. The [maintained #546 problem page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/546), last edited November 18, 2025, identifies that multicolor question as still open.

The associated finite regression verifies exact small Ramsey numbers by exhaustive two-color enumeration and two independent graph-embedding checks. It tests the handshaking bound and gives isolate-based counterexamples when the hypothesis is dropped. These are **bounded semantic checks**, not proofs of the universal $2^{250\sqrt m}$ theorem.

## What remains

No mathematical work is required to resolve the original yes/no conjecture: Sudakov proved it. Remaining provenance work could include a detailed independent reconstruction of the 2011 argument or faithful formalization of Theorem 1.1. The distinct multicolor question and the sharper #545 comparison are genuinely separate research obligations.
