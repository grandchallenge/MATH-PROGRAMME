## The question in plain language

Let $S_1,S_2,\ldots$ be a countable sequence of subsets of the complex plane, each **without a finite accumulation point**. Erdős asked whether there is a **transcendental entire function** $f$ and a corresponding sequence of nonnegative integers $k_1,k_2,\ldots$ such that

$$
f^{(k_j)}(z)=0\quad\text{for every }z\in S_j.
$$

The derivative order $k_j$ may depend on the **set index** $j$ but must be common to **all points of $S_j$**. Different sets may overlap, and each set may be infinite.

## A small example

If $S_1=\{0,1\}$ and $S_2=\{2\}$, then a polynomial such as $z^2(z-1)^2$ has both $f(0)=f(1)=0$ and a sufficiently high derivative identically zero, but it is **not transcendental**. Finite interpolation by polynomials therefore misses the most important requirement.

A set such as $S=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$ has no finite accumulation point, although it is infinite. A set accumulating at zero, for example $\{1,1/2,1/3,\ldots\}$, violates the source hypothesis and cannot be inserted into the theorem unchanged.

## What is known

**PROVED.** K. F. Barth and W. J. Schneider, [*On a Problem of Erdős Concerning the Zeros of the Derivatives of an Entire Function*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1972-0285780-7), *Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society* 32 (1972), 229–232, proved exactly the simultaneous derivative-zero theorem. Their original abstract states that, for an arbitrary sequence of sets having no finite limit point, one can construct a transcendental entire function whose designated derivatives vanish on those sets.

The proof uses repeated polynomial interpolation and approximation on expanding discs. Local finiteness of every $S_j$ makes it possible to impose the relevant conditions on a finite region during each stage, while keeping the infinite process convergent.

This is not a theorem that **one fixed derivative** must vanish on the union of all sets. It is also not an unconstrained interpolation theorem on sets with accumulation points: the identity theorem makes those substantially different.

## What remains

A bounded task creates examples of finite and infinite locally finite sets, tests detection of finite accumulation points, and constructs polynomials satisfying finite derivative interpolation constraints with exact rational coefficients. Acceptance: all points in each $S_j$ use a single $k_j$, a set accumulating at zero is rejected, and finite polynomial fixtures are never presented as a transcendental entire-function construction.
