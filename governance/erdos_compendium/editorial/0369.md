## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 369 concerns strings of consecutive integers whose prime factors are small. If $P^+(m)$ denotes the largest prime factor of $m$ (with the harmless convention $P^+(1)=1$), the literal question recorded by the catalogue is: for every real ε > 0 and integer k ≥ 2, does every sufficiently large integer n contain k consecutive integers in {1, …, n} for which $P^+(m) ≤ n^ε$?

The wording matters. It does not require the block to lie near n, and it measures smoothness against n, not necessarily against each member m. For fixed k, the first k positive integers already form such a block once n is large enough. Thus the literal statement is elementary. The serious nearby question is whether the block can be forced into a region such as [n/2, n].

This distinction is present in the live Erdős Problems entry and in the formal source discussed below. It is not a cosmetic reformulation: a theorem about every large n near n is substantially stronger than the literal initial-block observation.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check of the stronger near N shape, included only as an illustration. Take N = 64, k = 3, and ε = 1/2. The consecutive block 48, 49, 50 lies in [32, 64], and

$$
48 = 2⁴ · 3, 49 = 7², 50 = 2 · 5².
$$

Therefore the largest prime factors are 3, 7, and 5. Their squares are 9, 49, and 25, each at most 64, so each largest prime factor is at most √64 = 8. This verifies the finite instance $P^+(m) ≤ m^{1/2} ≤ 8$ for these three values by exact integer arithmetic.

The check does not establish an asymptotic theorem, does not identify a threshold for all N, and does not validate any Lean implementation. It simply makes the two notions—consecutive and smooth—concrete, while showing that the near N variant is not vacuous at small scale.

Those are catalogue facts, not by themselves a proof or a GCL decision.

The primary published result most directly related to the older literature is Antal Balog and Trevor D. Wooley, “On strings of consecutive integers with no large prime factors,” *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society (Series A)* 64 (1998), 266–276, DOI [10.1017/S1446788700001750](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788700001750). Its Theorem 1, p. 267, gives infinitely many long strings of consecutive integers with no prime factor above a small power of the scale. This is an “infinitely many” result, not automatically a block in [N/2, N] for every sufficiently large N.

The accessible primary manuscript for the stronger every-large-N interpretation is Yueer Yang, “Proof of Erdős Problem #369: Consecutive Smooth Integers Near N,” Zenodo record [19361034](https://zenodo.org/records/19361034), DOI [10.5281/zenodo.19361034](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19361034), created 31 March 2026. The deposited TeX file has Zenodo MD5 `1af08fc9065365a70cc405287e7a9b5f` and SHA-256 `00a1aedb6d5188bf33ae6a162ecac9feb9bcbf6983bc988b7354bcd986d5ebfa`. Its Theorem 1 asserts that, for every ε > 0 and k ≥ 2, all sufficiently large N have a block of k consecutive integers in [N/2, N] with $P^+(m) ≤ m^ε$. The written proof uses small Euler-product ratios, the Chinese remainder theorem, cyclotomic factorization, and a covering argument from powers of 2 and 3. This manuscript is a source of an external mathematical claim; it is not a GCL certification.

The live entry at [erdosproblems.com/369](https://www.erdosproblems.com/369), accessed during this audit, labels the problem “PROVED (LEAN)” but also explains that the literal formulation is trivial and that the near N readings are variants. Its discussion cites Balog–Wooley and the Yang construction. The page reports a last edit of 28 April 2026, so its status text is later than the frozen catalogue packet and must be treated as a separate dated revision.
