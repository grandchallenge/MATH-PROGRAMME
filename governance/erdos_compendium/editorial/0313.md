## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 313 asks whether a particular kind of Egyptian fraction can be built in infinitely many ways. Choose distinct primes $p_1<\cdots<p_k$ and an integer $m\geq 2$. The question is whether there are infinitely many choices for which

$$
\frac{1}{p_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{p_k}=1-\frac{1}{m}.
$$

The original source is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 40, where this is recorded as an open problem. The formulation matters: the primes must be distinct, $m$ is an integer, and the question concerns infinitely many solutions, not merely finding another example.

There is a useful normalization. If $P=p_1\cdots p_k$, clearing denominators shows that every $p_i$ divides $m$; writing $m=Pt$ then forces $t=1$. Thus every solution has $m=P$. Equivalently, one asks for infinitely many squarefree integers $m$ satisfying

$$
\frac{1}{m}+\sum_{p\mid m}\frac{1}{p}=1.
$$

These $m$ are called primary pseudoperfect numbers.

## A small example

Take the primes $2$, $3$, and $7$. Exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{7}=\frac{21+14+6}{42}=\frac{41}{42}=1-\frac{1}{42}.
$$

So $m=42=2\cdot3\cdot7$ is one solution. The same calculation verifies the first terms $m=6$ and $m=1806$, using $(2,3)$ and $(2,3,7,43)$ respectively; the denominators are products of the displayed distinct primes. This is a reproducible finite check, not evidence of infinitude. A pattern that works for several steps can stop when the next candidate is composite, and a finite list cannot settle the unbounded question.

The normalization also explains why the two languages are equivalent. Multiplying the reciprocal identity by $m$ gives $1+\sum_{p\mid m}m/p=m$, which is exactly the primary-pseudoperfect equation. Conversely, a squarefree $m$ satisfying that equation supplies the prime set of an Erdős solution.

This reduction is elementary but important for auditing claims. A statement about infinitely many admissible prime sets is the same statement as one about infinitely many admissible squarefree products; it is not a change to a weaker counting problem. It also explains why primality of every displayed factor must be checked before a numerical candidate is called a solution.

The relevant primary source is the Erdős–Graham volume cited above, linked through the stable PDF copy [here](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). Later literature gives finite progress rather than a resolution. Butske, Jaje, and Mayernik's paper [“On the equation ...”](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-99-01088-1) is the standard computational source for the first layers; the 2026 preprint [*Port Fillings for Primary Pseudoperfect Numbers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21518), version 1 dated 18 May 2026, reports two further examples, including $5998279018951962402$ and its inherited successor $35979351189199316534587473905773572006$. Its stated unconditional results are finite constructions and a port formalism. Its infinitude statement is conditional on a multivariable prime-points hypothesis, not a proof of Problem 313.

The live Erdős Problems page has three public comments. One says that the chain $6,42,1806$ continues infinitely, but another comment records that the next suggested term fails because $1807=13\cdot139$ is not prime. The third points to the 2026 preprint and explicitly calls it partial. These comments are useful leads, not verified mathematical authority; the page itself says comments are not checked for correctness.

## What remains

The central unresolved task is an unconditional proof or disproof that the set of primary pseudoperfect numbers is infinite. The current preprint offers a conditional recursive route and new finite examples, but neither closes that gap. A safe next investigation is a bounded audit of one fixed successor layer: take the exact key port and identities from the preprint, verify its displayed factorizations and primality certificates with independently reproducible integer arithmetic, and enumerate only the stated finite parameter interval. The acceptance test should be exact equality plus primality and squarefreeness checks; a failed equality, composite candidate, or incomplete certificate is a falsification of that finite candidate, not of the original conjecture. Stop when the prescribed interval is exhausted or a source identity cannot be reproduced.
