## The question in plain language

An interval of integers $[a,b]$ means the consecutive set

$$
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\},
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers with $a\le b$. Its reciprocal weight is

$$
W([a,b])=\sum_{n=a}^{b}\frac{1}{n},
$$

and its length is $b-a+1$. Erdős Problem #289 asks whether, once $k$ is large enough, one can choose exactly $k$ such blocks so that every block has length at least two, different blocks do not overlap, at least one integer is left unused between consecutive blocks, and the total reciprocal weight is exactly $1$.

In symbols, the maintained modern formulation asks whether there is a threshold $K$ such that for every integer $k\ge K$ there are intervals $I_1,\ldots,I_k\subset\mathbb N$ satisfying

$$
\lvert I_i\rvert\ge 2,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k}W(I_i)=1,
$$

with the blocks pairwise non-overlapping and non-adjacent. “For every $k\ge K$” is an eventual statement: it is stronger than finding examples for infinitely many values of $k$.

There is an important historical qualification. On printed p. 34 of *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, Erdős and Graham define the notation $\sum_{a,b}$ for the reciprocal sum from $a$ through $b$, and say it is “probably true” that, for sufficiently large $k$, $1$ can be written as a sum of $k$ such block-sums with $b_i>a_i$. The printed sentence does not itself say that the blocks must be distinct, disjoint, or separated by an unused integer. The stronger wording is the maintained catalogue’s natural restriction, not something that can silently be attributed to every word of the 1980 page.

## A small example

The source gives a useful finite example for $2$, rather than for the conjectured target $1$:

$$
[2,7],\quad [9,10],\quad [17,18],\quad [34,35],\quad [84,85].
$$

Each block has at least two integers, and there is a gap between every pair. Exact rational arithmetic gives

$$
W([2,7])=\frac{223}{140},\quad W([9,10])=\frac{19}{90},\quad W([17,18])=\frac{35}{306},
$$

$$
W([34,35])=\frac{69}{1190},\quad W([84,85])=\frac{169}{7140},
$$

and

$$
\frac{223}{140}+\frac{19}{90}+\frac{35}{306}+\frac{69}{1190}+\frac{169}{7140}=2.
$$

I independently checked these fractions with Python’s exact `Fraction` arithmetic, including the lengths $6,2,2,2,2$ and the strict separation condition $b_i+1<a_{i+1}$. This verifies one finite identity and the intended geometry of separated blocks. It does not represent $1$, does not cover every large $k$, and gives no asymptotic proof. The example is attributed by the source to the solution of Monthly problem E2689, proposed by Hahn and associated with Montgomery’s 1979 publication; that attribution is not evidence that #289 itself is solved.

## What is known

The directly inspected primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed p. 34. Its wording is conjectural and less restrictive than the maintained formulation. On the same page it points to Hahn 1978 in connection with the proposed representation and to Montgomery 1979 for a representation of $2$.

The cited Montgomery item is P. L. Montgomery, “E2689,” *The American Mathematical Monthly* 86 (1979), no. 3, p. 224, DOI [10.2307/2321534](https://doi.org/10.2307/2321534). The DOI metadata confirms the author, volume, year, page, and DOI. The source audit did not obtain a direct page image, so the precise attribution of the displayed five-block identity is retained as source-reported rather than presented as a fresh page-level transcription.

The live Erdős Problems entry currently displays the strengthened interval formulation as open and lists three unreviewed proof claims. Those are useful leads, not independent verification. One associated finite verifier reportedly passes arithmetic checks, but finite checks do not establish the eventual assertion for every sufficiently large $k$. A repository or author manuscript can be evidence to inspect; it is not, by itself, a theorem certified by GCL.

## What remains

First, a source adjudication must decide how the printed p. 34 formulation relates to the strengthened modern one. In particular, it must not silently turn an omitted condition into an original quotation. Second, the formal proposition must be compared line by line with the intended quantifiers: exactly $k$ blocks, eventuality in $k$, length at least two, exact reciprocal sum $1$, non-overlap, non-adjacency, and positive endpoints.

Third, the external Lean artifact needs a clean replay at its exact source commit, followed by a dependency and axiom report. A successful compilation would still require checking that the theorem being compiled is the intended theorem and that no hidden placeholder or weaker predicate supplies the result. The positive-endpoint issue is especially concrete: if endpoints range over $\mathbb N$ with $0$ allowed, then the reciprocal convention at $0$ must not quietly change the mathematical problem.

The bounded stopping condition is therefore clear: preserve **STATUS_CONFLICT** until the primary-source comparison, exact replay, dependency review, and endpoint bridge are all documented. The finite identity for $2$ should remain a checked example, not be promoted into evidence for a claim covering every sufficiently large $k$.
