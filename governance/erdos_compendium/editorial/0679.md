## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $r$, let $\omega(r)$ count its **distinct prime factors**. Erdős Problem [#679](https://www.erdosproblems.com/679) asks whether, for each $\varepsilon>0$, infinitely many choices of $n$ make $\omega(n-k)$ uniformly small for every sufficiently large $k<n$: $\omega(n-k)<(1+\varepsilon)\log k/\log\log k$. The cutoff for 'sufficiently large' depends on $\varepsilon$, not separately on $n$. The maintained question remains OPEN.

## A small example

For $n=31$, the choice $k=1$ gives $n-k=30=2\cdot3\cdot5$, so $\omega(30)=3$. With $k=7$, one has $n-k=24=2^3\cdot3$, hence $\omega(24)=2$ while $\Omega(24)=4$ when prime factors are counted **with** multiplicity. These finite factorizations illustrate the two functions, but they say nothing about the required all-large $k$ asymptotics.

## What is known

A related, stronger demand replaces the bound by $\log k/\log\log k+O(1)$. The [maintained discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/679) reports a **refutation** using a primorial $Q_m=2\cdot3\cdots p_m$ just below $n$ and $k=n-Q_m$, for which $\omega(n-k)=m$. Its quantitative comparison relies on prime-number-theorem asymptotics, and the complete historical proof is not internally source-locked. This refutes the additive-constant strengthening, *not* the original $1+\varepsilon$ bound. A primary 2026 article by [Cheuk Fung Lau](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15042v2) proves that infinitely many integers have prime-factor counts bounded by a constant times $\log k$ over relevant shifts. A $C\log k$ upper bound is substantially weaker than $\log k/\log\log k$; it does not resolve #679. Lau also discusses a conjectural mechanism pointing toward a negative answer, not an established proof.
