## The question in plain language

For fixed integers $k_1\ge k_2\ge3$, consider two nonoverlapping blocks of positive consecutive integers,

$$
A=\prod_{i=1}^{k_1}(n_1+i),\qquad
B=\prod_{j=1}^{k_2}(n_2+j),\qquad n_2\ge n_1+k_1.
$$

Can $A$ and $B$ have **exactly the same set of prime divisors** for infinitely many pairs $(n_1,n_2)$? Erdős conjectured finiteness. The question remains open. Equality of *prime support* means that the primes appearing are the same, **not** that their multiplicities or the products agree. [Maintained statement](https://www.erdosproblems.com/931).

## A small example

*Two concrete examples*

Tijdeman observed that the blocks $19,20,21,22$ and $54,55,56,57$ have the same prime support:

$$
\{2,3,5,7,11,19\}.
$$

Indeed, $19\cdot20\cdot21\cdot22$ has factors $19,(2^2\cdot5),(3\cdot7),(2\cdot11)$, while the latter block contributes $54=2\cdot3^3$, $55=5\cdot11$, $56=2^3\cdot7$, and $57=3\cdot19$.

Another recorded example uses $n_1=0,k_1=10,n_2=13,k_2=3$:

$$
10!=2^8 3^4 5^2 7,\qquad14\cdot15\cdot16=2^5\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7.
$$

Both have support $\{2,3,5,7\}$. This is also a counterexample to one possible **stronger** suggested spacing inequality $n_2>2(n_1+k_1)$, but one counterexample does not contradict a conjecture allowing finitely many exceptions.

## What is known

*A simple rigorous partial reduction*

Fix $n_1$, and let $S$ be the finite set of primes dividing $A$. If the second block has identical support, each term $n_2+j$ is an $S$‐unit integer (all its prime factors belong to $S$). In particular two consecutive terms in the second block are $S$‐smooth. By Størmer's classical finiteness theorem for consecutive $S$‐smooth integers, there are only finitely many such $n_2$ for fixed $n_1$. **This does not establish global finiteness**, since $n_1$, and therefore $S$, may grow without bound.

The maintained discussion reports further arguments and computations for $k_1=4,k_2=3$. These community claims need separate source and mathematical review before they are promoted to certified theorem status. In particular, bounded numerical searches do not establish global finiteness.

## Why the problem is difficult

In this problem equality concerns the union of prime divisors across a block. A prime appearing in one integer may be supplied by a different integer in the other block. The very strong requirement that *all* primes match must be reconciled with infinitely many possible changing support sets. Standard fixed‐$S$ finiteness arguments cannot rule out an unbounded sequence of distinct $S$.

## GCL's current state

Both displayed witnesses can be checked by direct factorization. The fixed‐$n_1$ argument depends on the cited classical Størmer theorem; it is not an original proof of that theorem. The universal finiteness conjecture is unresolved, and no machine-checked proof is claimed.
