**Editorial status:** An **unaccepted** explanation of a still-open problem. The historical source and a published author's partial-case note were directly inspected. The original full conjecture has not been proved, and the pinned formalization needs correction.

## The question in plain language

Choose an integer $k$, for example $k=3$. For each positive integer $n$, divide $2^n$ by $n$ and inspect the remainder.

Can a particular remainder $k$ occur for **infinitely many different choices of $n$**, once $k$ is fixed?

The congruence in question is

$$
2^n\equiv k\pmod n,
$$

equivalently, $n$ divides $2^n-k$. It works equally well when $k$ is negative: $2^n\equiv-1\pmod n$ means $n$ divides $2^n+1$.

Graham asked, as recorded by Erdős and Graham in 1980:

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\text{For every fixed integer }k\ne1,\ 
\#\{n\ge1:n\mid 2^n-k\}=\infty\;?
}
$$

The answer is **unknown in general**. A theorem for a particular infinite family of values of $k$, such as powers of two, is useful progress but does not establish the assertion for every integer.

There is a necessary exception: $k=1$ cannot work for any $n>1$. The one-element case $n=1$ technically satisfies every congruence, since all integers are equal modulo $1$. But one solution is not infinitely many solutions.

## A small example

Some simple congruences can be verified by hand.

For $k=2$, every odd prime $p$ is a solution because Fermat's little theorem gives

$$
2^p\equiv2\pmod p.
$$

Thus $n=3,5,7,11,13,\ldots$ work for $k=2$.

For $k=8=2^3$, the numbers $15,21,33,39,51,57,\ldots$ also work:

$$
2^{15}\equiv8\pmod{15},
\qquad
2^{21}\equiv8\pmod{21}.
$$

These are instances of a general construction explained below.

For $k=-1$, the numbers $3,9,27,81,\ldots$ satisfy

$$
2^n\equiv-1\pmod n.
$$

Indeed, for $n=3^j$, the lifting-the-exponent lemma gives

$$
v_3\bigl(2^{3^j}+1\bigr)=j+1,
$$

so the integer $2^{3^j}+1$ is divisible by $3^j$.

The case $k=3$ is far harder. Erdős and Graham reported in 1980 that the smallest solution with $n>1$ they had found was

$$
n=4\,700\,063\,497
=19\cdot47\cdot5\,263\,229.
$$

The arithmetic identity

$$
2^{4\,700\,063\,497}\equiv3
\pmod{4\,700\,063\,497}
$$

has been independently checked using exact modular exponentiation. The claim that this is the *smallest* solution was **not independently established** here by checking billions of smaller integers. Moreover, the existence of one extremely large solution does not imply infinitely many.

## What is known

### The original reported partial cases

On printed page 96 of *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), Erdős and Graham state that Graham, D. H. Lehmer and Emma Lehmer established infinitude when

$$
k=2^i\quad(i\ge1),\qquad\text{or}\qquad k=-1.
$$

Their bibliography identifies the Graham–Lehmer–Lehmer result as an **unpublished manuscript**. Its original proof has not been recovered in this audit.

Another elementary family is $k=0$: the solutions are exactly the powers of two. The case $k=-2$ also has an infinitude argument recorded in a solution to Problem 323 of *Mathematical Excalibur* (2009), but that argument has not been rederived here.

These special cases leave all other fixed values of $k$ unresolved in the account provided by Quanyu Tang's 2025 note. In particular, the general question is still classified **OPEN** by the maintained Erdős Problems catalogue.

### An explicit proof for the powers of two

Quanyu Tang's six-page note, *A Note on Erdős Problem #479: Infinitude of the Sets $A(2^i)$ and Related Results* (2 December 2025), presents a complete elementary reduction for each $k=2^i$ to Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions. Tang explicitly attributes the underlying claim to the older Graham–Lehmer–Lehmer result and does not claim it as new.

Here is the argument, with all divisibility conditions made explicit.

Fix $i\ge1$ and seek solutions of the form

$$
n=ip,
$$

where $p$ is a prime not dividing $2i$.

By Fermat's little theorem,

$$
2^{ip}=(2^p)^i\equiv2^i\pmod p.
$$

To obtain the congruence modulo $ip$, we also need it to hold modulo $i$.

Factor

$$
i=2^s\prod_{j=1}^tq_j^{e_j},
$$

where the $q_j$ are distinct odd primes. For each odd prime-power divisor, define

$$
d_j=\mathrm{ord}_{q_j^{e_j}}(2),
\qquad
m_j=\frac{d_j}{\gcd(d_j,i)},
\qquad
L=\mathrm{lcm}(m_1,\ldots,m_t).
$$

If there are no odd prime factors, take $L=1$. Choose a prime $p$ satisfying

$$
p\equiv1\pmod L,\qquad p\nmid 2i.
$$

Then $d_j$ divides $i(p-1)$ for every $j$, giving

$$
q_j^{e_j}\mid2^{i(p-1)}-1.
$$

The factor $2^s$ already divides $2^i$. Therefore

$$
i\mid2^i\bigl(2^{i(p-1)}-1\bigr)
=2^{ip}-2^i.
$$

Combined with divisibility by $p$ and $\gcd(i,p)=1$, this yields

$$
\boxed{2^{ip}\equiv2^i\pmod{ip}.}
$$

Dirichlet's theorem ensures infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod L$; excluding the finitely many prime divisors of $2i$ still leaves infinitely many. Thus this construction yields infinitely many distinct solutions $n=ip$ for **every** $i\ge1$.

The proof is meaningful progress in explaining known mathematics. It does not solve the original conjecture for arbitrary $k$.

### Why $k=1$ is excluded

Suppose $n>1$ and $2^n\equiv1\pmod n$.

If $n$ is even, this is impossible modulo $2$. If $n$ is odd, let $p$ be the smallest prime dividing $n$. The multiplicative order $d=\mathrm{ord}_p(2)$ then divides both $n$ and $p-1$.

But any prime divisor of $d$ would divide $n$ and be strictly smaller than $p$, contradicting the choice of $p$. Thus $d=1$, which would mean $2\equiv1\pmod p$, impossible. Hence $n=1$ is the sole solution for $k=1$.

### The remaining difficulty

For general $k$, neither Fermat's theorem nor an isolated modular solution automatically gives an infinite family. The extension from one solution $n$ to another $np$ would require compatible prime divisors of $2^n-k$ and additional congruence conditions modulo $n$. A useful precise lemma is:

If $n\mid2^n-k$, if $p$ is prime with $p\nmid n$ and $p\mid2^n-k$, and if $k^p\equiv k\pmod n$, then

$$
np\mid2^{np}-k.
$$

The proof checks the new congruence modulo $p$ with Fermat, modulo $n$ by raising $2^n\equiv k$ to the $p$‑th power, and combines them by the Chinese remainder theorem.

The missing step is not this elementary extension lemma. It is a theorem guaranteeing **infinitely many compatible extension primes** for an arbitrary fixed $k$.

## GCL's current state

The [authenticated GCL audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1749#issuecomment-6167988790) directly inspected the 1980 printed question and the actual six-page December 2025 Tang PDF, not merely a later summary.

The PDF was fetched with SHA-256 checksum

`7054c3f9598d29299c9a50878036d0bb0219c73713fd166e3cbb9c809ccac3ac`.

The audit independently reproduced small integer cases of Tang's construction, as well as the large historical $k=3$ congruence. It distinguished a checked **modular identity** from any claim of a complete search for the first solution.

A formal-source discrepancy is particularly important. GCL's protected Formal Conjectures snapshot, commit `85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020`, file `FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/479.lean`, uses a binder restricted to $k>1$ and a natural-number modular relation. That is weaker than Graham's assertion for every **integer** $k\ne1$.

A newer upstream file explicitly uses `(k : ℤ) (k ≠ 1)` and signed modular congruence, correcting the statement-level discrepancy. But this later source has **not** been admitted into the protected provider snapshot and still contains an unproved `sorry` theorem body. Source metadata and a formal statement are not a formal proof.

## What remains

The full problem is open for general $k$. A focused successor has three parts.

First, prove a precise statement-equivalence lemma between the historical integer-domain conjecture and its intended Lean representation. The pinned version cannot be silently treated as equivalent when it excludes $k\le1$ other than the special case $k=1$.

Second, independently replay the published-note construction for $k=2^i$, with Dirichlet's theorem and finite multiplicative-order conditions stated explicitly. The finite examples should be regenerated with an independent primality and modular-arithmetic checker, and every exceptional index should be recorded.

Third, investigate whether the prime-extension lemma can be iterated for a fixed unresolved case such as $k=3$ or $k=-5$. That requires a genuine theorem about prime divisors of $2^n-k$ obeying an extra residue-class constraint. A long finite list of successful extensions would not establish an infinite branch.

The original Graham–Lehmer–Lehmer manuscript is still unlocated. Its absence should remain a bibliographic blocker rather than be replaced by an invented citation.
