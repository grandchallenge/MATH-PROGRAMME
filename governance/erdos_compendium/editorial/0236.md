## The question in plain language

For each positive integer $n$, count the ways to represent it as a **prime plus a power of two**:

$$
R(n)=\#\{(p,k): p\text{ prime},\ k\ge0,\ n=p+2^k\}.
$$

Erdős asked whether $R(n)=o(\log n)$ as $n\to\infty$. The count includes the representation with $2^0=1$, and different exponents count as different solutions if their remainders are prime.

## A small example

For $n=11$, the eligible powers below $n$ are $1,2,4,8$. The remainders $10,9,7,3$ are prime precisely for the last two, giving $R(11)=2$. At any $n$, the trivial bound is $R(n)\le1+\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$, of order $\log n$. The question is whether the ratio to $\log n$ actually tends to zero for **every** large integer.

A finite list with a small ratio cannot prove a little-oh estimate, while a single integer with a high ratio cannot disprove it.

## What is known

**OPEN.** Erdős (1950) constructed infinitely many integers with $R(n)\gg\log\log n$. Thus the number of representations can grow without bound, but this lower bound is still compatible with $o(\log n)$.

A harder related question, #1142, asks whether there are infinitely many $n$ for which **every** eligible remainder $n-2^k$ is prime. Even if such numbers existed, one would need them on an unbounded sequence to disprove the little-oh claim. The maintained [Erdős #236](https://www.erdosproblems.com/236) cites OEIS A109925.

## What remains

A bounded task computes $R(n)$ for all $n$ up to a fixed cutoff with an exact prime sieve, checks $R(11)=2$ and graphs maximum ratios over growing windows. Acceptance: allow $k=0$ but require a **positive prime** remainder; distinguish existence of many representations along a subsequence from a universal upper bound.
