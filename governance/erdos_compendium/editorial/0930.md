## The question in plain language

Choose any fixed number $r$ of disjoint intervals of positive consecutive integers. Erdős asks whether a sufficiently large minimum length, depending only on $r$, makes it impossible for the product of **all** integers in these intervals to be an exact perfect power:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\prod_{m\in I_i}m \ne a^b\qquad(a,b\ge2).
$$

The question is open for general $r$. The intervals may be arbitrarily far apart: a bound on their lengths must work regardless of their locations. [Source statement](https://www.erdosproblems.com/930).

## A small example

*Example and the known one-interval case*

For the single interval $\{8,9,10\}$, the product is $720=2^4 3^2 5$; the exponent of $5$ equals one, preventing any nontrivial perfect power. One example, however, is not the theorem.

Erdős and Selfridge proved in 1975 that the product of two or more consecutive **positive integers** is never a perfect power. This settles $r=1$ (with minimum length two). The theorem is deep: seeing a prime with exponent one in some examples does not justify assuming that such a prime always exists.

## Why several intervals are different

Write $v_p(M)$ for the exponent of prime $p$ dividing a positive integer $M$. Then $M$ is a perfect $b$‐th power exactly when every $v_p(M)$ is divisible by $b$. Exponents arising in different intervals *add*:

$$
v_p\!\left(\prod_i\prod_{m\in I_i}m\right)
=\sum_i\sum_{m\in I_i}v_p(m).
$$

Thus distinct blocks can cancel one another's exponent obstructions modulo $b$, even though the individual block products are never powers. For $r=2$, the two singleton intervals $\{2\}$ and $\{8\}$ give product $16=4^2$, illustrating cancellation but not challenging the conjecture, which requires long blocks. The primary catalogue explicitly notes that length hypotheses are necessary even with two intervals.

## What is known and what is not

The Erdős–Selfridge theorem establishes the one-block case. The maintained catalogue identifies the first open case as $r=2$, and points to related problem #363, particularly for squares. No general theorem covering arbitrarily separated collections of $r$ sufficiently long blocks is asserted here. A finite search cannot show the required universal statement, since both interval starts and perfect-power exponents are unbounded.
