## The question in plain language

Let $A$ be an infinite set of positive integers for which the harmonic subseries converges. Form the three numbers

$$
\left(\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n},\;\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n+1},\;\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n+2}\right).
$$

Problem 268 asks whether the collection $X_3$ of all such triples contains a nonempty open set in $\mathbb{R}^3$. “Contains an open set” means that there is at least one point and a positive radius $r$ such that every point of the Euclidean ball of radius $r$ around it belongs to $X_3$. It does not mean that every triple is attainable, or that one fixed set $A$ works for all triples. The quantifiers are: there exists a whole open region, and for each target point in that region there exists an infinite set $A$ that represents it.

The equivalent sequence language is to enumerate $A$ increasingly as $a_1<a_2<\cdots$. The positive-integer convention matters: the denominators are $n,n+1,n+2$ with $n\geq 1$, so no zero denominator is present. The convergence assumption on the first sum also makes the other two sums converge, since each shifted term is no larger than $1/n$.

The status is **proved at the published-paper level**. The formal-status evidence is a separate unresolved matter, reported below rather than silently promoted to certification.

## A small example

Take the finite set $A_0=\{1,3,10\}$. Its three partial sums are exactly

$$
\sum_{n\in A_0}\frac1n=1+\frac13+\frac1{10}=\frac{43}{30},
$$

$$
\sum_{n\in A_0}\frac1{n+1}=\frac12+\frac14+\frac1{11}=\frac{37}{44},
\qquad
\sum_{n\in A_0}\frac1{n+2}=\frac13+\frac15+\frac1{12}=\frac{37}{60}.
$$

This is a deliberately finite calculation: $A_0$ is not an admissible infinite set and therefore does not solve the problem. It does show how one target triple is assembled and makes the indexing visible. The arithmetic is independently reproducible with exact rational arithmetic:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
A = {1, 3, 10}
print(tuple(sum((Fraction(1, n + j) for n in A), Fraction()) for j in range(3)))
```

The output is `(Fraction(43, 30), Fraction(37, 44), Fraction(37, 60))`. The same three-coordinate normalization, including the positive-index convention, is the one used in Kovač’s Theorem 1 and in the exact audit calculation linked in the sources below.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham’s 1980 book records the two-dimensional theorem of Erdős and Straus and then asks on printed p. 65 whether the analogous three-dimensional set, with denominators $a_k$, $a_k+1$, and $a_k+2$, contains an open set. Erdős’s 1988 paper repeats that the planar set contains open sets, says the higher-dimensional analogue was probably true, and explains that their proof was not published because the higher-dimensional case had not been worked out.

Kovač’s paper, “On the set of points represented by harmonic subseries,” gives the affirmative answer. Its Theorem 1 states that

$$
X_3=\left\{\left(\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n,\sum_{n\in A}\frac1{n+1},\sum_{n\in A}\frac1{n+2}\right): A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\text{ infinite},\ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n<\infty\right\}
$$

has nonempty interior, where the paper explicitly takes $\mathbb{N}$ to mean the positive integers. The proof uses a linear change of coordinates and a convergence game to handle perturbation errors. Section 5 gives a concrete open ball of radius $10^{-24}$ inside the set. This is a published mathematical proof of the registered question, not merely a numerical search.

Kovač and Tao’s later paper, “On several irrationality problems for Ahmes series,” gives a broader result. Their Theorem 2.8 proves nonempty interior for tuples of $d$ consecutive reciprocal sums even while requiring a suitably double-exponential lower-growth condition on the increasing sequence. Corollary 2.10 removes that growth restriction in the set formulation and proves the analogous assertion for every positive integer $d$, hence for $d=3$. Their construction is existential and does not provide one explicit sequence for every chosen rational target; that distinction is important.
