**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #491 is **PROVED**, by E. Wirsing in 1970. Its conclusion permits a bounded error; replacing that conclusion by an exact equality would be wrong. The original Wirsing full proof has not been independently replayed in this editorial audit.

## The question in plain language

Consider a real-valued function $f(n)$ on positive integers. It is an **additive arithmetic function** if

$$
f(ab)=f(a)+f(b)\qquad\text{whenever }\gcd(a,b)=1.
$$

The coprimality condition matters: one may not infer $f(p^k)=k f(p)$ from this definition. Such a stronger rule is called *complete additivity*.

Suppose that $f$ also changes by only a bounded amount between consecutive integers: there exists $C>0$ with

$$
|f(n+1)-f(n)|<C\qquad\text{for every }n\ge1.
$$

Erdős conjectured that $f$ must resemble the ordinary logarithm, up to a uniformly bounded correction. Precisely, does there exist a real constant $c$ such that

$$
\boxed{f(n)=c\log n+O(1)?}
$$

Here $O(1)$ means that $|f(n)-c\log n|$ is bounded **independently of $n$**, not that the error tends to zero. The maintained [Erdős #491 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/491) lists the answer as **PROVED**.

This is a rigidity phenomenon: arithmetic additivity together with small changes on the ordinary number line severely limits possible growth.

## A small example

The basic example $f(n)=c\log n$ is even *completely* additive because $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$. Its consecutive differences are

$$
f(n+1)-f(n)=c\log\left(1+\frac1n\right),
$$

whose absolute values are at most $|c|\log2$.

But exact equality with $c\log n$ is **not necessary**. Define

$$
g(n)=
\begin{cases}
t,&2\mid n,\\
0,&2\nmid n,
\end{cases}
\qquad f(n)=c\log n+g(n),
$$

where $t\ne0$. If $a,b$ are coprime, at most one is even, so $g(ab)=g(a)+g(b)$. Thus $f$ is additive **on coprime arguments**. Moreover $|g(n)|\le|t|$ and

$$
|f(n+1)-f(n)|\le |c|\log2+|t|.
$$

The error oscillates between $0$ and $t$ rather than disappearing. For $c=0$, we have a bounded, nonzero additive function whose consecutive differences are bounded. In particular $g(4)=t$ but $2g(2)=2t$: it is not completely additive. Any editorial statement claiming $f=c\log$ *exactly* from merely bounded consecutive differences would be falsified by this example.

A finite-prime generalization is

$$
f(n)=c\log n+\sum_{\substack{p\in P\\p\mid n}}t_p,
$$

where $P$ is a fixed finite set of primes. The correction is bounded by $\sum_{p\in P}|t_p|$ and is additive for coprime inputs. This gives a useful suite of exact finite fixtures.

## What is known

### The original 1961 question

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.237, item I.30, Erdős defines additivity only for relatively prime inputs and explicitly writes

$$
f(n)=c_2\log n+g(n),\qquad |g(n)|<c_3,
$$

under a uniform bound on the consecutive difference. This printed page was visually inspected. The same paragraph recalls his 1946 result that the stronger assumptions $f(n+1)-f(n)\to0$ or $f(n+1)\ge f(n)$ force *exact* logarithmic form. The two different hypotheses and conclusions must not be conflated.

### Wirsing's theorem

E. Wirsing proved the conjecture in *A characterization of log n as an additive arithmetic function*, *Symposia Mathematica* **IV** (1970), pp.45–57. A contemporary first-person Wirsing theorem announcement in the *Notices of the American Mathematical Society* (November 1970), abstract 681-A21, asserts even the **one-sided** condition

$$
f(n+1)-f(n)\ge-K
$$

suffices for $f(n)=c\log n+O(1)$ in the additive arithmetic setting. A one-sided lower bound is weaker than a two-sided uniform absolute-value bound. The full 1970 proceedings proof and all its quantified refinements have **not** been independently retrieved or reconstructed. The author's announcement, later mathematical surveys and maintained catalogue consistently attribute the theorem to Wirsing.

Erdős, *On the distribution function of additive functions*, *Annals of Mathematics* **47** (1946), pp.1–20, is the earlier primary source for the stronger special assumptions. The complete proof has not been replayed here. Related questions are catalogued under [Erdős #897](https://www.erdosproblems.com/897) and [#1122](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1122), without altering the hypothesis of #491.
