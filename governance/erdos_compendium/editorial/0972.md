## The question in plain language

Fix an **irrational real number** \(\alpha>1\), and let \(p\) range over the ordinary rational primes. Erdős asked whether there are **infinitely many prime inputs \(p\)** for which the integer
\[
\boxed{q=\lfloor\alpha p\rfloor}
\]
is also prime. The quantifiers are
\[
\forall\alpha\in(1,\infty)\setminus\mathbb Q,\
\forall X>0,\
\exists p>X:\ p\text{ prime and }\lfloor\alpha p\rfloor\text{ prime}.
\]
The maintained catalogue classifies the problem as **OPEN**. A verified finite list of successful pairs for one irrational \(\alpha\) does not establish this statement for that \(\alpha\), let alone uniformly for **every** irrational \(\alpha>1\). [Canonical Erdős Problem #972](https://www.erdosproblems.com/972).

## Exact examples for the square root of two

Choose \(\alpha=\sqrt2\). Computing the floors with **integer arithmetic**, not floating-point approximation, gives
\[
\lfloor p\sqrt2\rfloor=\left\lfloor\sqrt{2p^2}\right\rfloor .
\]
For the primes \(p=2,5,29,31\), the corresponding integers are
\[
(2,2),\quad(5,7),\quad(29,41),\quad(31,43).
\]
Each output in these four pairs is prime. For contrast,
\(\lfloor3\sqrt2\rfloor=4\) and \(\lfloor7\sqrt2\rfloor=9\) are composite. The mixed behavior illustrates that **equidistribution of fractional parts** does not decide simultaneous primality.

The formula using the integer square-root operation \(\operatorname{isqrt}(2p^2)\) yields an exact finite computational certificate even for very large \(p\). A floating-point implementation might misclassify floor values close to integers and should not be considered source-quality mathematical evidence.

## An important theorem that is weaker than the question

Vinogradov's 1948 theorem implies that, for every irrational \(\theta\), the fractional parts
\[
\{p\theta\},\qquad p\text{ prime},
\]
are uniformly distributed in \([0,1)\). The catalogue uses this to prove that, for every irrational \(\alpha>1\), infinitely many primes \(p\) belong to the **Beatty sequence**
\[
B_\alpha=\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\ge1\}.
\]

Here is a full derivation of that consequence. A prime \(p\) belongs to \(B_\alpha\) precisely when there is an integer \(n\) such that
\[
p\le n\alpha<p+1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac p\alpha\le n<\frac{p+1}{\alpha}.
\]
This interval has length \(1/\alpha<1\). Since \(\alpha\) is irrational, \(p/\alpha\) is not an integer. Hence the interval contains an integer if and only if
\[
\left\{\frac p\alpha\right\}>1-\frac1\alpha .
\]
The multiplier \(1/\alpha\) is irrational; Vinogradov's theorem says the last strict inequality holds for a positive fraction of primes, and in particular infinitely often. Thus there are infinitely many primes **of the form**
\[
p=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor,\quad n\in\mathbb N.
\]

**This is not the conjecture.** It starts with an **arbitrary integer \(n\)** and proves that \(\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor\) is prime for infinitely many such \(n\). Erdős #972 demands that the input \(n=p\) **itself be prime**. Confusing these two nested conditions incorrectly upgrades a classical single-primality theorem into a simultaneous-primality proof.

## Why the simultaneous problem is genuinely harder

The conjecture is a correlation question about two arithmetic constraints:
\[
p\in\mathbb P,\qquad \lfloor\alpha p\rfloor\in\mathbb P.
\]
Classical Vinogradov equidistribution controls fractional parts along **one** prime sequence. It says nothing directly about whether the second, floor-transformed value also avoids divisibility by every small prime. Likewise, the prime number theorem for Beatty sequences concerns primality of values at **unrestricted integer inputs**, not the required intersection with prime inputs.

A common heuristic predicts on the order of
\[
\frac{x}{(\log x)^2}
\]
successful inputs \(p\le x\) for a fixed generic irrational \(\alpha\), because there are about \(x/\log x\) prime inputs, and a typical nearby integer is prime with probability about \(1/\log x\). This is an **uncertified heuristic**, not a theorem, and arithmetic correlations may alter its constant or invalidate naive independence assumptions.

For perspective, extending the question beyond the stipulated irrational \(\alpha>1\) includes a related hard rational case. If \(\alpha=1/2\), then for an odd prime \(p\) the integer \(\lfloor p/2\rfloor=(p-1)/2\) is prime precisely when \(p=2q+1\) and \(q\) is prime: this is the Sophie Germain prime problem, another open infinitude assertion. It illustrates the difficulty of a broader two-prime correlation problem but is **not** a reduction establishing any implication for the original irrational domain. Integer multipliers \(\alpha=m\ge2\) are trivially excluded, since \(\lfloor mp\rfloor=mp\) is composite.

## What the sources establish

The maintained problem page cites Erdős [Er65b] for the original question and Vinogradov [Vi48] for the one-prime Beatty theorem. Comments by Stijn Cambie and Terence Tao in October 2025 compare the question with prime-pair problems and emphasize its expected difficulty. They present no claimed resolution. The page reports statement formalization, but an encoded theorem with an unresolved proof is **not** proof certification. No later source encountered in this tranche establishes the required infinitude for every irrational \(\alpha>1\). Accordingly the present mathematical standing is **OPEN**. [Discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/972).

There is an exact source-fidelity condition worth retaining: \(\alpha\) must be **both** irrational and strictly greater than one. The algebraic boundary at \(\alpha=1\) gives \(\lfloor p\alpha\rfloor=p\) for every prime; that case is trivially true and not part of the stipulated open problem.
