**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #506 is classified **DECIDABLE**: a sharp formula for the minimum number of distinct circles is reported for sufficiently large point sets, while a finite range of smaller cases remains unsettled. The exact nondegeneracy assumptions matter: excluding configurations lying entirely on one circle is **not enough**; configurations lying on one line must also be excluded.

## The question in plain language

Given $n$ **distinct points** in the Euclidean plane, how few **different circles** can they determine? A circle is counted once if it passes through at least three of the points, however many different triples determine that same circle.

Three **noncollinear** points determine a unique nondegenerate Euclidean circle. Three **collinear** points do not determine any circle. So a single circle can be counted many times if we naively enumerate triples, and some triples contribute nothing.

To make the extremal problem nontrivial, write

$$
c(n)=
\min_{\substack{P\subset\mathbb R^2,\ |P|=n\\
P\text{ not all collinear}\\
P\text{ not all concyclic}}}
\#\{\text{distinct circles containing at least three points of }P\}.
$$

The qualification “not all collinear” is necessary. A configuration consisting of $n$ points on a straight line is not contained in any circle for $n\ge3$, but determines **zero circles**. Without that exclusion the answer would be trivially zero.

The stronger hypothesis “no three points collinear” is **not equivalent**. It would exclude some of the extremal constructions below and would define a genuinely different problem.

The original Erdős 1961 source only explicitly says the points are not all on a circle. The maintained [#506 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/506) notes that a nondegeneracy condition was evidently intended. Its special **DECIDABLE** status is not the same as a universal theorem proving a closed-form answer for every $n$.

## A small example

Take a unit circle with four distinct points arranged into **two antipodal pairs**, and add its centre $O$. There are now five points, not all on a circle and not all on a line.

Three outer points always lie on the original circle, contributing **one** circle. Any pair of outer points together with the centre usually determines another circle. However, a pair of **opposite** points is collinear with $O$ and therefore determines **no** circle.

There are $\binom42=6$ outer pairs, of which **two** are antipodal. The total is therefore

$$
c(P)=1+6-2=5.
$$

This is an **exact count for the displayed configuration**. It proves $c(5)\le5$, not by itself that every five-point nondegenerate configuration determines at least five circles.

For general $n$, place $m=n-1$ points on one circle as $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ antipodal pairs, with one additional unpaired point when $m$ is odd. Add the circle's centre $O$. The construction gives

$$
\boxed{
C(P)=1+\binom{n-1}{2}
-\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
}
$$

Why does each nonantipodal outer pair give a **different** circle? Each new circle contains the centre $O$, which is not on the original circle. If two distinct outer pairs produced the same new circle, that circle would contain **at least three distinct outer points**. Those three points also lie on the original circle, but two different circles cannot pass through the same three noncollinear points. Therefore there can be no duplication.

This yields an exact **upper bound** on the minimum $c(n)$. Showing it is a lower bound for *all* eligible configurations is the difficult extremal part.

## What is known

### Erdős's 1961 question and Elliott's 1967 theorem

The original *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.245, Section III.4, [source scan](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=25), asks for the minimum number of circles determined by $n$ points not all on a circle. The original page was directly examined. A complete theorem must make the additional non-all-collinear assumption explicit to avoid the zero-circle counterexample.

P. D. T. A. Elliott, *On the number of circles determined by n points*, *Acta Mathematica Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae* **18** (1967), 181–188, [DOI 10.1007/BF02020972](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02020972), studied the large‑$n$ problem. Elliott claimed for $n>393$ a lower bound

$$
c(n)\ge\binom{n-1}{2}.
$$

This assertion was **too strong**: a correction attributed to Purdy and Smith shows that the actual argument supports only

$$
c(n)\ge
1+\binom{n-1}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
\qquad(n>393).
$$

Because the circle-plus-centre construction meets this corrected lower bound exactly, the large‑$n$ **extremal value** is identified. For instance, the corrected formula gives $c(394)=76833$, provided the corrected general lower bound is accepted.

The maintained catalogue also reports this lower bound in A. Bálintová and V. Bálint, *On the number of circles determined by n points in the Euclidean plane*, *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* **63** (1994), 283–289, [DOI 10.1007/BF01874133](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01874133). Its publisher's 1994 journal inventory independently confirms the publication.

**Primary-proof boundary:** Elliott's 1967 full proof, the 1994 paper's full proof and the originally cited Purdy–Smith correction were not retrieved in full during this audit. The public catalogue does not supply a bibliographic record for Purdy and Smith. The universal corrected lower bound is therefore a **published-result attribution**, not a theorem independently reproved by GCL from those missing papers.

### Why the original lower bound is false for small configurations

For $n=8$, take seven points on a circle arranged as three antipodal pairs plus one additional distinct point, and its centre. Exactly

$$
1+\binom72-\left\lfloor\frac72\right\rfloor
=1+21-3=19
$$

circles are determined. This is strictly below $\binom72=21$. Hence Elliott's originally stated **unadjusted** inequality cannot hold for every $n$. The maintained catalogue also cites an earlier geometric observation by Segre involving a projected cube at $n=8$.

This construction shows why the phrase “$n-1$ points on a circle and one point off” is incomplete when describing equality. A **generic** off-circle point is not aligned with the outer pairs and would yield one circle through the outer points plus $\binom{n-1}{2}$ distinct circles through that point and any two outer points. Placing the additional point at the **centre** is what makes antipodal triples collinear and removes exactly $\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$ circles.

### What DECIDABLE means here

The special catalogue status **DECIDABLE** means that the asymptotic or sufficiently-large range is settled and only a finite range remains to be checked. It does **not** mean that the actual minimum has been computed for each integer $n\le393$. The catalogue explicitly states those remaining small cases are not all resolved.

There is also a formal, algorithmic sense of decidability. For each **fixed** $n$, configurations can be described by finitely many real coordinates, and predicates such as collinearity, concyclicity and equality of circumcircles can be expressed by polynomial equations and inequalities. The existence of a configuration with at most a specified number of distinct circles is therefore expressible in the first-order theory of **real closed fields**, which admits quantifier elimination. This provides a decision procedure in principle for a fixed $n$, but it is likely computationally huge and is not a substitute for a published list of exact extremal values.

The base theory here is ordinary real Euclidean geometry, not an undecidable axiom system or a set-theoretic independence question. The word “decidable” should not be converted into “every case solved.”

It is not a protected GCL formal theorem or an independently verified kernel receipt.

A new exact-arithmetic fixture constructs rational points on the unit circle together with their centre, enumerates noncollinear triples and **normalizes every resulting circle** to a polynomial equation

$$
x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F=0,
$$

where $D,E,F$ are rational. Distinct normalized coefficient triples are precisely distinct circles. This avoids floating-point tolerance and the severe overcounting that would result from simply counting triples.

## What remains

The immediate bounded task is to verify the count formula on explicit rational configurations with both even and odd numbers of outer points and to check that an all-collinear set determines zero circles. Those are reproducible exact facts and clear source-fidelity guards.

For full critical mathematical certification, the Elliott 1967 and Bálint–Bálintová 1994 originals must be recovered and their **corrected universal lower bound** checked, including the $n>393$ threshold and whether the proof allows some collinear triples. The Purdy–Smith correction needs a bibliographically identified original source.

Any attempt to settle all remaining small $n$ is a new finite extremal-geometry investigation. This article does not launch it or reclassify DECIDABLE as either ordinary SOLVED or OPEN.
