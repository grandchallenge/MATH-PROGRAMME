## The question in plain language

Take $n$ distinct points in the Euclidean plane. Count unordered pairs separated by exactly distance one. Erdős conjectured that the maximum possible count was close to linear in $n$, specifically that it was bounded by

$$
n^{1+O(1/\log\log n)}.
$$

This is the celebrated **unit-distance problem**, now known to have a negative answer. The asymptotic conjecture asserts that the exponent excess over one tends to zero, not merely that some polynomial upper bound exists.

## A small example

The vertices of a unit square contain four pairs at distance one, corresponding to its four sides. Its two diagonals have length $\sqrt2$ and do not count. Scaling a point configuration changes its distance-one incidences, so this problem is about actual Euclidean distance, not an arbitrary graph with the same adjacency pattern.

A grid produces many unit-distance pairs, but its count stays linear in the number of vertices. Such an example does not disprove the conjecture.

## What is known

**DISPROVED.** The primary announcement [*An OpenAI model has disproved a central conjecture in discrete geometry*](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/) (20 May 2026) links the mathematical paper *Planar Point Sets with Many Unit Distances* and the accompanying human-authored *Remarks on the Disproof of the Unit Distance Conjecture*. Their main assertion is that some absolute $\delta>0$ exists and infinitely many $n$ admit $n$ planar points determining at least $n^{1+\delta}$ unit-distance pairs. This contradicts a uniform $n^{1+o(1)}$ upper bound.

This result does not give a matching sharp exponent. The classical Spencer–Szemerédi–Trotter upper bound $O(n^{4/3})$ remains a separate statement. The new construction uses algebraic number theory and is not merely a computational search for a large finite unit-distance graph.

## What remains

A bounded verification task is to implement the elementary implication: if there exist point sets with at least $n^{1+\delta}$ unit-distance pairs for infinitely many $n$, then the historical bound $n^{1+O(1/\log\log n)}$ is false. Acceptance: explicitly prove that every fixed $\delta>0$ eventually exceeds every sufficiently bounded $C/\log\log n$, and verify that points are distinct and pairs unordered. This does not certify the number-theoretic construction itself. A separate formal replay would need the exact third-party Lean source and an axiom audit.
