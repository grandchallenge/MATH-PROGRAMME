## The question in plain language

Sometimes the least common multiple of a block of $k$ consecutive integers exceeds the lcm of a **later block containing $k+1$ integers**. Erdős Problem [#678](https://www.erdosproblems.com/678) asks whether there are infinitely many such triples of starting points and lengths. The answer is **yes**, established by Stijn Cambie in 2024. The length $k$ is allowed to increase across the family of examples. It is *not* true that each fixed sufficiently large $k$ has infinitely many qualifying pairs of starts.

## A small example

The original 1979 referee example gives $M(96,7)=8\,321\,670\,749\,700$ and $M(104,8)=3\,803\,928\,503\,760$, where $M(n,k)=\operatorname{lcm}(n+1,\ldots,n+k)$. Thus $M(96,7)>M(104,8)$ with separated intervals because $104\geq96+7$. Cambie's paper provides another exact example, $M(52,7)=143\,282\,767\,320 > M(62,8)=75\,522\,807\,360$. All four numbers were checked by exact integer gcd/lcm recurrence. Two examples alone do not establish infinitude.

## What is known

The directly inspected [Cambie Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09138) is stronger than the existential conjecture: for every constant $C\geq1$ and every sufficiently large length $k$, one can find disjoint blocks with $k$ and $k+1$ terms whose lcm ratio exceeds $C$. This supplies infinitely many triples as $k$ varies. The distinction from a fixed-$k$ infinite family is mathematical, not editorial pedantry. For fixed $k$, one has $M(n,k)\leq(n+k)^k$, while the consecutive-lcm binomial estimate gives $M(m,k+1)\geq\binom{m+k+1}{k+1}$. With $m\geq n+k$, the second grows as $n^{k+1}$ for fixed $k$, so $n$ must remain bounded if the desired reversed inequality holds. Then $m$ must also be bounded. The fixed-$k$ infinite-pairs version is therefore false.
