**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #499 is **PROVED**: every doubly stochastic matrix has a permutation diagonal whose product is at least $n^{-n}$. This is weaker than the now-proved van der Waerden permanent inequality, but it is not the same assertion as the still weaker positive-diagonal sum statement.

## The question in plain language

Suppose $M=(a_{ij})$ is a real $n\times n$ matrix with nonnegative entries. It is **doubly stochastic** when every row sums to $1$ and every column sums to $1$:

$$
a_{ij}\ge0,\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}=1,\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij}=1.
$$

For any permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, choose one entry from each row and from each column, namely $a_{i,\sigma(i)}$. Erdős asked whether **some** such permutation satisfies

$$
\boxed{\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}\ge n^{-n}.}
$$

The quantifier is *there exists a permutation*, rather than *every permutation*. A matrix can have many zero products and nevertheless contain a suitable positive diagonal. We take $n\ge1$, as the question concerns ordinary positive-order stochastic matrices.

The [maintained Erdős #499](https://www.erdosproblems.com/499) classifies this claim **PROVED (LEAN)** and cites Marvin Marcus and Henryk Minc's 1962 work.

## A small example

Every $2\times2$ doubly stochastic matrix has the form

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
t&1-t\\1-t&t
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad0\le t\le1.
$$

The two permutation products are $t^2$ and $(1-t)^2$. Since at least one of $t$ and $1-t$ is at least $1/2$,

$$
\max\{t^2,(1-t)^2\}\ge\frac14=2^{-2}.
$$

This completely proves the $n=2$ case. Equality is attained when $t=1/2$. For $n=3$, the uniform matrix with entries $1/3$ gives *every* permutation product exactly $1/27=3^{-3}$.

At another extreme, a permutation matrix has exactly one permutation diagonal with product $1$; all other permutation products are zero. Thus it would be false to demand that every diagonal have large product.

The important operation here is a **product** across a permutation diagonal. It must not be replaced with the *sum* of the diagonal entries or the permanent of the whole matrix without explaining the relationship among these quantities.

## What is known

### From Erdős's 1961 question to the permanent

The original *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.242, Problem II.7, was visually inspected at [the original page](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=22). Erdős states van der Waerden's permanent conjecture and then proposes two successively weaker statements. The first is precisely the existence of a permutation product at least $n^{-n}$. The second asks for a **nonzero** permutation diagonal whose entry **sum** is at least $1$; Erdős attributes that earlier weaker result to Marcus and Ree (1959).

The permanent of a square matrix is

$$
\mathrm{perm}(M)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}.
$$

Unlike the determinant, the permanent uses no alternating signs. Van der Waerden conjectured that for every $n\times n$ doubly stochastic matrix,

$$
\mathrm{perm}(M)\ge\frac{n!}{n^n}.
$$

This stronger inequality was established by the early-1980s work of Egorychev and Falikman, with Gyires also credited for an earlier result in the maintained catalogue. An original 1981 Falikman [journal record](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=mzm&option_lang=rus&paperid=6253&wshow=paper) states the full permanent lower bound.

### One-line deduction of Erdős's bound

The $n!$ diagonal products in the permanent are **nonnegative**. Hence their maximum is at least their average:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\sigma\in S_n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}
&\ge\frac{1}{n!}\mathrm{perm}(M)\\
&\ge\frac1{n^n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first inequality is elementary and self-contained. The second invokes the *published* van der Waerden theorem, whose full original proof is not reconstructed here. This proves the implication from the stronger theorem to #499; it does not claim that Erdős's weaker result was historically first proved only in 1981.
