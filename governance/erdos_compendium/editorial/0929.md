## The question in plain language

For each integer \(k\ge2\), define \(S(k)\) to be the smallest real \(x\) such that a positive-density set of integers \(n\) has the property that every member of \(n+1,\ldots,n+k\) is divisible by some prime at most \(x\). Erdős asked how \(S(k)\) grows, particularly whether \(S(k)\ge k^{1-o(1)}\). This remains open.

## A small example

For \(k=3\), if \(n\equiv1\pmod{24}\), the integers \(n+1,n+2,n+3\) are respectively divisible by \(2,3,2\). The progression has positive density \(1/24\). For general \(k\), choose \(n\equiv1\pmod{(k+1)!}\). Then \(n+j\equiv j+1\pmod{(k+1)!}\) and \(n+j\) has a prime divisor at most \(j+1\le k+1\). Thus \(S(k)\le k+1\). The modulus \((k+1)!\), rather than \(k!\), is essential to this particular argument.

## What is known

The primary catalogue records the unconditional Rosser-sieve estimate
\[
S(k)>k^{1/2-o(1)}.
\]
Work of Ford, Green, Konyagin, Maynard and Tao on large gaps between primes implies
\[
S(k)\ll k\frac{\log\log\log k}{(\log\log k)(\log\log\log\log k)}.
\]
These asymptotic estimates leave a substantial gap. The proposed near-linear *lower* bound has not been proved. See the [maintained source entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/929).

## Why the problem is difficult

A prime \(p\) can cover positions \(j\) in just one residue class modulo \(p\), once the residue of \(n\) is fixed. Several small primes must cooperate to cover each of the \(k\) positions. A compatible finite system of residue conditions automatically produces positive density by the Chinese remainder theorem. A lower bound therefore has to rule out unusually efficient residue coverings, not merely show that random intervals usually contain a number with large least prime divisor.

## GCL's current state

The elementary factorial-progression upper bound is verified directly. The sieve lower bound and deep prime-gap upper bound are accurately attributed literature results; this article does not claim independent proofs. Exhaustive small-parameter computations cannot decide the \(k\to\infty\) conjecture.
