## The question in plain language

Prime numbers grow irregularly. A prime might have unusually small gaps on one side and unusually large gaps on the other. Erdős Problem #454 asks whether the middle prime can sometimes lie **arbitrarily far below every symmetric pair-average** of surrounding primes, when the surrounding primes are chosen an equal number of positions to the left and right.

Write $p_n$ for the $n$th prime, with $p_1=2$. For every integer $n\geq2$, define

$$
f(n)=\min_{1\leq i<n}\bigl(p_{n-i}+p_{n+i}\bigr)
$$

and the **signed gap**

$$
D(n)=f(n)-2p_n.
$$

The precise question is

$$
\boxed{\limsup_{n\to\infty}D(n)=+\infty\;?}
$$

This means that for every real threshold $B$ and every starting index $N$, some $n\geq N$ makes **every** symmetric sum greater than $2p_n+B$. Finding a single pair with a large sum is insufficient because $f(n)$ selects the *smallest* sum across all allowed offsets.

It is important to distinguish this question from neighboring [Erdős Problem #453](https://www.erdosproblems.com/453). That problem concerns the **products** $p_{n-i}p_{n+i}$ and has been disproved. Problem #454 concerns **sums** and asks for an *unbounded additive difference*. It remains open.

## A small example

Take the fourth prime, $p_4=7$. We must check all three legal offsets:

| Offset $i$ | Symmetric sum $p_{4-i}+p_{4+i}$ | Difference from $2p_4=14$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| $1$ | $5+11=16$ | $2$ |
| $2$ | $3+13=16$ | $2$ |
| $3$ | $2+17=19$ | $5$ |

The smallest sum is $16$, so $f(4)=16$ and **$D(4)=2$**.

This is not what happens at every index. For $n=3$, the sums are $3+7=10$ and $2+11=13$, giving $D(3)=0$. For $n=5$, choosing $i=1$ gives $7+13=20<22=2p_5$, while all other offsets give at least $22$; hence **$D(5)=-2$**. At $n=2$ the only offset gives $2+5-2\cdot3=1$.

These are exact integer calculations, illustrating why $D(n)$ must be treated as a signed quantity. A computer calculation of thousands or millions of indices could suggest that positive gaps grow, but no finite search can establish an infinite limsup.

## What is known

The question appears on printed page 90 of Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), immediately after their discussion of Pomerance's prime-number-graph results.

Carl Pomerance's original paper, *The Prime Number Graph* (1979), provides the relevant geometric tool. His **Theorem 2.1**, printed page 400, proves that if $0<a_1<a_2<\cdots$ is a strictly increasing sequence with $n/a_n\to0$, then there are infinitely many $n$ such that

$$
2a_n<a_{n-i}+a_{n+i}
\qquad\text{for every }1\leq i<n.
$$

Geometrically, those indices are vertices on the lower convex boundary of the points $(n,a_n)$. The prime number theorem implies $p_n/n\to\infty$, so the theorem applies to $a_n=p_n$. Consequently, infinitely many indices satisfy $D(n)>0$.

**That is not the same as proving $D(n)$ unbounded.** Pomerance explicitly makes the distinction on printed page 406, §5. He defines the same function (called $A(n)$ there), proves that it exceeds $2p_n$ infinitely often, and conjectures that $A(n)-2p_n$ takes arbitrarily large values. He reports a finite search through $n\leq1000$ with maximum observed gap $24$ at $n=985$. This historical computation is evidence about small inputs, not proof about arbitrarily large indices.

The stronger established statement is

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}D(n)\geq2.
$$

One can see why the integer $2$ appears. For any large $n$ among Pomerance's strictly positive witnesses, examine all offsets $i$:

- If $1\leq i\leq n-2$, both $p_{n-i}$ and $p_{n+i}$ are odd. Their sum and $2p_n$ are even, so a *strictly positive* difference is at least $2$.
- For the exceptional offset $i=n-1$, the left-hand prime is $2$. The difference is $p_{2n-1}+2-2p_n$. A sufficiently precise classical asymptotic for the $n$th prime gives

$$
p_n=n\bigl(\log n+\log\log n-1+o(1)\bigr),
$$

and hence

$$
p_{2n-1}+2-2p_n=2n\log2+o(n)\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

Thus that exceptional offset is also greater than $2$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Every sufficiently late positive convex-hull witness therefore has $D(n)\geq2$.

A technical warning matters: the weaker first-order approximation $p_n\sim n\log n$ **alone is not enough** to justify subtracting these two prime terms and obtaining the asymptotic $2n\log2$. The above calculation requires the next-order Cesàro–Cipolla expansion, or another comparably strong prime estimate.

## What remains

The unresolved task is not to show the existence of positive gaps: that was established decades ago. It is to prove or refute the existence of **arbitrarily large** positive gaps when **every possible symmetric prime pair** is tested at an index.

A bounded successor project could formalize the known statement $\limsup D(n)\geq2$, auditing the zero-based indexing, the exclusion of offset zero, the exceptional prime $2$, the parity step, and the precise asymptotic expansion. Success would establish a checked benchmark, not solve the open conjecture.

A separate exploratory project could test how high gaps correlate with extremal vertices of the prime-number graph. Such computations may guide a proof strategy but cannot by themselves justify an asymptotic claim.
