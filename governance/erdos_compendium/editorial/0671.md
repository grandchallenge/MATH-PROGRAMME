## The question in plain language

When we approximate a continuous function by a polynomial through chosen sample points, can the interpolation procedure be **unstable** at a point but still converge there for the function under consideration?

[Erdős Problem #671](https://www.erdosproblems.com/671) asks two carefully quantified versions of that question for sequences of Lagrange interpolation nodes in $[-1,1]$. Each row of the node array has $n$ distinct points $a_1^n,\ldots,a_n^n$, with Lagrange cardinal polynomials $p_i^n$. Define

$$

L_nf(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n f(a_i^n)p_i^n(x),\qquad

\Lambda_n(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n|p_i^n(x)|.

$$

The quantity $\Lambda_n(x)$ measures how strongly interpolation at $x$ can amplify small changes to values at the nodes. The problem remains **OPEN** in the maintained catalogue.

## A small example

Take three nodes at $-1,0,1$. Their cardinal polynomials are

$$

p_{-1}(x)=\frac{x(x-1)}2,\qquad

p_0(x)=1-x^2,\qquad

p_1(x)=\frac{x(x+1)}2.

$$

At $x=1/2$ the values are $-1/8,3/4,3/8$. They sum to one, yet the sum of their absolute values is

$$

\Lambda_3(1/2)=\frac18+\frac34+\frac38=\frac54.

$$

For the quadratic function $f(x)=x^2$, interpolation is exact because a degree-two polynomial is reproduced by the three-node interpolant. Thus $L_3f(1/2)=1/4=f(1/2)$ even though $\Lambda_3(1/2)>1$.

This finite calculation illustrates that numerical instability and exact reproduction of a particular function can coexist. It does **not** exhibit divergence of $\Lambda_n$ or solve the infinite-sequence question.

## What is known

The **first** question asks whether one can choose the entire node array so that, for **every** continuous function $f$, there is a point $x$ (allowed to depend on $f$) satisfying **both**

$$

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_n(x)=\infty

\quad\text{and}\quad

L_nf(x)\longrightarrow f(x).

$$

The **second** asks whether some array can have $\limsup_n\Lambda_n(x)=\infty$ at **every** point of the interval, and still, for **every** continuous function $f$, have **some** point $x$ at which its interpolants converge to $f(x)$. These two orders of quantifiers must not be interchanged.

The maintained record attributes to Bernstein [Be31] the older theorem that, no matter what nodes are chosen, there is at least one point where the interpolation Lebesgue functions become unbounded along a subsequence. It attributes to Erdős and Vértesi [ErVe80] the existence, for every node array, of a **continuous function** whose interpolation diverges in magnitude almost everywhere. Neither attribution alone answers the simultaneous existence of a point where a specified function converges and the Lebesgue function diverges.

There is also a useful functional-analytic warning. For any two continuous functions,

$$

|L_nf(x)-L_ng(x)|\leq\Lambda_n(x)\|f-g\|_\infty.

$$

If the operator norms $\Lambda_n(x)$ are unbounded at one **fixed** $x$, the uniform boundedness principle gives a continuous function with unbounded interpolation values at that particular point. This does not rule out the problem's possibility of finding a *different good point for each function*. The quantifier distinction is the heart of the question.

## What remains

For a bounded mathematical successor, fix one explicit triangular array of interpolation nodes and investigate two sets:

$$

D=\{x:\limsup_n\Lambda_n(x)=\infty\},\qquad

C_f=\{x:L_nf(x)\to f(x)\}.

$$

The first problem asks whether it is possible to choose nodes with $D\cap C_f\neq\varnothing$ for every continuous $f$. The second strengthens the condition to $D=[-1,1]$ but still permits the convergence point to depend on $f$.

Any use of Baire category or uniform boundedness must preserve the order of quantifiers. Finite-degree experiments are illustrations and potential checks on an intermediate lemma, never a proof of the desired simultaneous infinite behavior.
