## The question in plain language

Choose a graph uniformly at random among all simple graphs with $n$ labelled vertices and exactly $\lfloor cn\rfloor$ edges, where $c$ is a fixed real number greater than $1/2$. Does the graph contain a **simple path** visiting a positive proportion of all vertices with probability tending to one as $n$ increases?

Erdős asked more precisely for a function $f(c)$ such that a path with at least $f(c)n$ edges exists **with high probability**, with $f(c)$ approaching zero as $c$ decreases toward $1/2$, and approaching one as $c$ increases without bound. A simple path visits distinct vertices and never repeats them. A path with $L$ edges has $L+1$ vertices; changing between these conventions affects a finite count but not the limiting fraction.

This is [Erdős Problem #900](https://www.erdosproblems.com/900), settled affirmatively by Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi. The key quantifier is **with high probability**. It is false that *every* graph having more than $n/2$ edges must contain a path through a linear proportion of its vertices.

## A small example

For a graph with vertices $1,\ldots,n$, let $L(G)$ denote its greatest possible number of vertices in a simple path. If an edge is added, $L(G)$ cannot decrease: every old simple path still exists. The exact finite verifier enumerates all graphs on $n=3,4,5$ vertices, recording $L(G)$ for every possible edge count. This totals

$$
2^{\binom32}+2^{\binom42}+2^{\binom52}
=8+64+1024=1096
$$

different labelled graphs.

For a counterexample to a **deterministic** reading, take the star with one central vertex connected to all others. It has $n-1$ edges, which exceeds $n/2$ for $n>2$, but its longest simple path has only **three vertices**, independent of $n$.

A uniformly random graph with $n-1$ edges almost never resembles a star; the random-graph theorem is a statement about the whole distribution. These elementary examples illustrate why edge density alone cannot replace probabilistic typicality.

## What is known

Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi, [*The longest path in a random graph*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579172), *Combinatorica* **1** (1981), pp.1–12, proved the existence of a linear-length path with high probability in a uniformly random graph containing $(1+\varepsilon)n/2$ edges, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$. Their published abstract also treats random directed graphs, requiring $(1+\varepsilon)n$ directed edges for a linear directed path. The directed and undirected thresholds should not be interchanged.

The authors' university-hosted [publication record](https://www.researchwithrutgers.org/en/publications/the-longest-path-in-a-random-graph/) independently confirms the title, full author list, journal, pages and theorem description. A [Rutgers open-access record](https://scholarship.libraries.rutgers.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-longest-path-in-a-random/991031550002004646) lists the version of record as a PDF. The complete twelve-page proof was **not** independently rederived or formally certified by GCL in this task.

The [maintained Erdős Problem #900](https://www.erdosproblems.com/900) records the more detailed boundary requirements

$$
\lim_{c\downarrow1/2}f(c)=0,
\qquad
\lim_{c\to\infty}f(c)=1.
$$

It cites Erdős [Er78, p.32] and [Er82e], noting that Erdős expected a linear path while Szemerédi originally expressed skepticism. The catalogue marks the problem **PROVED** and records no formalized statement. This historical difference of opinion preceded the joint 1981 theorem.

The $n/2$ transition also has a graph-theoretic explanation: in the sparse random graph regime, the emergence of a giant connected component occurs at average degree one, corresponding to approximately $n/2$ edges. A giant component is a prerequisite for a linear simple path but does not itself automatically supply such a path; Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi prove the stronger property.

## GCL's current state

A finite graph can be represented by a binary variable for each unordered vertex pair. Fix the number $m$ of selected edges. The uniform random graph $G(n,m)$ gives each of the

$$
\binom{\binom n2}{m}
$$

edge sets equal probability. This is different from $G(n,p)$, where edges are included independently and the edge count is random. For fixed $c$, the parameter $m=\lfloor cn\rfloor$ must also satisfy $m\le\binom n2$, which holds for all sufficiently large $n$.

The exact program at governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_900_902.py computes every graph on three, four and five vertices. For each graph, dynamic programming over subsets of vertices identifies all possible endpoints of simple paths, then extracts the largest visited-vertex count. It groups the results by $m$ and verifies that each group contains exactly the expected number of graphs.

It independently checks the monotonicity of longest-path length when each missing edge is added. That monotonicity is elementary and holds for all graphs, but the enumerated finite probability tables cannot establish a high-probability limit as $n\to\infty$, nor the two limits of the unknown function $f(c)$.

## What remains

A bounded reusable formal theorem is the **finite path encoding and edge-monotonicity lemma**. Define $L(G)$ by existence of a sequence of pairwise distinct vertices with consecutive pairs adjacent. Prove that adding edges cannot reduce $L(G)$ and that the number of labelled $G(n,m)$ graphs is $\binom{\binom n2}{m}$.

Acceptance is a complete proof or pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem with actual axiom output, exact agreement with all 1,096 exhaustive small graphs, and explicit separation between path length measured in edges versus vertices. A repeated vertex in a reported path, or a graph for which adding an edge reduces $L(G)$, falsifies the interface.

The genuinely asymptotic successor is an independent reconstruction of the 1981 probabilistic argument, beginning with its exact event, quantifiers, constants and directed/undirected distinction. The primary theorem may be cited as established mathematics, but its full proof is not independently replayed by the finite fixture.
