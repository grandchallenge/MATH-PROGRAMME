**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #541 is **PROVED (LEAN reported externally)**. R. L. Graham asked whether a sequence of $p$ residues modulo a prime $p$, whose nonempty zero-sum subsequences all have **one fixed length**, can use more than two distinct residue values. Erdős and Szemerédi proved the conclusion for sufficiently large primes, and Gao, Hamidoune and Wang established the stronger result for **all positive integer moduli** in 2010.

## The question in plain language

Let $n\ge1$, and list exactly $n$ residues modulo $n$:

$$
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z.
$$

The entries may **repeat**. What matters when selecting a subsequence is which **index positions** are chosen.

For a nonempty subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, call $S$ a zero-sum subsequence when

$$
\sum_{i\in S}a_i\equiv0\pmod n.
$$

Suppose there is some integer $r$ such that **every** such nonempty zero-sum subsequence has exactly $r$ positions. Must the original sequence have at most **two distinct residue values**?

The answer is yes:

$$
\boxed{
\left|\left\{a_i:1\le i\le n\right\}\right|\le2.
}
$$

The fixed $r$ concerns only those index subsets whose sums are zero. It does **not** say that every nonempty subsequence has the same length.

This differs from #540. In #540 the starting collection is a **set of distinct residues** and one asks for existence of a zero-sum subset. Here the collection is an indexed **sequence that can repeat residues**, and the hypothesis restricts the lengths of *all* its nonempty zero-sum subsequences.

## A small example

For any modulus $n\ge2$, take $n$ copies of the residue $1$:

$$
a=(1,1,\ldots,1).
$$

A subsequence with $k$ entries has sum $k$ modulo $n$. For $1\le k\le n$, that sum vanishes only when $k=n$. Thus all nonempty zero-sum subsequences have the **same length $n$**, and the sequence uses only one value.

A two-value example is

$$
a=(0,1,\ldots,1)
$$

with one zero and $n-1$ ones. Its only nonempty zero-sum subsequence is the singleton containing the zero: a subsequence containing $k$ copies of one has sum $k$ with $0\le k<n$, and it vanishes only when $k=0$. Thus all zero-sum subsequences have length one, and there are exactly **two values**.

Repeated occurrences of a residue still create different index-subset choices. For example, in the length-four sequence $(1,1,2,2)$ modulo four, the pairs consisting of the two ones or two twos have different sums, even though the underlying set of distinct values has only two elements. Treating the input as an ordinary set would discard multiplicities and distort the theorem.

There is also always **at least one** nonempty zero-sum subsequence in a sequence of length $n$ modulo $n$. Take the $n$ prefix sums

$$
P_j=a_1+\cdots+a_j\pmod n,\qquad1\le j\le n.
$$

If one equals zero, that prefix works. Otherwise all $n$ prefix sums are among the $n-1$ nonzero residues, so two coincide. Their difference yields a nonempty **consecutive** index block summing to zero. Consequently the assumption about one possible length is not vacuous.

## What is known

### Graham's original question and Erdős–Szemerédi's first proof

In *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, Chapter 12 of *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), [printed p.126](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=10), Erdős poses the problem as one of two questions due to Graham. The **original scanned page was visually inspected**. The $p$ indexed entries are explicitly **not necessarily distinct**, and the conclusion concerns the number of *different residue classes* occurring among them.

P. Erdős and E. Szemerédi, *On a problem of Graham*, *Publicationes Mathematicae Debrecen* **23** (1976), 123–127, proved the assertion for **sufficiently large prime** moduli. That qualifier matters: a theorem only for all large primes leaves small primes and composite moduli outside its stated scope. The complete original 1976 proof was not obtained in this audit, but its exact hypotheses are quoted in the later primary paper.

### Gao, Hamidoune and Wang's 2010 resolution for all moduli

Weidong Gao, Yahya Ould Hamidoune and Guoqing Wang, *Distinct Length Modular Zero-sum Subsequences: A Proof of Graham's Conjecture*, *Journal of Number Theory* **130** (2010), 1425–1431, [DOI 10.1016/j.jnt.2009.11.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2009.11.012), proved the universal theorem.

Their [complete author preprint, arXiv:0902.4758](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0902.4758), was downloaded and **Theorem 1.1 on PDF p.2 was visually examined**. In its precise terms, for every positive integer $n$ and every sequence of $n$ integers from $[0,n-1]$, if all nonempty zero-sum subsequences modulo $n$ have the same length, then the sequence contains at most two distinct values.

This immediately answers Graham's prime-modulus conjecture and extends it to **every composite modulus**. The source uses a subsequence of a sequence, allowing repeated values at distinct positions: it is exactly the indexed-subset interpretation above.

The original proof divides into two cases depending on whether the common zero-sum length $r$ is at least $n/2$ or less than $n/2$.

In the large‑$r$ case, the proof uses a lemma giving a zero-sum subsequence whose length is at most the greatest multiplicity of any one value. This constrains how often a repeated value can appear, and eventually forces incompatibility between three distinct residues and unique zero-sum length.

In the small‑$r$ case, deleting a zero-sum subsequence leaves a long zero-sum-free sequence. A structural theorem about long zero-sum-free sequences, attributed to Savchev–Chen and Yuan, then enables the authors to derive two zero-sum subsequences of **different lengths**.

The complete proof was examined for this structure, but has **not** been reproduced as a separate GCL mathematical certification. The central conclusion and quantified modulus range are directly supported by the original published result.

### The formal proof and its different historical route

The pinned [Formal Conjectures #541 source](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/541.lean), Git blob 7fdcd8599d91f9b78149b59a3e62f61cf575d46f, was directly inspected. Its main theorem is framed for **prime $p$**, an index function from a finite $p$‑element set into residues modulo $p$, and one permissible length for every nonempty zero-sum indexed subset. It also includes separate variants for all moduli and sufficiently large primes.

The mathematical answer is marked affirmative, but the research theorem bodies in the **pinned Formal Conjectures file** are **proof placeholders**. They should not be reported as the site of a completed protected Lean proof.

The source links to a separately developed [Lean 4.24.0 formalization](https://github.com/plby/lean-proofs/blob/main/src/v4.24.0/ErdosProblems/Erdos541.lean). That external file was directly fetched. Its introductory notes state that the proof follows an approach closer to David J. Grynkiewicz's *Note on a Conjecture of Graham*, [arXiv:0903.3200](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.3200), than to the Gao–Hamidoune–Wang paper.

This difference in proof route does not automatically mean the theorem is different; it is a **provenance distinction**. The code works with multisets, bounds on maximum multiplicity, partitioning and additive sumsets, then specializes to the problem's indexed residue sequence.

It also verifies the non-vacuity lemma and the one- and two-value examples. Such finite enumeration is source QA, not the proof that the assertion holds for every modulus.
