## The question in plain language

A **covering system** is a finite list of congruences

$$
n\equiv a_i\pmod {m_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,k,
$$

such that **every** integer $n$ satisfies at least one congruence. The modulus $m_i$ is the step between integers in the corresponding progression. In a *distinct* covering system the moduli $m_i$ are pairwise different.

[Erdős Problem #7](https://www.erdosproblems.com/7), associated with Erdős and Selfridge, asks:

> Can all the moduli in a distinct covering system be odd integers greater than $1$?

Precisely, does there exist a positive integer $k$, pairwise distinct odd integers $m_1,\ldots,m_k\geq3$, and residues $a_i$ such that

$$
\forall n\in\mathbb Z\;\exists i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:
       n\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}\,?
$$

**This existence question remains open** in the checked [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/7) and the available 2026 literature. It does **not** require the moduli to be square-free. That extra restriction defines an important *stronger* problem for which nonexistence has been proved.

The catalogue marks this question **VERIFIABLE**, meaning *an affirmative answer could be witnessed by a finite, exactly checkable list*. It does not mean that a witness exists, that exhaustive searches settle the negative answer, or that any logical independence/undecidability theorem has been established.

## A small example

Consider the three **distinct odd moduli** $3,5,7$, with residue classes

$$
n\equiv0\pmod3,\qquad
n\equiv1\pmod5,\qquad
n\equiv2\pmod7.
$$

These **do not** cover all integers: for instance $n=4$ belongs to none of them. In fact, the entire configuration repeats every

$$
L=\mathrm{lcm}(3,5,7)=105.
$$

An independent integer enumeration of $n=0,1,\ldots,104$ gives **57 covered and 48 uncovered** residues.

There is also a short proof of the exact number 48, without relying on enumeration. Because $3,5,7$ are pairwise coprime, the Chinese remainder theorem identifies a residue modulo $105$ with one choice of residue modulo each of $3,5,7$. To be *uncovered*, we must avoid one prescribed residue for each modulus. Hence there are exactly

$$
(3-1)(5-1)(7-1)=48
$$

uncovered classes, **whatever three residues are chosen**.

This does not exclude all possible odd covering systems: distinct odd moduli need not be pairwise coprime.

For comparison, an actual covering system with **distinct but not all odd** moduli is

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
m_i&2&3&4&6&12\\
a_i&0&0&1&5&7
\end{array}.
$$

Checking the 12 residues modulo $12$ finds **zero uncovered**: the class modulo $2$ covers all even residues; the remaining odd residues $1,3,5,7,9,11$ are covered respectively by moduli $4,3,4,12,3,6$. This positive control illustrates both what a finite certificate looks like and why the *odd-moduli* qualification cannot be dropped.

More generally, for any proposed finite list let $L=\mathrm{lcm}(m_1,\ldots,m_k)$. Since coverage is periodic with period $L$, the list covers **every integer if and only if** it covers all $L$ residue classes $0,\ldots,L-1$. A candidate **yes** certificate is therefore decidable by finite arithmetic. There is no known global bound on $k$ or $L$ making failure to find a certificate a proof of **no**.

## What is known

- **Necessary divisibility, not existence.** Hough and Nielsen, *Covering systems with restricted divisibility*, *Duke Mathematical Journal* **168** (2019), 3261–3295, prove that every distinct covering system has at least one modulus divisible by $2$ or $3$. Therefore a hypothetical all-odd system must include a modulus divisible by $3$. It remains hypothetical.
- **The square-free subclass is impossible.** Balister, Bollobás, Morris, Sahasrabudhe and Tiba, *The Erdős–Selfridge problem with square-free moduli*, *Algebra & Number Theory* **15** (2021), 609–626, rule out a covering whose moduli are simultaneously odd, square-free and distinct. The proof does **not** eliminate odd moduli containing a repeated prime factor.
- **Further restrictions on a hypothetical solution.** Balister and collaborators, *On the Erdős covering problem: the density of the uncovered set*, *Inventiones Mathematicae* **228** (2022), 377–414, provide additional covering-system estimates. **Theorem 1.4 (page 4) of the [authors' accessible preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.03547)** states that for a distinct covering system with $L=\mathrm{lcm}(m_1,\ldots,m_k)$, one has $2\mid L$, $9\mid L$, or $15\mid L$. For an all-odd system this reduces to the necessary condition $9\mid L$ or $15\mid L$. Importantly, $15\mid L$ **does not imply that any single modulus is divisible by 15**: the paper explicitly warns against that inference. This establishes a constraint, not the existence of such a covering.
- **Finite formalized exclusions.** Mian and Siddique's July 2026 preprint, [*Kernel-Checked Exclusions for the Erdős–Selfridge Odd Covering Problem*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25628), reports a Lean-checked exclusion of candidates whose least common multiple is at most $10{,}000$. This is the **authors' reported formalization**, not a kernel replay independently performed here and not a resolution of the unbounded existence question.
- **Changing the hypotheses changes the question.** Bispels, Cohen, Harrington, Lowrance, Pontes, Schaumann and Wong, *A further investigation on covering systems with odd moduli*, *Discrete Mathematics* **349** (2026), article 115013, study a variant permitting one odd modulus to occur more than once. That is not the original **distinct-moduli** question.

These statements have different logical strengths. A theorem about every covering system imposes a condition; a theorem about all square-free systems eliminates only a subclass; and a finite-period exclusion rules out only bounded periods. None of these steps by itself answers the unrestricted odd-covering question.

## What remains

The central yes/no question is still:

- **Yes route:** exhibit finitely many *pairwise distinct odd* moduli $>1$ and corresponding residues, then verify all residues modulo their least common multiple.
- **No route:** prove that **every** such finite candidate leaves some integer uncovered, without assuming an unproved bound on the number or size of the moduli.

The square-free result shows that any hypothetical example must exploit nonsquare-free structure. Restrictions such as divisibility by $3$, the **Theorem 1.4** necessary condition that $9$ or $15$ divide the least common multiple, and finite formalized exclusions narrow possibilities but do not remove the unbounded search space.
