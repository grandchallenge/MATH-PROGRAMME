## The question in plain language

Take any infinite sequence of points $z_1,z_2,\ldots$ in the unit interval. For an interval $I\subseteq[0,1]$, compare the number of the first $N$ points that fall in $I$ with the idealized number $N|I|$, where $|I|$ is the interval's length. The discrepancy is

$$
D_N(I)=\#\{n\leq N:z_n\in I\}-N|I|.
$$

Erdős Problem 255 asks whether

$$
\forall (z_n)_{n\geq1}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb N}\;\exists\, I\subseteq[0,1]:\quad \limsup_{N\to\infty}|D_N(I)|=\infty.
$$

The interval must be chosen once, after seeing the whole sequence. This is stronger than saying that for every $N$ there is some convenient interval $I_N$; an interval that changes with $N$ would not answer the problem. A half-open convention, such as $I=[0,x)$, is useful because it fixes what happens when a point lies at an endpoint.

Erdős stated the original version on the unit circle: for every sequence of points on the circle, is there a fixed arc whose discrepancy is unbounded? In “Some unsolved problems” (1961), Problem (1.21.6), printed p. 234, he defined discrepancy using arcs and asked exactly this fixed-arc question. A non-wrapping half-open arc becomes a half-open interval after identifying the circle with $[0,1)$. A wrapping arc can be replaced by its complementary arc: with compatible half-open endpoints, the two discrepancies are negatives of one another. That normalization is elementary, but its endpoint convention must be stated in a formal treatment.

## A small example

Consider the first eight terms of the van der Corput sequence in base $2$:

$$
0,\;\frac12,\;\frac14,\;\frac34,\;\frac18,\;\frac58,\;\frac38,\;\frac78.
$$

For the fixed interval $I=[0,1/2)$, exactly every other displayed point is counted. Thus the exact finite values are

$$
D_N(I)=\frac12,0,\frac12,0,\frac12,0,\frac12,0\qquad(1\leq N\leq8).
$$

The arithmetic is reproducible with only rational numbers:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
z = [Fraction(0), Fraction(1,2), Fraction(1,4), Fraction(3,4),
     Fraction(1,8), Fraction(5,8), Fraction(3,8), Fraction(7,8)]
half = Fraction(1, 2)
print([sum(x < half for x in z[:N]) - N*half for N in range(1, 9)])
```

It prints `[Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(0, 1)]`. This is a finite check of the definition, not evidence that this interval has bounded discrepancy forever and not a proof of Problem 255. It illustrates why a long numerical prefix cannot decide a statement involving a fixed interval and infinitely many $N$.

## What is known

The original problem is classically solved in the affirmative. W. M. Schmidt, “Irregularities of distribution,” *Quarterly Journal of Mathematics* 19 (1968), 181–191, DOI [10.1093/qmath/19.1.181](https://doi.org/10.1093/qmath/19.1.181), is the proof credited by the maintained problem page. The publisher record confirms the author, year, pages, and DOI, but the article's full text was not available in this audit. Consequently, no theorem number or page inside Schmidt 1968 is claimed here.

An accessible source gives a precise usable interface. In Wolfgang M. Schmidt, “Irregularities of distribution. VI,” *Compositio Mathematica* 24 (1972), 63–74, the introduction on printed pp. 63–64 defines the endpoint discrepancy and, for $\kappa\geq0$, the set $S(\kappa)=\{x:D_N([0,x))\text{ has absolute value at most }\kappa\text{ for every }N\}$, with $S(\infty)=\bigcup_{\kappa\geq0}S(\kappa)$. Schmidt's Theorem on printed p. 64 says that for every derivative order $d>4\kappa$, $S^{(d)}(\kappa)=\varnothing$; its Corollary, also p. 64, says that each $S(\kappa)$, and in particular $S(\infty)$, is at most countable. Since $(0,1)$ is uncountable, some endpoint $x$ has unbounded discrepancy for $[0,x)$—indeed, all but countably many endpoints do. This is a borrowed deep theorem of Schmidt, not an original proof supplied by GCL.

Schmidt 1972 also records the distinction between endpoint and arbitrary-interval statements. For the sequence of fractional parts of multiples of an irrational number, it notes that the bounded-discrepancy endpoint set is countable, while continuum many intervals can nevertheless have bounded discrepancy because their lengths have a special form. Thus “almost every endpoint works” and “every interval works” are very different claims. R. Tijdeman and G. Wagner, “A sequence has almost nowhere small discrepancy,” *Monatshefte für Mathematik* 90 (1980), 315–329, DOI [10.1007/BF01540851](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01540851), give the quantitative strengthening recorded in the source audit and compatible with the maintained page's $\gg 1$ formulation: for every sequence, for almost every $x\in[0,1)$,

$$
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|D_N([0,x))|}{\log N}\geq\frac1{400}.
$$

The constant and almost-everywhere theorem are reported from that paper and the maintained page; they are not independently reproved here. Erdős’s later discussion, “Problems and results on diophantine approximations” (1964), *Compositio Mathematica* 16, 52–65, pp. 53–54, revisits fixed-interval discrepancy questions and is useful historical context, not a replacement for Schmidt’s proof.

## What remains

There is no open original yes/no problem to solve: Schmidt's classical result answers it affirmatively. The remaining work is exactness and documentary reproducibility. A formalization must choose the ambient space, interval type, endpoint convention, indexing of the sequence, and the meaning of boundedness before translating the circle statement. It must then formalize the implication

$$
\text{the bounded-discrepancy endpoint set is countable}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\exists x\in(0,1)\text{ with }\limsup_N|D_N([0,x))|=\infty.
$$

That implication is elementary once Schmidt's countability theorem is available; Schmidt's theorem itself is the deep borrowed lemma. A checked formal proof would still need an actual statement file, source-semantic comparison, and kernel replay in its pinned toolchain. The catalogue's `formal_status: Lean` field, an external Lean pointer, or a reported formal proof would not by itself discharge any of those requirements.

The 1968 primary article remains source-limited here: its exact theorem numbering and proof pages are not asserted. The 1972 paper supplies an accessible theorem and page for the countability route, but it does not retroactively create a GCL formalization. Endpoint and circle-to-line equivalence should be recorded as explicit lemmas rather than silently assumed.
