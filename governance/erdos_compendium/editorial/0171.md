## The question in plain language

For an alphabet of $t$ symbols, consider all words of length $N$, namely $[t]^N$. A *combinatorial line* is a collection of $t$ words formed by fixing some coordinates and replacing every remaining coordinate by a common variable that takes values $1,\ldots,t$.

The **density Hales–Jewett theorem** asserts that, for every fixed $t\ge1$ and density $\varepsilon>0$, every sufficiently high-dimensional set $A\subseteq[t]^N$ with at least $\varepsilon t^N$ words contains a complete combinatorial line. It is a statement about *positive density*, not merely one colour class in a finite colouring.

## A small example

In $[3]^2$, the three words $11$, $22$, $33$ form a combinatorial line: both positions vary together. So do $12$, $22$, $32$, because the second position is fixed while the first varies. The words $11$, $23$, $32$ do not form such a line because the two coordinates do not share a common pattern of being fixed or varying.

**Statement-fidelity warning:** the displayed maintained problem page says “for each coordinate $1\le j\le t$,” but the vectors have **$N$ coordinates**. The quantifier must run over $j=1,\ldots,N$ for the standard theorem. This is a minor indexing transcription error, not a new conjecture.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Furstenberg and Katznelson established the density theorem in 1991 using ergodic methods. The D. H. J. Polymath collaboration published [*A new proof of the density Hales–Jewett theorem*](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2012.175.3.6), *Annals of Mathematics* 175 (2012), 1283–1327, providing an elementary combinatorial proof and quantitative bounds.

For alphabet size three, the Polymath paper gives a quantitative sufficient dimension bounded by a tower of twos whose height is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$. The quantitative height is not a universal claim of polynomial dependence on $1/\varepsilon$.

This is stronger than ordinary Hales–Jewett, where any finite colouring of a sufficiently high-dimensional cube forces a monochromatic line; the density theorem forces one **inside every fixed-positive-density subset**.

## What remains

A bounded semantics task enumerates all combinatorial lines in $[3]^2$ and $[3]^3$, classifies each coordinate as fixed or varying, and compares this enumeration with the corrected $N$ coordinate definition. Acceptance: the diagonal and one-coordinate-variable examples above are admitted, invalid mixed-coordinate cases rejected, and no numerical experiment is confused with the infinite-dimensional density theorem.
