## The question in plain language

The gap between consecutive primes is irregular: sometimes two primes occur unusually close together, and sometimes a long stretch contains none. Let $p_n$ be the prime of index $n$ and write

$$
g_n=\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log n}\qquad(n\ge2).
$$

[Erdős Problem #5](https://www.erdosproblems.com/5) asks whether, for **each prescribed real number** $C\ge0$, there are infinitely many increasing prime indices along which $g_n$ approaches $C$. Equivalently, does every nonnegative real number occur as a finite limit point of the normalized prime-gap sequence? This problem is open in the reviewed 2026 sources.

## A small example

The consecutive primes $5,7,11$ have successive gaps 2 and 4. With indices $n=3$ and $n=4$, the corresponding normalized values are $2/\log3$ and $4/\log4$. A few such values demonstrate the definition but tell us nothing about limit points requiring arbitrarily large indices.

There is a useful normalization equivalence. The prime number theorem implies

$$
\frac{\log p_n}{\log n}\longrightarrow1.
$$

Hence dividing the prime gap by $\log p_n$ instead of by $\log n$ yields exactly the same **finite** subsequential limits. This elementary equivalence depends on the prime number theorem; it does not prove the limit-point conjecture.

## What is known

Classical results establish extremely large normalized gaps (Westzynthius) and zero as a limit point (Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım). Later results identify substantial parts of the spectrum. In particular Jori Merikoski, *Limit points of normalized prime gaps*, *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* **102** (2020), 99–124, proves that the set $S$ of finite limit points has

$$
\mathrm{meas}(S\cap[0,T])\ge \frac{T}{3}
\qquad(T\ge0),
$$

and that gaps between finite limit points are bounded by an absolute constant. This is a theorem about **at least one third of each interval by measure**, not a theorem that every nonnegative $C$ lies in $S$.

## What remains

Show every prescribed finite $C\ge0$ is attained as a limit along an unbounded sequence of consecutive-prime indices, or find a missing value. Positive measure, a small initial interval and bounded gaps between limit points do not imply that all possible values occur.
