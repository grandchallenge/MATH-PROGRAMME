## The question in plain language

Euler's totient function $\phi(m)$ counts the integers from $1$ through $m$ that share no common factor with $m$ other than $1$. Its image is the set of totients. Let $V(x)$ be the number of distinct totient values that are at most $x$; equivalently, it counts the positive integers $n\le x$ for which the equation $\phi(m)=n$ has at least one solution $m$.

Erdős Problem #416 asks two related questions. First, does

$$
\frac{V(2x)}{V(x)}\longrightarrow 2
$$

as $x$ tends to infinity? Second, is there an asymptotic formula for $V(x)$, meaning a formula whose ratio to $V(x)$ tends to $1$? The first question would follow from a sufficiently regular asymptotic formula, but a good estimate up to a bounded multiplicative factor is not enough.

The distinction between distinct values and preimages matters. If many different $m$ have the same value of $\phi(m)$, they contribute only once to $V(x)$. The problem is therefore about the shape of the image of $\phi$, not about how many integers map into it.

## A small example

The finite calculation behind the definition is concrete. For $m=15$, the integers in $\{1,\ldots,15\}$ coprime to $15$ are

$$
1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14,
$$

so $\phi(15)=8$. Thus $8$ is counted by $V(10)$. Exact evaluation also gives the witnesses $\phi(1)=1$, $\phi(3)=2$, $\phi(5)=4$, $\phi(7)=6$, $\phi(15)=8$, and $\phi(11)=10$.

As a reproducibility check, direct integer enumeration of $1\le m\le200$, using $\phi(m)=\#\{a:1\le a\le m,\gcd(a,m)=1\}$, returns the distinct values at most $10$ as

$$
\{1,2,4,6,8,10\}.
$$

This verifies a finite set of exact counts and witnesses; it does not by itself prove that no larger $m$ supplies another value at most $10$, and it says nothing about the limiting ratio. Finite enumeration can test implementations and small cases, but it cannot decide a statement quantified over all sufficiently large real $x$.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems #416 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/416) records the problem as open and says that it cannot be resolved by finite computation. It reports that Pillai proved $V(x)=o(x)$ and that Erdős obtained $V(x)=x(\log x)^{-1+o(1)}$. Those historical claims are also discussed in the primary literature cited below.

Maier and Pomerance proved a much sharper scale in *On the number of distinct values of Euler's $\phi$ function*, Acta Arithmetica 49 (1988), 263–275. Their theorem, stated on p. 264, is

$$
V(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}\exp\!\left\{(C+o(1))(\log\log\log x)^2\right\},
$$

with an explicit positive constant $C$. This determines the principal growth scale but leaves a substantial subleading factor unresolved.

Kevin Ford's primary paper, *The distribution of totients*, Ramanujan Journal 2 (1998), 67–151, gives a more precise result. Theorem 1 on p. 68, also available in the corrected [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3264), states that

$$
V(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}\exp\!\left\{C(\log_3x-\log_4x)^2+D\log_3x-(D+1/2-2C)\log_4x+O(1)\right\}.
$$

Here $\log_j$ denotes the iterated logarithm applied $j$ times, and Ford gives explicit constants $C$ and $D$. The theorem is a two-sided order statement because the exponent has an $O(1)$ remainder. In the introduction, Ford explicitly notes that his method falls short of proving $V(cx)\sim cV(x)$ for fixed $c>1$; this includes the question with $c=2$.

## What remains

The central unresolved target is sharper than the already-known order of magnitude: either prove an asymptotic formula with a sufficiently controlled remainder to imply $V(2x)/V(x)\to2$, or find a genuine obstruction to that ratio. A bounded-factor estimate, including Ford's theorem, does not decide it.

A bounded successor task should first normalize the analytic target. It can extract from Ford's Theorem 1 the exact constants, domains, and meaning of the $O(1)$ term, then prove a conditional implication: a specified remainder bound on the exponent would imply the ratio limit. The acceptance test is symbolic and quantitative, with every uniformity assumption written explicitly. Falsification is a counterexample to that implication's hypotheses or a source mismatch. It should stop after the source comparison and implication check, without attempting a full analytic proof or a Lean build.
