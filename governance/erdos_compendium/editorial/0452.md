## The question in plain language

Most large positive integers have roughly $\log\log n$ distinct prime factors. Erdős Problem #452 asks how long a **consecutive run** of integers can be if *every number in the run* has strictly more distinct prime factors than that benchmark.

Write $\omega(n)$ for the number of distinct primes dividing $n$. Thus $\omega(12)=2$, because $12=2^2\cdot3$ contains only two distinct primes, even though one of them appears twice.

For large $x$, let $L(x)$ be the greatest number of consecutive integers in $[x,2x]$ such that

$$
\omega(n)>\log\log n
$$

at **every** position in the run. The logarithms are natural. The question is to estimate the maximum length $L(x)$ as $x$ grows, not merely to find an occasional qualifying integer.

The distinction between typical behavior and unusually long runs is important. Even knowing the proportion of integers satisfying a property does not tell us how its successful occurrences can cluster.

## A small example

Take three consecutive integers of the form

$$
a+1,\quad a+2,\quad a+3,
$$

and force each to have at least four *different* prime factors. We can do this with the Chinese remainder theorem (CRT), which lets us prescribe congruences modulo pairwise coprime integers.

Use four primes for each position:

| Position | Required distinct prime factors | Their product |
|---|---|---:|
| $a+1$ | $5,7,11,13$ | $5005$ |
| $a+2$ | $17,19,23,29$ | $215441$ |
| $a+3$ | $31,37,41,43$ | $2022161$ |

The three moduli are pairwise coprime. We impose

$$
\begin{aligned}
a+1&\equiv0\pmod{5005},\\
a+2&\equiv0\pmod{215441},\\
a+3&\equiv0\pmod{2022161}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The CRT gives a unique residue class modulo their product

$$
Q=2180460221945005.
$$

One representative is

$$
a=2099999228442114.
$$

Direct integer division verifies all three congruences. Each number $a+1,a+2,a+3$ therefore has **at least four distinct prime factors**. Moreover,

$$
\log\log(a+3)\approx3.5634<4.
$$

Consequently all three consecutive integers satisfy the strict inequality. This is a completely reproducible construction, but a fixed three-element run cannot establish the growth rate of $L(x)$.

## What is known

In his 1937 paper *Note on the Number of Prime Divisors of Integers*, Erdős proved that the integers satisfying

$$
\omega(n)>\log\log n
$$

have natural density $1/2$. Thus, among the first $X$ positive integers, the count is asymptotic to $X/2$. This is a statement about the **overall proportion** of qualifying integers, not their longest consecutive run.

Erdős and Graham's 1980 collection records a further lower bound, deduced using the Chinese remainder theorem:

$$
\boxed{
L(x)\geq (1+o(1))\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^2}.
}
$$

Here the $o(1)$ term tends to zero as $x\to\infty$; more precisely, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $x$, there is a consecutive run of at least

$$
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^2}
$$

qualifying integers inside $[x,2x]$, up to integer rounding.

Why should CRT construct such a run? Choose a length

$$
\ell=\left\lfloor
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^2}
\right\rfloor
$$

and reserve

$$
m=\lceil\log\log(2x)\rceil+1
$$

different small primes for *each* of the $\ell$ positions. Assign each position its own $m$ primes. There are enough primes of size between $\ell$ and $(1+\varepsilon/4)\log x$ for sufficiently large $x$: the prime number theorem supplies approximately $(1+\varepsilon/4)\log x/\log\log x$ of them, whereas we need only $(1-\varepsilon+o(1))\log x/\log\log x$.

Let $Q$ be the product of all the assigned primes. Because there are $\ell m$ of them and each is at most $(1+\varepsilon/4)\log x$, elementary logarithmic estimates give

$$
\log Q
\leq \ell m\log\bigl((1+\varepsilon/4)\log x\bigr)
=(1-\varepsilon+o(1))\log x.
$$

Hence $Q<x^{1-\varepsilon/2}$ once $x$ is sufficiently large. The CRT gives a residue class $a\pmod Q$ so that every $a+j$ is divisible by its assigned primes for $1\leq j\leq\ell$. Since $Q=o(x)$, some representative of the residue class places the entire run inside $[x,2x]$. For every number in that run,

$$
\omega(a+j)\geq m>\log\log(a+j).
$$

This reconstructs the classical lower bound using the prime number theorem and the CRT, not a new result or a resolution of the maximum-length question.

The much harder open question is whether substantially longer runs exist. Erdős and Graham speculated that the maximum might reach $(\log x)^k$ for arbitrarily large fixed exponents $k$, a possibility rather than an established theorem. Neither the density‑$1/2$ result nor the CRT lower bound decides it.

## GCL's current state

GCL's authenticated [#452 source-audit return](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1722#issuecomment-6144051742) records the 1980 original problem, the 1937 theorem, the meaning of the extremal quantity, and an explicit CRT construction. The finite modular arithmetic was checked with exact integers, rather than decimal approximations.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #452](https://www.erdosproblems.com/452) reports the question as **open**. The older pinned formalization metadata and the maintained site's later metadata do not agree; neither one is proof certification. The bound given above is documentary reconstruction of established methods. The prime number theorem is used as an established external mathematical input, and no Lean replay is claimed.

## What remains

The main mathematical problem is to give stronger lower bounds or meaningful upper bounds for $L(x)$, with all assumptions on $x$, run endpoints, and the strict threshold stated exactly. The 1980 source explicitly says no useful upper bound was then known.

A bounded verification task would formalize the CRT construction, including the number of available primes, rounding of $\ell$ and $m$, the inequality $Q<x^{1-\varepsilon/2}$, and the placement of a whole residue-class block in $[x,2x]$. Those steps should be separately checked before any formal-certification claim.

A computation can examine moderate values of $x$, but no finite search can determine whether runs of length $(\log x)^k$ occur on arbitrarily large scales.
