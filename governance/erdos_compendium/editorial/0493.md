## The question in plain language

Given any sufficiently large integer $n$, can we choose a **fixed number** $k$ of integers $a_1,\ldots,a_k$, all at least $2$, such that

$$
n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_i-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i?
$$

The same $k$ must work for every sufficiently large $n$, although the individual $a_i$ are allowed to depend on $n$. Erdős recorded the question in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), p.238, attributing it to Andrzej Schinzel.

At first sight, a product minus a sum might appear arithmetically restrictive. But under the **stated** conditions there is a simple solution with $k=2$ that works for **every nonnegative integer** $n$, not just sufficiently large ones. The maintained [Erdős #493](https://www.erdosproblems.com/493) credits Eli Seamans with pointing out the identity.

The result is not a new unsolved Diophantine theorem. It is a source-fidelity lesson: a conjecture can look substantially harder if a possible extra historical restriction has been omitted, but one cannot silently add such a restriction after finding a counterintuitively simple proof.

## A small example

Fix $k=2$ and, given $n\ge0$, choose

$$
a_1=2,\qquad a_2=n+2.
$$

Both are integers at least $2$. Their product minus their sum is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_1a_2-(a_1+a_2)
&=2(n+2)-\bigl(2+(n+2)\bigr)\\
&=2n+4-n-4\\
&=n.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a **complete proof** of the literal positive answer. For example, $n=7$ is represented by $2\cdot9-(2+9)=18-11=7$, and $n=100$ by $2\cdot102-(2+102)=204-104=100$.

Even the endpoint $n=0$ works with $a_1=a_2=2$: $2\cdot2-(2+2)=0$. The published problem imposes **no distinctness condition** on the $a_i$. For every $n\ge1$, the two chosen factors are distinct anyway.

With $k=1$, the expression is always $a_1-a_1=0$, so it cannot represent any positive $n$. Thus $k=2$ is not only sufficient but is also **the smallest permissible fixed number of factors** for representing all sufficiently large positive integers.

## What is known

### The original statement

Erdős's 1961 collection, printed p.238, asks whether there exists a *single* $k$ for which all sufficiently large integers admit the indicated product-minus-sum form with each factor at least $2$. It attributes the problem to Schinzel but does **not** specify additional restrictions such as pairwise coprimality, primality, prescribed bounds or independence of the factors from the target integer.

The maintained catalogue classifies it as **PROVED (LEAN)** and publishes the two-factor identity above. This is the decisive mathematical mechanism. The catalogue nevertheless warns that Schinzel's original problem might have included some further condition that did not survive in Erdős's formulation. Without a primary source specifying such a condition, a separate stronger version must not be presented as the original theorem.

### Exact quantifiers and formal evidence

The pinned [FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/493.lean](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/493.lean), blob 7020bd4f35fd107a081b72a9b9eff4ad53505a73, encodes the existential statement

$$
\exists k\in\mathbb N\;\exists N\in\mathbb Z\;
\forall n\in\mathbb Z,\ n\ge N
\Longrightarrow
\exists (a_i)_{i=1}^{k},\quad
a_i\ge2,\quad
\prod_i a_i-\sum_i a_i=n.
$$

The explicit witness $k=2$, $N=0$, $a_1=2$, $a_2=n+2$ satisfies these conditions exactly.
