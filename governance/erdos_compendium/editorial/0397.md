## The question in plain language

Write $C(k)=\binom{2k}{k}$ for the central binomial coefficient. Erdős Problem 397 asks whether there are only finitely many equalities

$$
\prod_i C(m_i)=\prod_j C(n_j)
$$

when all the indices $m_i$ and $n_j$ are distinct. The original wording does not spell out a fixed number of factors, positivity convention, or whether “distinct” means distinct within each side or across both sides. The natural reading for the problem is a finite pair of disjoint lists of positive integers, with order irrelevant. The later formal encoding uses disjoint finite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$, which makes that interpretation precise but also admits $0$ and empty sets.

The source question is printed on page 77 of P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980). The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/397) gives the same question and records a negative answer. The exact GCL assignment and its source packet are in [issue #1667](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1667).

## A small example

The maintained entry gives a family. For every integer $a\geq 2$, set $c=8a^2+8a+1$. It claims

$$
C(a)C(2a+2)C(c)=C(a+1)C(2a)C(c+1).
$$

Take $a=2$, so $c=49$. The two index sets are $\{2,6,49\}$ and $\{3,4,50\}$; they are disjoint and both sums are $57$. Exact factorial arithmetic gives

$$
C(2)=6,\quad C(6)=924,\quad C(49)=25477612258980856902730428600,
$$

and

$$
C(3)=20,\quad C(4)=70,\quad C(50)=100891344545564193334812497256.
$$

Multiplication on both sides gives the identical integer

$$
141247882363789870668737496158400.
$$

This is reproducible with exact arithmetic using `comb(2*k,k)` from Python’s standard `math` library; no floating-point approximation is involved. It verifies one finite collision and shows why the original finiteness claim cannot survive if the displayed family holds for every $a$. It does not, by itself, prove the identity for all values of $a$, establish the intended domain convention, or certify the formal Lean theorem.

## What is known

The scanned primary source on page 77 records the problem as an open question about distinct indices. It is a source for the original statement, not evidence that the question was solved there. The maintained page, last edited 12 January 2026, attributes the negative answer to Neel Somani and says that further families occur in the discussion. Its formula is mathematically checkable without trusting a numerical approximation.

Indeed, the standard ratio

$$
\frac{C(t+1)}{C(t)}=\frac{2(2t+1)}{t+1}
$$

reduces the displayed identity to a polynomial identity. After cancellation, it is equivalent to

$$
(4a+1)(4a+3)(c+1)=2(2a+1)^2(2c+1),
$$

which holds exactly when $c=2(2a+1)^2-1=8a^2+8a+1$. Since $c$ strictly increases with $a$, the family supplies infinitely many different collisions under the disjoint-positive-index interpretation. This is an elementary verification of the claimed mechanism, not a claim that GCL has certified a theorem.

A 2013 [MathOverflow discussion](https://mathoverflow.net/questions/138209/product-of-central-binomial-coefficients) supplies an earlier explicit nontrivial collision and exploratory arguments. Those posts are useful corroboration and context, but they are not the original Erdős–Graham source and do not replace a named proof publication for the later family.

## What remains

The main documentary obligation is provenance. The original book gives the question, while the live entry gives the later family but attributes it to “Somani, using ChatGPT” without a conventional paper, theorem number, or proof manuscript. A bounded successor should preserve that attribution exactly, derive the family from the displayed ratio identity, and compare its hypotheses with the original page and the Lean encoding.

The formal route also needs a semantic check: decide whether $\mathbb{N}$ should be replaced by positive integers, whether empty sets and the index $0$ are admissible, and whether pairwise disjoint finite sets exactly represent “the $m_i,n_j$ distinct.” A formal proof of the elementary family or of infinitude would be useful, but a theorem ending in `sorry` remains a statement source, not a proof result.
