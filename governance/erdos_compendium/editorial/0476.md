## The question in plain language

Take $k$ different residues modulo a prime $p$. Add them in pairs, but never add a residue to itself. How many different answers must appear? Define

$$
A\widehat{+}A=\{a+b\pmod p:a,b\in A,\ a\ne b\}.
$$

The Erdős–Heilbronn inequality states that

$$
|A\widehat{+}A|\ge \min\{p,2|A|-3\}\qquad(|A|\ge2).
$$

The minimum with $p$ is necessary because there are only $p$ residue classes. For $|A|<2$, the restricted sumset is empty and the lower bound is interpreted as zero.

## A small example

For $p=11$ and $A=\{0,1,2,3\}$, the six unordered pairs give sums $1,2,3,3,4,5$, so

$$
A\widehat{+}A=\{1,2,3,4,5\},\qquad |A\widehat{+}A|=5=2(4)-3.
$$

The bound is sharp. For $p=7$ and $A=\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, the restricted sums cover every residue class. Exhaustive integer-arithmetic checks accompanying this manuscript verify every subset of $\mathbb F_p$ for primes $p\le13$, including empty sets and singletons. Those checks illustrate the theorem; they do not prove it.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 95, ask whether sums $a_i+a_j$ with $i\ne j$ represent at least $2k-3$ distinct classes modulo $p$ when $p\ge2k-3$. The original statement does not demand an impossible unsaturated bound for smaller $p$. Primary scan, PDF page index 90: https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf.

J. A. Dias da Silva and Y. O. Hamidoune, *Cyclic Spaces for Grassmann Derivatives and Additive Theory*, *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* **26** (1994), pp.140–146, DOI https://doi.org/10.1112/blms/26.2.140, establish the stronger $m$-term result: for $1\le m\le k$, the number of sums of $m$ distinct members of $A$ is at least

$$
\min\{p,m(k-m)+1\}.
$$

Setting $m=2$ yields the requested inequality. The authors' publisher-hosted abstract was inspected; the entire seven-page original proof was not independently read in this audit. The following self-contained polynomial proof derives the two-term case without claiming to reproduce their exterior-algebra argument.

## GCL's current state

We use a finite-grid coefficient principle. If $F(X,Y)$ over a field has total degree at most $a+b$ and its coefficient of $X^aY^b$ is nonzero, then $F$ cannot vanish on all of $U\times V$ when $|U|=a+1$ and $|V|=b+1$. Indeed, Lagrange interpolation in each variable gives that coefficient as

$$
\sum_{u\in U}\sum_{v\in V}
\frac{F(u,v)}{\prod_{u'\in U\setminus\{u\}}(u-u')
\prod_{v'\in V\setminus\{v\}}(v-v')}.
$$

Every denominator is nonzero. For monomials of total degree at most $a+b$, the interpolation sum is zero unless both exponents are at least $a$ and $b$, in which case the monomial is exactly $X^aY^b$. Thus vanishing on the grid forces the specified coefficient to vanish.

Let $k=|A|\ge2$ and $C=A\widehat{+}A$. First suppose $p\ge2k-3$. If $|C|\le2k-4$, define

$$
F(X,Y)=(X-Y)(X+Y)^{2k-4-|C|}\prod_{c\in C}(X+Y-c).
$$

It has degree $2k-3$ and vanishes on $A\times A$: the diagonal is killed by $X-Y$, and every off-diagonal sum lies in $C$. Its coefficient of $X^{k-1}Y^{k-2}$ is the corresponding coefficient of the leading homogeneous part $(X-Y)(X+Y)^{2k-4}$:

$$
\binom{2k-4}{k-2}-\binom{2k-4}{k-1}
=\frac{1}{k-1}\binom{2k-4}{k-2}\not\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

The nonzero claim holds because $p>2k-4$, so the relevant factorials and $k-1$ are invertible modulo $p$. Take $U=A$ and any $(k-1)$-element subset $V\subset A$. The coefficient principle contradicts vanishing on $U\times V$. Hence $|C|\ge2k-3$.

Now suppose $p<2k-3$. Necessarily $p$ is odd: for $p=2$, the bound $k\le p$ makes this case impossible. If $C$ omits a residue $c_0$, put

$$
G(X,Y)=(X-Y)\prod_{c\in\mathbb F_p\setminus\{c_0\}}(X+Y-c).
$$

It vanishes on $A\times A$ and has degree $p$. Set $a=k-1$ and $b=p-k+1$, so $a+b=p$ and $0\le b<k$. The coefficient of $X^aY^b$ in the leading homogeneous part $(X-Y)(X+Y)^{p-1}$ is

$$
\binom{p-1}{k-2}-\binom{p-1}{k-1}
\equiv2(-1)^{k-2}\not\equiv0\pmod p,
$$

using $\binom{p-1}{j}\equiv(-1)^j\pmod p$. Choose $U=A$ and a $(b+1)$-element subset $V\subset A$. The coefficient principle again gives a contradiction. Therefore $C=\mathbb F_p$. Both cases establish the inequality.
