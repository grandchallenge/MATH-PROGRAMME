## The question in plain language

Start at an integer $n \geq 1$ and add consecutive reciprocals:

$$
\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{m}.
$$

Let $m$ be the first index at which this running sum reaches or passes $1$. The overshoot is

$$
\epsilon(n)=\sum_{n\leq k\leq m}\frac{1}{k}-1.
$$

Because $m$ is the first crossing, the preceding sum is strictly below $1$ and the displayed quantity is nonnegative. Erdős and Graham asked whether infinitely many starting points come unusually close to the threshold, in the precise sense

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty} n^2\epsilon(n)=0.
$$

The factor $n^2$ is important: a typical final reciprocal is of order $1/n$, so the question asks for much more accurate-than-usual crossings.

## A small example

The calculation can be checked with exact fractions. Take $n=8$. Summing through $19$ gives

$$
\sum_{k=8}^{19}\frac{1}{k}=\frac{14819303}{15519504}<1,
$$

whereas adding the next term gives

$$
\sum_{k=8}^{20}\frac{1}{k}=\frac{77976391}{77597520}=1+\frac{378871}{77597520}>1.
$$

Thus the minimal index is $m=20$, the overshoot is $\epsilon(8)=378871/77597520$, and

$$
8^2\epsilon(8)=\frac{1515484}{4849845}.
$$

This verifies the definition, the strict minimality check, and the exact arithmetic for one finite input. It does not establish any limiting statement: one example, even a small overshoot, cannot prove that arbitrarily large $n$ have the required behavior.

## What is known

The primary source located for this audit is Jeck Lim and Stefan Steinerberger, “On differences of two harmonic numbers,” arXiv:2405.11354v3 (11 June 2024), especially Theorem 1 on printed p. 1. It states that for every $c>0$ there are infinitely many positive integer pairs $(m,n)$ such that

$$
1\leq\sum_{\ell=n}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell}\leq 1+\frac{c}{n^2}.
$$

For any such pair, move left to the first index at which the sum reaches $1$. Monotonicity makes that first-crossing overshoot no larger than $c/n^2$. Since $c$ is arbitrary, this proves the affirmative answer to the original question. The argument uses asymptotics for harmonic sums and a selected subsequence of continued-fraction convergents to $e$.

Their Theorem 2, on printed p. 2, gives a refined but differently normalized statement: for every $\varepsilon>0$, infinitely many pairs satisfy

$$
\left|\sum_{\ell=n}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell}-1\right|\leq \frac{1}{n^2(\log n)^{5/4-\varepsilon}}.
$$

The paper expressly notes that this version does not impose that the sum exceed $1$. It therefore should not be silently identified with the minimal-crossing function $\epsilon(n)$, although it is relevant quantitative evidence. The stronger lower-growth expectation $n^{2+\delta}\epsilon(n)\to\infty$ for every $\delta>0$ remains presented as a belief, not as a proved theorem in the sources read here.

There is also a useful logical distinction between “infinitely many pairs” and “the least crossing index.” If a sum reaches $1$ at some $m$, then the first crossing exists because the partial sums increase by positive terms. Replacing $m$ by that first crossing can only remove positive summands, so it preserves the lower bound $1$ and decreases the overshoot. This is the small normalization argument that transfers Theorem 1 to the exact definition in the problem. It does not transfer the absolute-value estimate in Theorem 2 without an additional above-threshold argument. The primary paper’s own wording and the live page’s update preserve that distinction, even though catalogue summaries naturally compress both results under “proved.”

## What remains

The main mathematical question is externally resolved, but a bounded successor can make the interfaces safer. First, compare the published version with arXiv v3 and record whether Theorem 1’s one-sided construction is explicitly converted to the minimal index in the final text. Second, formalize only the finite, elementary bridge: if a pair $(n,m)$ has sum at least $1$ and at most $1+c/n^2$, prove that the first crossing index has overshoot at most $c/n^2$. This isolates the normalization step from the continued-fraction argument. A separate task could then audit the exact hypotheses and dependencies of the continued-fraction construction, without claiming a Lean proof until a checked artifact exists.
