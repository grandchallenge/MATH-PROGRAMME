## The question in plain language

The prime numbers do not arrive at regular intervals. If $p_n$ denotes the prime with index $n$, define its next gap by

$$
d_n=p_{n+1}-p_n.
$$

For a given index bound $x$, inspect the first $x$ gaps and ask which *even* distances have not yet appeared. Erdős Problem [#853](https://www.erdosproblems.com/853) defines $r(x)$ to be the smallest positive even gap length that is absent. It asks whether

$$
r(x)\longrightarrow\infty
$$

and, more ambitiously, whether

$$
\frac{r(x)}{\log x}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

Here $x$ limits the **index of the prime**, not the size of the prime. Evenness is essential: apart from the initial gap between 2 and 3, gaps between successive odd primes are even, so an odd value such as 3 can never be a prime gap.

## A small example

The first ten consecutive-prime gaps are

$$
1,2,2,4,2,4,2,4,6,2.
$$

The even distances $2,4,6$ have already appeared, but $8$ has not. Therefore $r(10)=8$. Independent enumeration by a prime sieve also gives

| Index limit $x$ | $r(x)$ |
|---:|---:|
| 4 | 6 |
| 10 | 8 |
| 30 | 10 |
| 100 | 16 |

The quantity cannot decrease as we examine more gaps: once an even distance occurs, it remains present among all later prefixes. That does not prove that every possible even distance will eventually appear.

## What is known

This problem connects directly to the much stronger **Polignac conjecture**, which predicts that every positive even number occurs *infinitely often* as a gap between consecutive primes. The first question here requires much less. In fact, one has the elementary equivalence

$$
r(x)\to\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every positive even number occurs at least once as a prime gap}.
$$

To see the forward implication, if one even distance $2m$ never occurs, the first missing even distance is always at most $2m$. For the converse, fix a finite initial collection $2,4,\ldots,2m$. If each occurs somewhere, all occur by the largest of their finite first-occurrence indices. Thus $r(x)>2m$ thereafter, for every fixed $m$.

This argument **does not** imply that each gap occurs infinitely often and says nothing about the rate relative to $\log x$. The maintained problem remains open. The historical paper by Erdős in 1985 asked about $r(x)$ but omitted the restriction to even distances; the maintained statement corrects this essential oversight.

## GCL's current state

The [authenticated source audit #2124](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2124#issuecomment-6143221481) compared the printed 1985 question with the present problem and the frozen [Formal Conjectures statement](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/853.lean). Its two Lean theorem declarations still have proof placeholders. The formal definition takes the infimum of missing positive even gaps. That matches the *shape* of the corrected question, but a full index-convention and coercion check has not been performed.

The numerical table is reproducible finite evidence, not a verified asymptotic theorem or mathematical certificate. This article is an explanatory draft, not an accepted GCL proof.

## What remains

The first research boundary is whether each fixed positive even gap occurs at least once. The stronger quantitative question asks how quickly the initial uninterrupted set of observed even gap sizes grows. Even a proof that each fixed distance appears would not provide a uniform growth rate for $r(x)$ without controlling the indices of its first occurrences.

A separate, bounded formal task is to check the exact indexing of the protected `primeGap` definition and the behavior of its minimum for small bounds, then encode the elementary equivalence above and reproduce the finite examples. This is not permission to fill the original open conjecture's proof placeholders.
