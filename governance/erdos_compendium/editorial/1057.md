## The question in plain language

A **Carmichael number** is a composite positive integer that behaves like a prime in one important modular-arithmetic test: for every integer $a$ relatively prime to $n$, it satisfies

$$
a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n.
$$

Let $C(x)$ count the Carmichael numbers no greater than $x$. Erdős [Problem #1057](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1057) asks whether they are sufficiently numerous that

$$
C(x)=x^{1-o(1)}.
$$

This means that $\log C(x)/\log x$ approaches $1$ as $x$ grows. Merely knowing that infinitely many Carmichael numbers exist does not answer it.

## A small example

Consider $561=3\cdot11\cdot17$. Its prime factors are distinct, and

$$
3-1=2\mid560,\quad
11-1=10\mid560,\quad
17-1=16\mid560.
$$

By Korselt's criterion, this makes $561$ a Carmichael number. The same exact test verifies $1105=5\cdot13\cdot17$ and $1729=7\cdot13\cdot19$: for each prime divisor $p$, $p-1$ divides $n-1$. We independently checked these factorizations and divisibilities. Three examples demonstrate the concept but reveal little about the asymptotic count $C(x)$.

## What is known

Erdős's 1956 paper *On Pseudoprimes and Carmichael Numbers* gave an upper bound smaller than $x$. Alford, Granville and Pomerance proved in 1994 that infinitely many Carmichael numbers exist and obtained a lower bound of order $x^{2/7}$ for sufficiently large $x$. Later improvements include a lower-bound exponent $0.3389$ attributed to Lichtman's work on primes in arithmetic progressions.

These are major results, but an exponent strictly below $1$ cannot establish the desired $x^{1-o(1)}$ conclusion. The [canonical catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1057) therefore still marks the problem **open**. A further reported exponent $0.34$ has been disputed in the site's history and is not adopted here as established.

## What remains

The open problem requires a lower bound approaching $x^{1-\varepsilon}$ for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ at sufficiently large $x$, or a counterexample to this growth law. A bounded next investigation is to pin the exact original and later lower-bound papers, compare their stated exponents and hypotheses, and verify Korselt's criterion computationally at a reproducible finite cutoff. Finite enumeration must not be extrapolated into an asymptotic proof.
