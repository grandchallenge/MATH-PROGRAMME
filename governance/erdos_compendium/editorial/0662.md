## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem [#662](https://www.erdosproblems.com/662), attributed to Erdős, Lovász and Vesztergombi, compares well-separated planar point sets with the **triangular lattice**, the familiar arrangement in which every point has six closest neighbours.

The catalogue asks about how many distances can occur below a given threshold $t$, under the constraint that every pair of distinct points is at least one unit apart. Unfortunately, the maintained question is **internally inconsistent as written**. It uses the word *distances* for quantities that appear to alternate between numbers of *neighbouring points* and numbers of *distinct distance values*. Some of its numerical examples do not agree with either interpretation.

The source itself warns that there must be at least one typo. The most responsible editorial treatment is therefore a **source-fidelity investigation**, not an assertion that the ill-posed literal statement is a new open theorem.

## A small example

Let the unit triangular lattice be

$$
\Lambda=\left\{\left(a+\frac b2,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}b\right):
a,b\in\mathbb Z\right\}.
$$

The squared distance from its origin to the lattice point indexed by $(a,b)$ is

$$
Q(a,b)=a^2+ab+b^2.
$$

There are six lattice points with $Q=1$, six with $Q=3$, six with $Q=4$, twelve with $Q=7$, and six with $Q=9$. These numbers were independently verified by exact integer enumeration.

Thus the numbers of **other lattice points within a disk** centred at the origin are:

| Radius $t$ | Count of lattice neighbours with distance $\leq t$ | Distinct positive distance values |
|---|---:|---:|
| $1$ | 6 | 1 |
| $\sqrt3$ | 12 | 2 |
| $2$ | 18 | 3 |
| $3$ | 36 | 5 |

This gives a precise test for the source's displayed examples. The maintained values $f(1)=6$ and $f(\sqrt3)=12$ match a **neighbour count**, not a count of different lengths. But its $f(3)=18$ does not match the same definition; **18 is the neighbour count at radius $2$**.

That discrepancy is mathematical evidence of a transcription or definition problem, not permission to silently replace the historical question with our preferred correction.

## What is known

The recorded version asks whether a set $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ satisfying

$$
\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert\geq1
\quad(i\ne j)
$$

has at most $f(t)$ distances of size at most $t$, once $n$ becomes large. It also asks whether the number of distances below $\sqrt3-\varepsilon$ is less than one.

Taken literally as the **number of distinct distance values anywhere in the configuration**, the proposed upper bound is immediately false for every fixed $t>1$.

To see why, choose any integer $K$ and $K$ distinct lengths

$$
1<r_1<\cdots<r_K<t.
$$

For each $i$, create a pair of points at separation $r_i$, and put the pairs far apart, with gaps larger than $3t$. Every pair of distinct points in the full set is at least one unit apart. Every cross-pair distance between *different* chosen pairs is larger than $t$, but each of the $K$ chosen pairs contributes its own different short distance $r_i$. We therefore have an arbitrarily large number $K$ of distinct distances at most $t$.

This directly refutes any universal **constant** upper bound $f(t)$ on the *global* number of short distance values. It remains a counterexample even when the number of points is arbitrarily large.

The second literal assertion is also suspect: if $0<\varepsilon<\sqrt3-1$, then $t=\sqrt3-\varepsilon>1$, and any configuration containing a unit-distance pair has at least one distance below $t$. Its global distance count cannot be *less than one*.

**What this does not settle.** An intended question might instead concern the maximum number of *neighbours of one point* within a disk of radius $t$, subject to unit separation, or the number of short edges incident to a chosen vertex. That is a genuine local packing problem, quite different from the number of distinct lengths anywhere among all point pairs. The catalogue's examples at radii $1$ and $\sqrt3$ suggest a local interpretation, but the original intended quantifiers remain unverified.

A further source inconsistency occurs in a displayed sequence of successive triangular-lattice lengths reported as $1,\sqrt3,3,5,\ldots$. The exact quadratic form above shows that the first positive lattice lengths include

$$
1,\quad\sqrt3,\quad2,\quad\sqrt7,\quad3,\ldots.
$$

The displayed sequence therefore cannot literally be the increasing sequence of all positive triangular-lattice distances.

## GCL's current state

The [original GCL catalogue issue #1933](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1933) has an [authenticated source-audit report](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1933#issuecomment-6144525548), with disposition **STATUS_CONFLICT**. The report records exact lattice-shell arithmetic, the contradiction in the original examples and the arbitrary-separated-pairs counterexample to the literal global interpretation.

This is not an assertion that the historical Erdős–Lovász–Vesztergombi problem has been solved negatively. It shows that a particular **maintained transcription**, understood literally, does not describe a coherent unsolved mathematical assertion. The precise [Er97e, p.532] source page has not yet been independently inspected in this cohort.

This is an **unaccepted source-defect draft**. No corrected theorem statement is being asserted as canonical.

## What remains

The first obligation is an exact primary-source reconstruction. Inspect Erdős's original *Some of my favourite unsolved problems* (1997), page 532, including surrounding explanatory text. Determine whether $f(t)$ counts **neighbours**, **edges**, **length values**, or some other object; whether $t$ is an ordinary distance, a squared-distance threshold or a shell index; and whether the inequality counts distances globally or from a fixed centre.

Then check candidate corrected readings against the lattice-shell table and the separated-pairs construction. A proposed formulation that fails either an elementary finite fixture or the basic large-separation example should be rejected rather than forwarded to an unrestricted mathematical worker.

Only after such a source lock can a bounded mathematical successor be defined. For example, a repaired **local packing** version could ask how many unit-separated points fit inside an annulus or disk about a fixed centre, with exact thresholds and extremizers specified. But that would be a **proposed reconstruction**, not necessarily Erdős's question.

The article should remain on source-fidelity hold until the historical formulation is independently checked. Any later publication would still need accountable critical review, protected merge, public MathJax browser readback and a separate V2 editorial admission.
