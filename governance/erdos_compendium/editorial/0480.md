## The question in plain language

Suppose we write an infinite sequence of numbers, all between zero and one:

$$
x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots\in[0,1].
$$

Even if we try to spread these numbers out, some pairs will have to be unusually close. Donald J. Newman asked how close they must become when the two terms are separated by a prescribed number of positions.

For a positive integer $n$, compare $x_m$ with $x_{m+n}$ far along the sequence. Multiply the distance by $n$ to account for the shift. The statistic investigated by Chung and Graham is

$$
C(x)=\inf_{n\ge1}\liminf_{m\to\infty}
n\,|x_{m+n}-x_m|.
$$

The **inner limit inferior** asks how small the scaled difference gets infinitely often as $m$ tends to infinity, for one fixed positive shift $n$. The **outer infimum** then allows us to select the most favorable shift. These operations are deliberately ordered; exchanging them without proof would ask a different question.

The modern Erdős Problem #480 asks whether every such sequence has

$$
C(x)\le\frac1{\sqrt5}\approx0.44721.
$$

**Yes.** The stronger and sharp bound found by Fan Chung and Ronald Graham is

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle C(x)\le
\alpha=\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{F_{2k}}\right)^{-1}
\approx0.39441967,}
$$

where $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$ and $F_{j+2}=F_{j+1}+F_j$ are the Fibonacci numbers.

The new constant is smaller than $1/\sqrt5$: every sequence has a fixed positive shift along which arbitrarily late differences come even closer than Newman's proposed threshold. Chung and Graham also construct a sequence for which **no smaller universal constant would work**.

## A small example

Consider the periodic sequence with period five

$$
x_m=\frac{m\bmod5}{4}.
$$

It repeats the five values $0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1$. Choosing shift $n=5$ gives

$$
x_{m+5}=x_m
$$

for every $m$. Thus the scaled difference is always zero and $C(x)=0$.

The same idea applies to any periodic sequence, even one that oscillates widely at neighboring positions: choose its period as the shift.

That makes the problem interesting for **aperiodic sequences chosen to resist close recurrence at every shift**. The sharpness construction is such a sequence; periodic examples demonstrate the definition, not why the best constant is positive.

A second small calculation shows where the Fibonacci constant comes from. The even-indexed Fibonacci values start

$$
F_2=1,\quad F_4=3,\quad F_6=8,\quad
F_8=21,\quad F_{10}=55,\quad F_{12}=144.
$$

Consequently the first few partial sums of the denominator are

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1&=1+1=2,\\
c_2&=1+1+\frac13=\frac73,\\
c_3&=\frac73+\frac18=\frac{59}{24}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Their reciprocal approximations decrease toward the true sharp bound:

| Number of Fibonacci terms | Reciprocal partial sum |
|---:|---:|
| $1$ | $0.500000$ |
| $2$ | $0.428571$ |
| $3$ | $0.406780$ |
| $5$ | $0.396175$ |
| $8$ | $0.394517$ |
| $12$ | $0.394422$ |

In particular, the elementary estimate $c\ge7/3>\sqrt5$ already proves

$$
\alpha=\frac1c<\frac1{\sqrt5}.
$$

The complete numerical value comes from the convergent reciprocal Fibonacci series, not from extrapolating a finite test to an infinite theorem.

## What is known

### The original problem and the 1984 resolution

Erdős and Graham's 1980 monograph, printed page 96, attributes the question to Donald J. Newman. Their original wording asks for infinitely many $m$ and $n$ such that

$$
|x_{m+n}-x_m|\le\frac1{n\sqrt5}.
$$

That same printed page unexpectedly says the conjecture is *false* and refers readers to an added-in-proof note on printed page 107.

But the added-in-proof note, also directly inspected, says that Chung and Graham have proved a stronger-looking statement: for any sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ and every bounded sequence, there is **one fixed positive $n$** with infinitely many $m$ satisfying

$$
|x_{m+n}-x_m|<
\frac1{(c-\varepsilon)n},
\qquad
c=1+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{F_{2k}}.
$$

Because $c>\sqrt5$, taking $\varepsilon$ sufficiently small makes the right side less than $1/(n\sqrt5)$.

There is therefore a genuine **historical wording issue**. “Infinitely many pairs $(m,n)$” may allow one fixed $n$ and infinitely many $m$; “infinitely many distinct shifts $n$” would be a stronger condition. The later theorem gives the former, not automatically the latter. The published 1980 claim “false,” the added-in-proof positive assertion and this possible quantifier distinction have not been fully reconciled. We should not silently rewrite the original historical question as though the pages were perfectly consistent.

The **modern, mathematically precise formulation** is the statistic $C(x)$ displayed above. Its resolution is established.

### Chung and Graham's sharp theorem

The authors announced their result in a one-page 1981 paper in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. They then published the full treatment in *On Irregularities of Distribution*, *Finite and Infinite Sets*, volume 37 of *Colloquia Mathematica Societatis János Bolyai* (1984), pages 181–222.

The complete 42-page paper is available from Graham's publication archive. On **printed page 182**, Theorem 1 states

$$
C(x)\le\alpha
$$

for every sequence in $[0,1]$. On **printed page 183**, Theorem 2 establishes optimality by constructing an extremal sequence $x^*$ satisfying

$$
C(x^*)=\alpha.
$$

In fact the authors establish the stronger, all-pairs inequality

$$
\inf_{n\ge1}\inf_{m\ge0}
n\,|x^*_{m+n}-x^*_m|=\alpha.
$$

The extremal sequence is constructed from an unusual numeral system based on **even-indexed Fibonacci numbers**. Each nonnegative integer $N$ has an appropriate constrained expansion

$$
N=\sum_{i\ge1}\varepsilon_i(N)F_{2i},
\qquad \varepsilon_i(N)\in\{0,1,2\},
$$

with rules ensuring uniqueness. The extremal sequence is

$$
x_N^*=\alpha\sum_{i\ge1}
\frac{\varepsilon_i(N)}{F_{2i}}.
$$

The construction is not a random sequence. Its arithmetic representation is deliberately chosen so that the difficulty of placing two close values is calibrated against the distance between their indices.

This is the mathematically substantive role of Fibonacci numbers in the theorem: they organize an extremal packing of infinitely many sequence entries into the unit interval. The full proof of the representation rules and sharpness occupies part of the authors' original 42-page paper. It has **not been independently reconstructed or formally replayed by GCL**.

### A further historical caution

The 1980 added-in-proof passage suggests that fractional parts of multiples of the golden-ratio conjugate

$$
\tau=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}
$$

show optimality. Taken literally, that example does not establish the published sharp constant. For the rotation sequence $y_m=\{m\tau\}$, adjacent terms differ in absolute value by either $\tau$ or $1-\tau$. Both occur infinitely often, so

$$
\liminf_{m\to\infty}|y_{m+1}-y_m|
=1-\tau=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}
\approx0.381966.
$$

This is strictly **smaller** than $\alpha\approx0.394420$. Consequently the golden rotation is not an extremizer for the 1984 value under the displayed definition of $C$. The proper primary sharpness witness is the even-Fibonacci-digit sequence from **Theorem 2**, not an unqualified repetition of the earlier remark.

The precise intent or possible editorial errors of that early parenthesis remain a historical source-review task; it would be unwarranted to invent a correction.

## What remains

The universal modern $C(x)$ problem has been solved. The next substantive obligations are careful **source adjudication and formal theorem verification**, rather than a search for a new constant.

First, identify the intended exact quantifiers in the 1980 historical request. If it requires infinitely many distinct $n$, determine whether the later theorem actually implies this stronger statement or whether an explicit counterexample exists. The original “false” note should not be contradicted or explained away without evidence.

Second, review the complete 1984 proof, especially the uniqueness constraints of the even-indexed Fibonacci representation and the construction of the extremal sequence. This is the essential route to an independently checked sharpness certificate.

Third, reproduce the mathematical formal statement under the appropriate Lean toolchain, including $n\ge1$, $\liminf_{m\to\infty}$ and the sharpness quantifiers. Compiling a theorem declaration with `sorry` would not satisfy the proof gate; the actual underlying bound and witness construction need complete checked proofs without unexpected axioms.

The existing article is suitable for source-bounded mathematical explanation, but final protected editorial acceptance must preserve the historical quantifier exception and the formal-checking boundary.
