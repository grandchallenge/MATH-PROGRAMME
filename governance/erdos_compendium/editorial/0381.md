## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, let $\tau(n)$ be its number of positive divisors. The integer $n$ is highly composite when every smaller positive integer has strictly fewer divisors. These are record-holders for the divisor-count function. If $Q(x)$ counts the highly composite numbers at most $x$, Problem 381 asks whether, for every fixed integer $k\geq 1$, there is a positive constant (depending on $k$) such that

$$
Q(x) \gg_k (\log x)^k
$$

for all sufficiently large $x$. In words: does the supply of record-holders eventually outrun every fixed power of the logarithm?

The quantifiers matter. This is not merely the claim that there are infinitely many highly composite numbers, nor that $Q(x)$ beats one particular power. It asks for one lower bound for each $k$, with the exponent allowed to grow without bound. The source statement uses $[1,x]$ and strict comparison with every $m<n$; this article keeps those conventions.

## A small example

The divisor-count identity gives an exact finite check. If $n=\prod p_i^{a_i}$, then $\tau(n)=\prod(a_i+1)$. Direct enumeration of divisors for $1\leq n\leq 100$ gives the record pairs

$$
(1,1),(2,2),(4,3),(6,4),(12,6),(24,8),(36,9),(48,10),(60,12).
$$

Thus the finite computation finds $Q(100)=9$. In particular, $60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$ has $\tau(60)=(2+1)(1+1)(1+1)=12$, while the largest divisor count among $1,\ldots,59$ is $10$, attained at $48$. Therefore $60$ is a highly composite number under the exact strict definition.

This check is reproducible with integer arithmetic: for each $n$, test each integer $d$ from $1$ through $\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$, count both $d$ and $n/d$ when distinct, and retain $n$ exactly when its count exceeds the previous maximum. It confirms terminology and a finite initial segment only. It does not estimate $Q(x)$ asymptotically, prove any theorem about all highly composite numbers, or decide the quantified statement by itself.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/381), accessed 13 October 2026, states that the answer is no. It attributes a lower bound $Q(x)\gg (\log x)^{1+c}$ for some $c>0$ to Erdős and the negative resolution to Jean-Louis Nicolas. The entry cites [Er44], P. Erdős, “On highly composite numbers,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 19 (1944), 130–133, and [Ni71], Jean-Louis Nicolas, “Répartition des nombres hautement composés de Ramanujan,” *Canadian Journal of Mathematics* 23 (1971), 116–130.

The primary Nicolas article is available through [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-mathematics/article/repartition-des-nombres-hautement-composes-de-ramanujan/4E3B60B2BC31651C55D01FFC9ECB4497), with DOI [10.4153/CJM-1971-012-6](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1971-012-6). I inspected the primary PDF. Its Theorem 4, printed p. 127, states a fixed-power upper bound of the form $Q(X)=O((\log X)^{1+c})$, with the constant inherited from the preceding argument. Its Theorem 5 on the same page records the lower bound of Erdős type. The upper bound is enough to refute the Problem 381 question: choosing any fixed $k>1+c$ makes the proposed lower bound incompatible with Nicolas’s upper bound for large $X$.

The phrase $(\log x)^{O(1)}$ on the maintained page is a status-level summary, not a replacement for the paper’s precise theorem. The primary source supports “disproved” through a bounded exponent; it does not justify silently assigning a new numerical value of $c$ or claiming that every detail of Nicolas’s proof has been formalized.

## What remains

The external resolution is clear enough for a source-locked editorial record, but a future formalization would still need a faithful interface. It would define highly composite numbers over positive naturals, define $Q(x)$ as a finite count, and encode the exact quantifier “for every $k\geq1$” before formalizing either Nicolas’s upper bound or the negation of the original question. The asymptotic notation also needs an explicit constant-and-threshold convention.

A bounded successor task is therefore to formalize only the finite lemma illustrated above: prove the divisor-count formula, prove that $60$ is highly composite, and compute the record list through $100$ by a transparent finite argument. Acceptance should require exact agreement with the listed nine records; a mismatch in the definition of $[1,x]$, divisor counting, or strict record comparison falsifies the task. This is useful interface work, not a proof of the asymptotic theorem. A larger task would require a source-faithful translation and specialist mathematical review of Nicolas’s Theorem 4.
