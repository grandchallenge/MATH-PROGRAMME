## The question in plain language

A prime number sometimes has the form

$$
p=2^kq+1,
$$

where $q$ is another prime and $k$ is a nonnegative integer. [Erdős Problem #1065](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1065) asks whether **infinitely many** primes have this form. It asks a second, more permissive question about

$$
p=2^k3^\ell q+1,
\qquad k,\ell\ge0,
$$

again with $q$ prime. The second family contains the first by setting $\ell=0$. Showing infinitude of the larger family would not automatically establish infinitude of the smaller one.

## A small example

Several small primes work: $3=2^0\cdot2+1$, $7=2\cdot3+1$, $11=2\cdot5+1$, $13=4\cdot3+1$, $29=4\cdot7+1$, and $41=8\cdot5+1$. These identities and the relevant primality checks were independently verified. The pattern alone does not guarantee a prime: $2^4\cdot3+1=49=7^2$.

There is also a useful boundary observation. When $k=0$, the first form reads $p=q+1$. If $q$ is any odd prime, $q+1$ is even and larger than $2$, hence composite. So the only $k=0$ example is $q=2$, $p=3$.

## What is known

The maintained [catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1065) records both infinitude questions as **open**. It cites Richard Guy, *Unsolved Problems in Number Theory*, problem B46. The precise printed Guy page and any earlier original Erdős source remain to be checked.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2336](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2336#issuecomment-6156700112) identifies the two different quantifiers and verifies finite prime witnesses. The [pinned Lean source](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/1065.lean) records both infinitude statements but does not prove them. Neither the finite arithmetic nor source metadata amounts to a GCL-certified theorem.

## What remains

A resolution needs an infinitude theorem or a counterexample to one of the conjectured infinite families. As a bounded precursor, independently enumerate prime witnesses through a fixed cutoff and compare Guy's exact mathematical formulation with the frozen formal statements. Explicitly preserve the $k=0$ and $\ell=0$ cases.
