## The question in plain language

Take $m!+1$ and choose one of its prime divisors $p$. Usually the remainder of $p$ upon division by $m$ can vary. Erdős, Hardy and Subbarao singled out the case where the prime does **not** satisfy $p\equiv1\pmod m$.

An integer $m$ with at least one such prime is called an **EHS number**. A prime $p$ appearing in at least one such instance is a **Pillai prime**.

[Erdős Problem #1074](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1074) asks whether EHS numbers have a natural density among positive integers, and whether Pillai primes have a limiting **relative density among primes**. The two density denominators are different.

## A small example

Since

$$
8!+1=40321=61\cdot661,
$$

and $61\equiv5\pmod8$, the integer $8$ is an EHS number and $61$ is a Pillai prime.

Similarly,

$$
9!+1=362881=19\cdot71\cdot269,
$$

and $71\equiv8\pmod9$. Hence $9$ is another EHS number.

A historically important example is $p=23$. It divides both $14!+1$ and $18!+1$, and its residues $9\pmod{14}$ and $5\pmod{18}$ are not $1$. Therefore $14$ and $18$ belong to the EHS set, and $23$ belongs to the Pillai-prime set.

One small edge case matters: for $m=1$, every integer is congruent to $1$ modulo $1$, so $1$ is not an EHS number.

## What is known

Hardy and Subbarao established that both the EHS and Pillai-prime sets are infinite. Their original numerical experiments led to competing possibilities for the eventual densities. **Infinitude does not imply positive density**, so it does not settle either limit question.

The maintained catalogue marks both density problems open and lists the first EHS numbers as $8,9,13,14,15,16,17,\ldots$ and the first Pillai primes as $23,29,59,61,67,71,\ldots$.

## What remains

The next source review must inspect the original 2002 Hardy–Subbarao article, verify both infinitude proofs and the historical limits, and independently reproduce finite memberships by factoring factorial-plus-one values. Any statistical estimate should specify its cutoff and whether its denominator counts integers or primes. None of those computations can replace a proof of a limiting density.
