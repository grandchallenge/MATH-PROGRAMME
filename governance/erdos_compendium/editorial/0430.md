## The question in plain language

Fix an integer $n$. Start with $a_1=n-1$, then construct a decreasing sequence of positive integers. At each step choose the largest smaller integer $a$ for which **every prime factor of $a$ is greater than $n-a$**. For example, the prime factors of $21$ are $3$ and $7$; if $n-a=1$, both are greater than the required threshold.

Erdős Problem 430 asks whether, for **every sufficiently large $n$**, some number in this decreasing sequence must be **composite**, rather than all selected numbers being prime. The question is one of eventual necessity, not of what happens at one specific $n$. A finite computation can find examples in both directions without resolving this statement.

There is a small but important convention: the maintained statement describes a sequence in $[1,n)$, while its example for $n=8$ stops after $7$ and $5$. If $1$ were included literally, the prime-factor condition would hold vacuously because $1$ has no prime factors, and $1$ itself would not be prime. The intended nontrivial problem therefore treats $1$ as excluded from the list of potential prime or composite entries. Any formal normalization must make this convention explicit.

## A small example

For $n=8$, the first selected value is $a_1=7$, whose prime factor $7$ is greater than $8-7=1$. The next qualifying value is $5$, because its prime factor $5$ exceeds $8-5=3$. The integer $6=2\cdot3$ fails since its prime factor $2$ is not greater than $8-6=2$; the values $4,3,2$ also fail their strict inequalities. Thus the nontrivial sequence is

$$
7,\ 5,
$$

and it consists only of primes. This reproduces the example on the maintained problem page.

The same exact factorization test gives, for $n=20$,

$$
19,\ 17,\ 13,\ 11,
$$

again all primes. These finite exceptions do not contradict a conjecture about **all sufficiently large** $n$, since the required threshold might be far larger. On the other hand, for $n=22$ the very first term is $21=3\cdot7$, a composite number with threshold $22-21=1$, so it is an immediate composite witness.

A reproducible finite algorithm simply checks every integer $m$ from $n-1$ down to $2$, factors $m$ exactly, and includes it if its smallest prime factor satisfies

$$
P^-(m)>n-m.
$$

Here $P^-(m)$ is the least prime factor of $m$. Since every other prime factor is at least as large, checking only the smallest factor is equivalent to checking all prime factors. This reduces the rule to an ordinary exact integer inequality.

## What is known

The question appears in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), in the [historical scanned monograph](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). Erdős and Graham described preliminary numerical calculations made by Selfridge suggesting an affirmative answer, while stating that no proof was then known. The numerical report is historical evidence, not a theorem.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #430](https://www.erdosproblems.com/430) page still labels the statement **OPEN**. It credits Sarosh Adenwalla with an equivalence to the first part of [Problem #385](https://www.erdosproblems.com/385). The static form is easier to state than the decreasing algorithm: for every sufficiently large $n$, does there exist **some composite** $m<n$ whose prime factors all exceed $n-m$?

Why is this equivalent? The greedy sequence lists qualifying numbers in descending order, with no further condition depending on the previously selected terms beyond being smaller. If a qualifying composite $m$ exists, it must appear when the algorithm reaches its position. Conversely, if the algorithm selects a composite, that number is a qualifying $m$ in the static formulation. This equivalence depends on using the same range, especially excluding the vacuously qualifying value $1$.

The source reports no published theorem settling the eventual-existence question. Related results about smooth or rough integers may provide research tools, but they do not by themselves imply the exact composite witness required here. It would be a mistake to interpret the early all-prime examples as disproving eventual truth, or the occasional composite witnesses as proving it.

## What remains

A suitable next task is an exact computational audit over the bounded domain $3\leq n\leq5000$. Use both a sieve of smallest prime factors and independent trial division to enumerate all qualifying numbers $2\leq m<n$. Record which inputs give an all-prime sequence and, for every other input, the largest qualifying composite. Verify the hand calculations at $n=8$, $20$ and $22$.

The mathematical source task is to read the first part of Problem #385 and write a proof that its quantified static statement is equivalent to Problem #430 under identical domain conventions. In particular, show why the strict inequality for the *least* prime factor suffices, and specify how the value $1$ is handled. The bounded task succeeds if two independent computations agree and the statement mapping is exact; it fails upon an incorrect prime factor, an off-by-one inequality or a claim that finite results settle eventual truth. Stop after this computational and source-concordance audit, keeping the original problem open.
