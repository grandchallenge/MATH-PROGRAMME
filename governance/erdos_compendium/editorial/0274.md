## The question in plain language

Erdős’s 1977 formulation asks about a finite abelian group $G$. Choose subgroups $H_1,\ldots,H_k$ and representatives $a_1,\ldots,a_k\in G$, with $k>1$. Can the cosets be an exact partition of $G$ while having different sizes? In symbols, the proposed counterexample would satisfy

$$
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}a_iH_i,
\qquad
(a_iH_i)\cap(a_jH_j)=\varnothing\quad(i\ne j),
$$

and $|a_iH_i|\ne|a_jH_j|$ whenever $i\ne j$. The answer to the original abelian question is no: some two cosets must have the same size.

For a finite group, $|a_iH_i|=|H_i|$ and

$$
|H_i|=\frac{|G|}{[G:H_i]}.
$$

Thus “two cosets have the same size” is equivalent to “two subgroup indices are equal.” The broader Herzog–Schönheim conjecture replaces “finite abelian” by an arbitrary group and uses finite indices explicitly: a finite partition into cosets of proper subgroups with $k>1$ should contain $i\ne j$ with $[G:H_i]=[G:H_j]$. That broader statement is the modern open problem. The distinction matters: the catalogue’s single `open` label conflates a proved special case with an open generalization.

Its quantifiers can be written as

$$
\forall G\ \forall k>1\ \forall (H_i,a_i)_{i=1}^{k},\quad
\left(G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}a_iH_i\ \text{and the cosets are pairwise disjoint}\right)
\Rightarrow
\exists i\ne j\quad [G:H_i]=[G:H_j].
$$

For an infinite $G$, the hypothesis is still a finite partition and the indices, not the cardinalities of the cosets, are the invariant being compared.

## A small example

Work additively in $C_6=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$. The following five cosets form an exact partition:

$$
C_6=\{0\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{2\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{3\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{5\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}\bigl(1+\{0,3\}\bigr).
$$

The first four parts are cosets of the subgroup $\{0\}$ and have size $1$; the last is the coset $\{1,4\}$ of $\{0,3\}$ and has size $2$. The sizes are therefore $(1,1,1,1,2)$, illustrating the repetition forced by the conjecture. Exact counting gives

$$
1+1+1+1+2=6=|C_6|.
$$

Here is an independently reproducible integer check of union, disjointness, and sizes; it uses no floating-point arithmetic.

```python
G = set(range(6))
parts = [{0}, {2}, {3}, {5}, {1, 4}]
assert set().union(*parts) == G
assert sum(len(part) for part in parts) == len(G) == 6
assert all(parts[i].isdisjoint(parts[j])
           for i in range(len(parts)) for j in range(i))
print(sorted(set().union(*parts)))
print([len(part) for part in parts])
```

The output is `[0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]` and `[1, 1, 1, 1, 2]`. This finite witness is a check of the example, not evidence that the global conjecture has been proved or disproved. The authenticated source audit records the same example and also reports an exhaustive $C_6$ coset enumeration with no all-distinct-size exact cover.

## What is known

Zhi-Wei Sun’s *On the Herzog–Schönheim conjecture for uniform covers of groups*, Journal of Algebra **273** (2004), 153–175, Theorem 1.1, proves that a nontrivial uniform cover whose subgroup factors are subnormal cannot have pairwise distinct finite indices. In a finite abelian group every subgroup is normal, hence subnormal, and an exact partition is a uniform cover of multiplicity one. This theorem therefore proves Erdős’s finite-abelian question. The theorem does not prove the unrestricted conjecture for arbitrary groups.

There are substantial partial results. Margolis and Schnabel, *The Herzog–Schönheim conjecture for small groups and harmonic subgroups*, Beiträge zur Algebra und Geometrie **60** (2019), 399–418, state Theorem A: every group of order less than $1440$ satisfies the conjecture. Garonzi and Margolis, *The Herzog–Schönheim conjecture for simple and symmetric groups*, arXiv:2509.25118v2 (5 May 2026), prove the conjecture for symmetric groups (Theorem 1.2) and simple groups (Theorem 1.3). These results leave the general finite-group problem open; they are not a counterexample and not a universal proof.

The maintained Erdős entry says precisely that the abelian special case is settled by Sun while the broader question remains open. Its comments and linked proof claims are useful status evidence, but they are not themselves a mathematical certificate.

## What remains

For the original abelian problem, the remaining work is documentary and semantic: preserve the primary-source wording, explain the reduction from equal coset sizes to equal indices, and reconcile it with the formal structure. The 1974 *Canadian Mathematical Bulletin* Research Problem No. 9 scan was not obtained in the audit, so its exact wording is not independently asserted.

For the broad conjecture, a bounded next task would enumerate groups of order exactly $1440$, after fixing a GAP/SmallGroups release and hash. Candidate distinct indices $d_i$ must first pass the exact necessary equation

$$
\sum_i\frac{1}{d_i}=1,
$$

because the disjoint cosets have total size $|G|$. Exact subgroup and coset enumeration could then test every candidate partition. One verified partition with pairwise distinct indices would disprove the conjecture; exhausting order $1440$ would establish only that order. Resource exhaustion without a certificate is `PARTIAL`, not a negative result. The 1997 source scan also remains unavailable, and its cited printed page differs from the live entry’s reference, so that historical wording stays an explicit exception.
