## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, write its prime factorization as $n=\prod_p p^{v_p(n)}$. The 2-full part is

$$
B_2(n)=\prod_{v_p(n)\geq 2}p^{v_p(n)}.
$$

Thus $B_2(n)$ keeps the complete prime-power contribution from primes that occur at least twice, and discards primes occurring only to the first power. Erdős Problem 367 asks how large a product of these parts can be over a short interval. For each fixed integer $k\geq 1$, set

$$
P_k(n)=\prod_{n\leq m<n+k}B_2(m).
$$

The main question is whether $P_k(n)\leq n^{2+o(1)}$ as $n$ tends to infinity. A stronger proposed bound is $P_k(n)\ll_k n^2$, with a constant allowed to depend on $k$. The wording “fixed $k$” matters: $k$ is chosen first and is not allowed to grow with $n$.

The page also asks about higher full parts $B_r$, where only prime powers of exponent at least $r$ are kept. Its literal higher r wording quantifies over every positive $\epsilon$, but the maintained formal statement now records the nontrivial reading in which some $\epsilon=\epsilon(r,k)>0$ may depend on $r$ and $k$. That distinction is mathematical, not editorial.

## A small example

The Pell recurrence

$$
x_0=1,\quad y_0=0,\qquad x_{j+1}=3x_j+8y_j,\quad y_{j+1}=x_j+3y_j
$$

satisfies $x_j^2-8y_j^2=1$. At $j=7$ it gives $x_7=114243$ and $y_7=40391$. Put $n=8y_7^2=13051463048$. Exact factorization gives

$$
n=2^3\cdot13^4\cdot239^2,\qquad n+1=3^2\cdot113^2\cdot337^2,
$$

and

$$
n+2=2\cdot5^2\cdot29\cdot269\cdot33461.
$$

Therefore $B_2(n)=n$, $B_2(n+1)=n+1$, and $B_2(n+2)=25$. The exact three-term product is

$$
P_3(n)=13051463048\cdot13051463049\cdot25=4258517192659022833800.
$$

This verifies the arithmetic certificate directly: $x_7^2-8y_7^2=1$ and $x_7^2\equiv-1\pmod {25}$. It does not prove an infinite family, a limsup statement, or any theorem about all $k$. It is one finite check of the Pell mechanism and of the definition of $B_2$.

## What is known

The original source cited by the maintained page is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 68, recorded as \`ErGr80\`. The accessible live page for [Problem 367](https://www.erdosproblems.com/367) was last edited on 23 March 2026 and still labels the problem open. It records the elementary bound $P_k(n)\ll n^2$ for $k\leq 2$ and reports Wouter van Doorn’s observation that this fails for every $k\geq 3$.

Van Doorn’s public November 2025 comment gives the Pell construction behind the finite example. Along a subsequence, $n_j=8y_j^2$ and $n_j+1=x_j^2$ are both 2-full, while a congruence forces $5^t\mid n_j+2$. Since the Pell sequence grows exponentially in the index and the selected index is exponential in $t$, this yields the reported lower bound $P_3(n_j)\gg n_j^2\log n_j$ infinitely often. This is strong evidence against the proposed $O_k(n^2)$ bound for $k\geq 3$, but the public discussion is not a GCL-certified proof record.

For related primary literature, David T. Walker’s “Consecutive Integer Pairs of Powerful Numbers and Related Diophantine Equations,” *The Fibonacci Quarterly* 14 (1976), pp. 111–116, develops the Pell-equation classification of consecutive powerful pairs. Its Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 explain how powers of a fundamental Pell solution produce the relevant square and powerful factors. That paper supplies context for the construction, not a solution of the short-interval product problem. A later public comment claims affirmative higher r bounds and a stronger $k=3$ limsup result, but those claims remain unverified external reports.

## What remains

The primary-source lock should first preserve the original Erdős–Graham wording and page reference while recording the later van Doorn clarification. A bounded mathematical successor could then formalize and prove the Pell congruence $x_{j_t}^2\equiv-1\pmod {5^t}$ for $j_1=1$ and $j_{t+1}=5j_t+2$, together with the exact growth estimate needed to turn $5^t$ into a logarithmic factor. Acceptance would require a proof for every $t$ and a separate check that the induced $B_2$ divisibility is stated correctly; failure of either implication would falsify the proposed route without deciding the original $n^{2+o(1)}$ question.

The first main question therefore remains open on the evidence reviewed here. The stronger $n^2$ claim is contradicted in the maintained page’s discussion for $k\geq 3$, while the $k\leq 2$ case is elementary. The higher r extension has a quantifier discrepancy and only reported, not accepted, progress.
