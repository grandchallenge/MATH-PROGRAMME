## The question in plain language

Let g(n)=#{m>=1:phi(m)=n}, the number of positive integer preimages under Euler's totient. Does every epsilon>0 admit infinitely many n with g(n)>n^(1-epsilon)? OPEN. Counting distinct m and allowing all m>=1 is essential.

## A small example

phi(3)=phi(4)=phi(6)=2, hence g(2)=3. phi(5)=phi(8)=phi(10)=phi(12)=4, giving g(4)=4. These are finite examples of multiple preimages, not evidence for near-linear fibers along infinitely many n.

## What is known

Maintained https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/821, read 2026-10-14: Pillai proves limsup g(n)=infinity, Erdős [Er35b] some positive power lower infinitely often. Lichtman [Li22], *Primes in arithmetic progressions to large moduli and shifted primes without large prime factors* (arXiv:2211.09641), gives exponent 0.71568... infinitely often, via smooth shifted primes; it does NOT prove all exponents tending to one. Baker–Harman [BaHa98] earlier; Luca–Pollack [LuPo11] average fibers. The exact numerical decimals and original full papers not independently inspected in this audit.

A finite totient fiber has a useful elementary restriction: if $\varphi(m)=n$ and $p$ divides $m$, then $p-1$ divides $n$. For $n=4$ this leaves possible prime divisors only $2,3,5$, and a short prime-power check gives exactly $m=5,8,10,12$. Thus $g(4)=4$; similarly $g(2)=3$ comes from $m=3,4,6$. Finite checks are not an argument for the near-linear infinitely-often conjecture.

The proposed near-linear multiplicity condition is

$$

\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists^\infty n:
\quad g(n)>n^{1-\varepsilon},\qquad
g(n)=\#\{m\geq1:\varphi(m)=n\}.

$$

If $p$ divides $m$, the factor $p-1$ divides $\varphi(m)$ and hence $n$. This restriction makes the finite cases $g(2)=3$ and $g(4)=4$ exactly checkable, but it does not force fibers of almost-linear size for infinitely many $n$.
