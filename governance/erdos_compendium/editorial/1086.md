## The question in plain language

How many triangles of the **same positive area** can be determined by $n$ points in the plane? [Erdős Problem #1086](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1086) asks for the maximum count $g(n)$, taken over all point configurations and choices of a common positive area.

The triangles are unordered triples of distinct vertices. Collinear triples have area zero and do not count. By uniformly scaling the plane, any fixed positive area can be normalized to one.

## A small example

Take the corners of a unit square. There are four ways to select three of the four vertices, and every resulting triangle has area exactly $1/2$.

Consequently $g(4)=4$: the square attains all $\binom43=4$ possible triangles. Scaling the square by $\sqrt2$ makes their common area exactly one.

In general, triangle area is half the absolute value of the determinant

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}x_2-x_1&y_2-y_1\\x_3-x_1&y_3-y_1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This gives an exact arithmetic test for equal areas without floating-point geometry.

## What is known

The question originated with Oppenheim and was studied by Erdős and Purdy in 1971. The maintained catalogue records early lower and upper estimates of the forms

$$
n^2\log\log n\ \ll\ g(n)\ \ll\ n^{5/2},
$$

with the upper bound subsequently improved by several authors. The reported best general upper bound is due to Raz and Sharir (2017):

$$
g(n)=O(n^{20/9}).
$$

The sharp growth is still **open**. Similar questions about simplices in higher-dimensional spaces require their own parameters and are not identical to the planar triangle problem.

## What remains

Inspect the primary Erdős–Purdy 1971 paper and the Raz–Sharir 2017 argument, including any logarithmic factors or generic-position assumptions. Use integer determinants for reproducible small-grid counts. No finite construction establishes the conjectured growth.
