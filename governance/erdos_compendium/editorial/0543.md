**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #543 is **DISPROVED**. Erdős asked whether about $\log_2N$ randomly selected elements of any finite abelian group would, with probability at least one-half, generate every group element as a **subset sum**, apart from an error term smaller than $\log\log N$. Jie Ma and Quanyu Tang established in 2026 that a positive multiple of $\log\log p$ is necessary for prime-order groups. Their result includes an explicitly acknowledged human–AI contribution. **Independent critical specialist review remains pending.**

## The question in plain language

Let $G$ be a finite **abelian group** with $N$ elements. Choose a uniformly random **subset of size $k$** $A\subseteq G$. Each element of $A$ is chosen at most once, so this means sampling **without replacement**.

Form the collection of all subset sums, including the sum of the empty subset:

$$
\Sigma(A)=\left\{\sum_{a\in S}a:S\subseteq A\right\}.
$$

The empty subset contributes the identity element $0\in G$. The question is whether $\Sigma(A)$ contains **every** element of $G$.

Define $f(N)$ to be the least $k$ such that, for **every abelian group** $G$ of order $N$, at least half of its uniformly chosen subset of size $k$s satisfy $\Sigma(A)=G$:

$$
\boxed{
\forall G,\ |G|=N:\quad
\Pr_{A\in\binom Gk}\bigl(\Sigma(A)=G\bigr)\ge\tfrac12.
}
$$

Erdős proposed that

$$
\boxed{
f(N)\le\log_2N+o(\log\log N).
}
$$

This says that the extra number of random generators beyond $\log_2 N$ could be **negligible compared with $\log\log N$**. This is false.

This problem is different from #540: there one asks whether **some** nonempty subset has sum zero; here one asks whether **every group element** can be expressed as a subset sum, with a specified success probability for random sets.

## A small example

Take the group $\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z$ and choose

$$
A=\{1,2,4\}.
$$

Its subset sums are

$$
\Sigma(A)=\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}\pmod7
=\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z.
$$

For instance, $3=1+2$, $5=1+4$, $6=2+4$, and $0=1+2+4$ modulo seven, also represented by the empty subset. All seven residues are covered.

Why do roughly $\log_2N$ generators enter? A set with $k$ distinct elements has only $2^k$ subsets, so its subset-sum set satisfies

$$
\boxed{|\Sigma(A)|\le\min(N,2^k).}
$$

To cover all $N$ elements one necessarily needs

$$
\boxed{k\ge\lceil\log_2N\rceil.}
$$

But this is only a **counting obstruction**, not a probability guarantee. Even when $2^k\ge N$, different subsets may have equal sums, leaving some group elements unrepresented.

A finite exact test uses the recurrence

$$
\Sigma(A\cup\{a\})
=\Sigma(A)\cup\bigl(a+\Sigma(A)\bigr),\qquad
\Sigma(\varnothing)=\{0\}.
$$

By applying this to every subset of size $k$ of a small group and counting the full-cover cases, one obtains the exact success probability as a rational number, not a Monte Carlo estimate.

## What is known

### Erdős and Rényi's original probabilistic bound

In his *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), [printed p.127](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=11), Erdős recalls joint work with Alfréd Rényi:

$$
f(N)\le\log_2N+O(\log\log N).
$$

The original page was retrieved and visually inspected. Erdős explicitly conjectured that the $\log\log N$ error term could **not** be replaced by $o(\log\log N)$. The problem catalogue frames the open yes/no question in the opposite direction, so the resolution is a **negative answer**.

The historical source is P. Erdős and A. Rényi, *Probabilistic methods in group theory*, *Journal d'Analyse Mathématique* **14** (1965), 127–138. The full original journal proof was not retrieved here; its bound and role are corroborated in the original 1973 survey and the 2026 first-party paper.

Erdős and Hall later gave a lower-bound obstruction on the smaller $\log\log\log N$ scale. That earlier bound did not by itself establish the positive $\log\log N$ correction predicted by Erdős.

### The 2026 disproof

Jie Ma and Quanyu Tang, *An Erdős problem on random subset sums in finite abelian groups*, [arXiv:2602.05768v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05768), revised March 19, 2026, prove the negative answer quantitatively.

The **complete thirteen-page primary revised preprint** was retrieved and examined. **Theorem 1.2 on PDF p.2** states that for every fixed constant

$$
0<c<\frac1{2\log 2},
$$

and sufficiently large primes $p$, if $A$ is a uniformly random subset of the additive group $\mathbb F_p$ with

$$
k=\left\lfloor\log_2p+c\log\log p\right\rfloor,
$$

then

$$
\boxed{
\Pr\bigl(\Sigma(A)=\mathbb F_p\bigr)\longrightarrow0
\qquad(p\to\infty).
}
$$

Therefore the success probability falls **below one-half**, not above it, even with a positive $\log\log p$ correction. **Corollary 1.3** gives the lower threshold

$$
\boxed{
f(p)\ge\log_2p+
\left(\frac1{2\log 2}+o(1)\right)\log\log p.
}
$$

Here $\log$ is the natural logarithm in the correction, and $\log_2p=(\log p)/\log2$. The theorem is proved for **large primes**, which is enough to refute a proposed bound supposed to work for every $N$ and every abelian group of that order. It does **not** assert an equivalent lower bound for every composite modulus or resolve an exact sharp threshold for all groups.

The paper describes itself explicitly as a **human–AI collaboration**. The authors credit ChatGPT-5.2 Pro with the first qualitative proof idea, while they developed and wrote the stronger quantitative refinement. These authorship details are part of source provenance, not a reason to diminish or exaggerate the theorem.

### Why the probabilistic argument is nontrivial

A direct model samples an unordered set $A$ of $k$ different residues without replacement. The proof first analyzes a more convenient model of **independent uniformly distributed residues**, which may repeat. It carefully transfers results back to the genuine random subset of size $k$ model. Treating the two distributions as identical without this step would change the problem.

The authors track the number of group elements **missed** by all subset sums. If many elements are missed, $\Sigma(A)\ne\mathbb F_p$. Their proof uses factorial moments to obtain Poisson-like estimates for selected missed residues, and then a **second-moment argument** to establish that uncovered residues persist with high probability at the stated $k$.

A central technical ingredient involves matrices of zeroes and ones encoding collections of subset sums. The rank of these matrices over rational numbers is compared with the rank over the finite field $\mathbb F_p$. The proof also bounds intersections between relevant linear spaces and the Boolean cube; a combinatorial lemma controls the low-rank exceptional configurations.

The coupling, the bounded-rank estimates and the growing-order moment calculation are necessary to reach the $\log\log p$ correction. None follows merely from $|\Sigma(A)|\le2^k$.

### What the result does not claim

The upper-bound benchmark and the lower bound now place the universal threshold within the **$\Theta(\log\log N)$ correction scale**, with a positive lower coefficient along prime moduli. This resolves the *original proposed improvement to* $o(\log\log N)$.

It does not determine an exact second-order constant for every finite abelian group. Moreover, the proof is a March 2026 research preprint; GCL has not independently reproduced its full argument as a separate mathematical specialist certification.

The maintained [#543 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/543) correctly labels the problem **DISPROVED**. The 2026 paper strengthens the dated evidence available at the pinned January source revision, but does not authorize silently replacing that protected source lock.
