## The question in plain language

A positive integer is **powerful** when every prime dividing it occurs to exponent at least two. Erdős asked whether infinitely many four-term arithmetic progressions consist of pairwise coprime powerful integers. Unlike the neighbouring problem #936, **the answer here is yes**: Bajpai, Bennett and Chan [BBC24] proved the existence of infinitely many such progressions. A solution of the existence question must not be confused with a claim that all powerful progressions have been classified. [Erdős Problem #937](https://www.erdosproblems.com/937).

## A small example

*Definitions and an important distinction*

A four-term arithmetic progression has the form

$$
a,\quad a+d,\quad a+2d,\quad a+3d,\qquad d>0.
$$

Pairwise coprimality means $\gcd(a+id,a+jd)=1$ for every $0\le i<j\le3$. These conditions rule out the elementary trick of multiplying each term by the same large square: that trick usually introduces a shared prime factor. Fermat's classical theorem forbids four **squares** in arithmetic progression, but powerful numbers need not be squares; a prime exponent of three, for instance, is allowed. Consequently the four-squares obstruction cannot be substituted for the powerful-number question.

There is a useful elementary identity:

$$
\gcd(a+id,a+jd)=\gcd(a+id,(j-i)d).
$$

In particular, if $\gcd(a,d)=1$, then $\gcd(a+id,d)=1$, and hence every pair of terms is coprime when $\gcd(a,d)=1$. Thus in an arithmetic progression, pairwise coprimality is equivalent to $\gcd(a,d)=1$. This is a precise and useful test for a finite verifier.

## What is known

Bajpai, Bennett and Chan [BBC24] proved infinitely many four-term arithmetic progressions of pairwise coprime powerful numbers. They also proved infinitely many three-term progressions of pairwise coprime **3-powerful** numbers (each prime occurs to exponent at least three). The catalogue further records that they proved **conditionally on abc** that for $r\ge4$ there are only finitely many three-term progressions of pairwise coprime $r$‐powerful numbers. These are three logically distinct results, and the abc qualifier applies to the last statement only. [Catalogue statement and bibliography](https://www.erdosproblems.com/937).

## Why the problem was difficult

Classical equations controlling squares are too rigid for this setting, but replacing squares with powerful numbers introduces nonlinear prime-exponent combinations. The common-divisor restriction removes trivial scale-up constructions. The proof of infinitude therefore requires families satisfying both additive equal-spacing relations and multiplicative valuation conditions, rather than merely one verified large example.

## GCL's current state

The current answer to the exact four-term question is **proved**, with a published literature attribution, not a new GCL proof. The coprimality reduction above is elementary. This manuscript does not independently reproduce the Bajpai–Bennett–Chan construction or claim a Lean-checked certificate.
