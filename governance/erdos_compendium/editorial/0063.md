## The question in plain language

A graph needs many colors if adjacent vertices must always have different colors. Erdős and Mihók asked whether a graph needing infinitely many colors must contain cycles whose lengths are powers of two, not merely once, but for infinitely many different powers.

For example, an eight-edge cycle has length $2^3$, and a sixteen-edge cycle has length $2^4$. The question is whether infinitely many such lengths must occur in every graph of infinite chromatic number.

## A small example

A finite graph with chromatic number $r$ has a subgraph of minimum degree at least $r-1$. To see this, repeatedly delete vertices having at most $r-2$ neighbors. If the entire graph disappears, color the deleted vertices in reverse order with $r-1$ colors. Each vertex sees at most $r-2$ previously colored neighbors, so this would color the original graph with $r-1$ colors, a contradiction.

For instance, the complete graph $K_5$ has chromatic number five and minimum degree four. The lemma turns a demand for many colors into a dense finite subgraph. It does not, by itself, produce a cycle of any specified length.

## What is known

The answer is **yes**. Liu and Montgomery proved a much stronger result about even cycle lengths. Their Theorem 1.1 says that a graph of sufficiently large average degree $d$ has every even cycle length in an interval from $(\log\ell)^8$ to $\ell$, for some $\ell\ge d/(10\log^{12}d)$. Their Corollary 1.3 directly treats sequences of even integers growing at most exponentially, including powers of two. See [Liu–Montgomery, arXiv:2010.15802v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.15802v2).

Why does this solve the infinite-chromatic problem? By the de Bruijn–Erdős compactness theorem, an infinitely chromatic graph contains finite subgraphs of arbitrarily large chromatic number. The deletion lemma above extracts finite subgraphs with arbitrarily large average degree. Liu–Montgomery then supplies long intervals of even cycle lengths. For large enough $\ell$, such an interval contains a power of two beyond any chosen bound. Repeating with larger bounds yields infinitely many distinct powers.

## What remains

A bounded formalization task is to prove the finite core lemma and the elementary statement that the interval $[(\log\ell)^8,\ell]$ contains arbitrarily large powers of two as $\ell$ grows. The deep cycle-length theorem should be imported with its precise hypotheses; replacing it by an unproved intuition about dense graphs would be invalid.
