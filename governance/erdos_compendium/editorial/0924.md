## The question in plain language

Ordinary Ramsey theory says that if a complete graph has sufficiently many vertices, then every colouring of its edges with a prescribed finite number of colours contains a complete subgraph whose edges all have the same colour.

Erdős and Hajnal asked whether the host must itself contain a **still larger complete graph**. More precisely, fix integers $k\ge2$ and $\ell\ge3$. Does a finite simple graph $G$ exist with **no clique on $\ell+1$ vertices**, but such that every colouring of the edges of $G$ using $k$ colours contains a monochromatic copy of $K_\ell$?

The answer to [Erdős Problem #924](https://www.erdosproblems.com/924) is **yes**. Folkman proved the two-colour case in 1970. Nešetřil and Rödl proved the case of **arbitrarily many prescribed finite colours** in 1976. The host may be extremely large; the claim is about its existence rather than an efficient construction of the smallest possible example.

## A small example

For two colours and triangles, the task is to find a graph containing **no $K_4$** but such that every red-blue colouring of its existing edges produces a red or blue triangle.

The graph $K_3$ alone does not work: colour one edge red and the other two blue. Neither colour contains a triangle. Nor can a graph on at most five vertices work. In fact the edges of $K_5$ admit a red-blue colouring with no monochromatic triangle: colour the edges of a five-cycle red and the remaining edges blue. Both red and blue graphs are five-cycles. Restricting this colouring to any smaller host graph avoids monochromatic triangles too.

On six vertices this simple complete-graph example no longer works: every red-blue colouring of $K_6$ has a monochromatic triangle, by $R(3,3)=6$. But $K_6$ itself contains $K_4$, so it is **not** an allowed Folkman host. The theorem builds a substantially less obvious sparse-in-cliques host that defeats every colouring without containing a larger forbidden clique.

This distinction is the heart of the result: ordinary Ramsey theory permits a larger host clique; the Folkman problem prohibits one.

## What is known

Jon H. Folkman, [*Graphs with Monochromatic Complete Subgraphs in Every Edge Coloring*](https://doi.org/10.1137/0118004), *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics* **18** (1970), no.1, pp.19–24, defines the minimum possible clique number of a graph whose red-blue edge colourings force a red clique of size $a$ or blue clique of size $b$. His **Theorem 1**, printed p.20, proves that this minimum is exactly $\max(a,b)$. Setting $a=b=\ell$ gives the required two-colour answer.

The original paper is directly available as a [scanned author copy](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/TOPICS/grt/Folkman.pdf). GCL inspected the printed pp.19–20 as page images. The same paper's **Theorem 2** constructs, for every finite graph $F$ and positive integer $r$, a graph $H$ with the **same clique number as $F$** whose every partition of the **vertices** into $r$ classes has a class containing an induced copy of $F$. That is a powerful *vertex-colouring* statement used in the two-colour edge construction, but it is not itself a proof of the full edge-colouring theorem for arbitrary $k$.

In fact, Folkman's closing remarks explicitly leave the analogous edge-Ramsey assertion for **more than two edge colours** as a conjecture. Thus attributing #924 for arbitrary $k$ to Folkman alone would be historically and mathematically inaccurate.

Jaroslav Nešetřil and Vojtěch Rödl, [*The Ramsey property for graphs with forbidden complete subgraphs*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0095-8956(76)90015-0), *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series B* **20** (1976), no.3, pp.243–249, prove the stronger result. Given any finite graph $F$ with clique number $m$ and any positive number $r$ of **edge colours**, there is a finite graph $H$ with the same clique number $m$ such that every edge-colouring of $H$ with $r$ colours contains an **induced copy of $F$ with every edge of that copy monochromatic**. Take $F=K_\ell$ and $r=k$. Then the host has clique number exactly $\ell$ and forces the requisite monochromatic $K_\ell$.

The publisher's [abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0095895676900150) states the general theorem and confirms its assumptions. The [Nešetřil–Rödl 1979 hypergraph sequel](https://dml.cz/handle/10338.dmlcz/101598) independently cites the original 1976 paper with its correct volume, page range and DOI.

A bibliographic trap must be avoided. Folkman also wrote *An upper bound on the chromatic number of a graph*, *Combinatorial Theory and Its Applications* II (1970), pp.437–457, which solves **Erdős #922**. The present #924 uses the **different** 1970 SIAM paper concerning monochromatic edge-colour cliques. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/924) labels #924 **PROVED**, and indicates no independently admitted formalized statement.

## GCL's current state

The general 1976 theorem implies #924 by a straightforward and independently checkable substitution. Let the forbidden clique size be $\ell+1$, let the target finite graph in the Nešetřil–Rödl theorem be $F=K_\ell$, and let the number of host edge colours be $r=k$. The resulting host $H$ has clique number exactly $\ell$. It therefore contains no $K_{\ell+1}$. Every edge-colouring of $H$ with $k$ colours forces an induced monochromatic copy of $F$, hence a monochromatic clique $K_\ell$. This proves the **logical reduction** from an imported general theorem to the source problem.

In contrast, the finite checks do not construct a Folkman host. The exact program at governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_924_925.py independently enumerates all **29,769** three-edge-colourings of complete graphs on one to five vertices in which the first two colours have no monochromatic triangle. It treats the third colour as absence of an edge and verifies a matching independence-number identity, useful for the adjacent #925 problem. The same program enumerates all **32,768** two-colourings of the edges of $K_6$ and verifies none avoids a monochromatic triangle; the five-vertex enumeration contains avoiding examples. These calculations confirm the elementary Ramsey obstruction, not the existence theorem for arbitrary $k,\ell$.

The distinction between **induced** and ordinary copies must also be kept precise. For a target that is itself complete, a monochromatic induced copy in the host is automatically a monochromatic clique. For an arbitrary target $F$, Nešetřil–Rödl's stronger induced-copy clause contains additional information not asked by #924.

## What remains

A bounded formalization task is the **general-to-specific Ramsey transfer**: from the 1976 finite-graph theorem preserving clique number under Ramsey forcing with $k$ edge colours, deduce the exact existential statement of #924 with $\ell\ge3$. Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or a source-normalized Lean theorem without placeholders, with literal axioms, and an explicit record of the distinction between vertex and edge colourings. A constructed host containing $K_{\ell+1}$, a quantifier only over two colours, or confusion of a host copy with a vertex-colour class falsifies the intended interface.

The deeper verification task is independently reconstructing the general Nešetřil–Rödl 1976 proof, including its partite extension, forbidden-clique preservation and induced monochromatic-copy property. A separate task can reproduce Folkman's original 1970 two-colour construction and quantify its enormous finite bounds; neither is certified by exhaustive searches on at most six vertices. Full original proof replay, machine checking and formal-source admission require separate governed evidence.
