## The question in plain language

At a vertex $x$ of a convex polygon, several other vertices may all lie on a circle centered at $x$. Let

$$
m(x)=\max_{r>0}|\{y\in A:y\ne x,\ |x-y|=r\}|.
$$

Erdős #97 asks whether every convex polygon has **at least one vertex** $x$ with $m(x)\le3$. Put plainly: is it impossible for every vertex to have **four** other vertices at some common distance? The distance $r$ may depend on $x$.

The problem is marked **FALSIFIABLE**, not disproved: one finite convex polygon with $m(x)\ge4$ for every vertex would settle it negatively.

## A small example

In a regular pentagon, every vertex sees two neighbours at the side length and two other vertices at the diagonal length. Thus $m(x)=2$ for all five vertices. This shows the conjectured property can hold, but does not prove it for every convex polygon.

The original Erdős conjecture asked for $m(x)\le2$. Danzer constructed a nine-vertex convex polygon with $m(x)\ge3$ at **every** vertex. Fishburn and Reeds subsequently constructed a 20-vertex convex polygon in which each vertex has three others at one fixed common distance. These invalidate the earlier 3-equal-neighbours exclusion, but neither supplies four equal-distance neighbours everywhere.

## What is known

**OPEN, finitely falsifiable.** The maintained [Erdős #97](https://www.erdosproblems.com/97) carefully distinguishes these constructions from the unresolved four-neighbour threshold. In one 1975 paper, Erdős attributed to Danzer a much stronger all-threshold counterexample, but the maintained source notes that the claim was not repeated in later papers and is likely a historical error. It must **not** be cited as a proven disproof.

The source also records the conditional reduction: if a bound $m(x)\le k$ holds at some vertex of every convex polygon, then deleting that vertex and inducting yields an upper bound $kn$ on the number of pairs at a fixed distance. Consequently an affirmative answer to #97 would imply the linear version of #96 with coefficient at most three.

The original source leads are Erdős's 1946, 1975 and 1987 papers and Fishburn–Reeds, *Unit distances between vertices of a convex polygon*, *Computational Geometry* (1992), 81–91. Their exact coordinates and full proof have not been independently retrieved for this audit.

## GCL's current state

The pinned catalogue classifies #97 as **FALSIFIABLE** and the maintained page agrees. The original assignment is [#1366](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1366). We preserve the distinction between the superseded three-neighbour claim and the still-open four-neighbour claim. The strong historical Danzer attribution is explicitly unresolved.

The original issue contains an authenticated [RESULT/1 source report](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1366#issuecomment-6161022378). This is unadjudicated source evidence only, not independent mathematical certification or editorial admission.

## What remains

A bounded successor is to implement an exact checker on points with rational coordinates: test strict convex position, and for each vertex group squared distances to the other vertices. Accept a refutation only when **every** vertex has at least four others at a single common squared distance. Otherwise report a finite fixture, not confirmation of the universal statement. The exact Danzer nine-point and Fishburn–Reeds twenty-point coordinates remain a separate primary-source replication task.
