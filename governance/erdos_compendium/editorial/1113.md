## The question in plain language

Take an odd positive integer $m$. If

$$
2^k m+1
$$

is composite for **every integer $k\ge0$**, then $m$ is called a *Sierpiński number*.

Some Sierpiński numbers have a simple certificate: a **finite collection of primes** so that every member of the sequence is divisible by at least one of those primes. This is called a *finite prime covering*.

[Erdős Problem #1113](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1113) asks whether there can be a Sierpiński number **without any finite prime covering**. The existence of numbers with finite coverings is already known; the possible absence of such a certificate is the hard question.

## A small example

The classical number

$$
m=78557
$$

has a finite prime covering

$$
P=\{3,5,7,13,19,37,73\}.
$$

This can be checked exactly, without testing infinitely many exponents. The multiplicative orders of two modulo these primes are respectively

$$
2,4,3,12,18,36,9.
$$

A particular prime divides $2^k m+1$ when $k$ lies in one residue class modulo its order. For the seven primes these classes are respectively $0,1,1,11,15,27,3$.

Their common period is $\mathrm{lcm}(2,4,3,12,18,36,9)=36$. Direct modular arithmetic verifies that **all 36 residue classes** of $k$ are covered.

Since $2^k78557+1$ exceeds every covering prime, the divisor is proper. Therefore every expression is composite, for *all* $k\ge0$. This is a rigorous example of a Sierpiński number **with** a finite covering, not an example answering Erdős's open question.

## What is known

Sierpiński (1960) proved that infinitely many such numbers exist, using covering constructions.

The maintained catalogue reports that Izotov (1995) proved a much larger fourth-power candidate was a Sierpiński number. Filaseta, Finch and Kozek (2008) discussed why it might have **no** finite covering, but the evidence reported is not a proof that no such covering exists.

They also proposed a refined possibility: perhaps a Sierpiński number either is a perfect power or has a finite covering. That is a different unresolved statement.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2384](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2384#issuecomment-6168858040) verifies the entire 36-residue covering certificate using exact modular arithmetic.

The [frozen formal-conjectures source](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/1113.lean) distinguishes the known infinitely-many theorem from the open no-cover statement. Its proof declarations have not been independently kernel-replayed.

## What remains

Read the original Erdős–Graham, Izotov and Filaseta–Finch–Kozek proofs. In particular, distinguish a proof that *every term is composite* from a proof that *no finite family of primes can cover all terms*. The latter is a substantially stronger claim and remains open.
