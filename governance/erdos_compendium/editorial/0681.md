## The question in plain language

How soon after an arbitrary large integer $n$ can we find a **composite number all of whose prime factors are much larger than the distance traveled**? [Erdős #681](https://www.erdosproblems.com/681) asks whether, for every sufficiently large $n$, some integer $k>0$ satisfies $n+k$ composite and $p(n+k)>k^2$, where $p(r)$ is the least prime factor. The composite requirement is essential. Problem #680 asks a related question but allows prime values of $n+k$.

## A small example

For $n=8,k=1$, the number $n+k=9=3^2$ is composite and its least prime factor is $3>1^2$, so it is a valid witness. In contrast, for $n=10,k=1$, the candidate $11$ is prime and therefore **does not qualify** for #681, even though its least prime factor is large. A small example cannot prove the universal assertion.

## What is known

If $r=n+k$ is composite with smallest prime divisor $p(r)>k^2$, write $r=p(r)q$. The other factor $q$ is at least $p(r)$, so $n+k=r\geq p(r)^2>k^4$. Any composite witness must satisfy this simple size inequality. The converse fails: a large number can still have a very small prime factor. This gives a bounded search region for $k$, but not a proof of existence. The maintained [source](https://www.erdosproblems.com/681) describes an Erdős–Eggleton–Selfridge question and suggests that perhaps $k^2$ could be replaced by $k^d$ for any fixed $d$. That is explicitly conjectural.

## What remains

A useful bounded investigation starts with the necessary window $k^4<n+k$, uses exact integer factorization to generate finite composite rough-number fixtures, and then asks whether a named sieve or almost-prime theorem controls suitable numbers in those windows for every sufficiently large $n$. A finite search or a prime-only argument cannot resolve the question. Complete original-source review, editorial critical review, protected merge, deployed mathematical typesetting readback and V2 admission remain due.
