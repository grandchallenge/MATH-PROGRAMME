## The question in plain language

A positive integer can have many divisors, but how evenly are those divisors spread across the number line? Erdős Problem #448 organizes the positive integers into **dyadic intervals**: each interval is twice as wide as the preceding one,

$$
[1,2),\ [2,4),\ [4,8),\ [8,16),\ldots.
$$

For a positive integer $n$, write $\tau(n)$ for its number of positive divisors. Let $\tau^+(n)$ be the number of these dyadic intervals containing **at least one divisor of** $n$.

Erdős asked whether, for **every fixed** $\varepsilon>0$, almost every positive integer satisfies

$$
\tau^+(n)<\varepsilon\tau(n).
$$

“Almost every” has a precise density meaning: among integers $1,\ldots,N$, the proportion failing the inequality should tend to zero as $N\to\infty$. The question is not whether the inequality holds for every integer, nor whether it holds for a few very highly composite integers.

There is a natural reason to suspect a small ratio: many divisors might share the same doubling interval, so the number of *occupied intervals* could be much smaller than the total number of divisors. The conjecture proposed that the ratio eventually becomes arbitrarily small for nearly all integers. **That conjecture is false.**

## A small example

Consider $n=12$. Its divisors are

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12.
$$

They occupy the intervals

| Dyadic interval | Divisors of $12$ |
|---|---|
| $[1,2)$ | $1$ |
| $[2,4)$ | $2,3$ |
| $[4,8)$ | $4,6$ |
| $[8,16)$ | $12$ |

Thus $\tau(12)=6$ but $\tau^+(12)=4$. The ratio is $2/3$, not zero. Similarly, for $n=6$ the divisors $1,2,3,6$ occupy three intervals, giving

$$
\tau(6)=4,\qquad \tau^+(6)=3.
$$

An elementary observation holds for **every** positive integer:

$$
1\leq\tau^+(n)\leq\tau(n).
$$

Indeed, send each divisor $d$ to the integer $\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor$. The resulting set of indices has exactly $\tau^+(n)$ members, and an image of a finite set cannot have more members than the set itself.

These computations establish the definitions and provide reproducible checks. They do **not** establish a theorem about what happens for almost all large integers. A handful of examples, no matter how large, cannot prove or disprove a density-one assertion.

## What is known

The original problem appears on printed page 89 of Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980).

In 1981, Erdős and Gérald Tenenbaum published a theorem that refutes the conjecture. In *Sur la structure de la suite des diviseurs d'un entier*, printed page 18, they define the same function $\tau^+(n)$ and state the conjecture as **C4**. Their **Theorem 1**, on printed page 19, is more informative than a simple negative answer.

Fix any real $\eta>0$. The theorem supplies a constant $C_\eta>0$ such that, for every $0<\alpha\leq1$,

$$
\overline d\bigl(\{n:\tau^+(n)\leq\alpha\tau(n)\}\bigr)
\leq C_\eta\alpha^{1-\eta}.
$$

Here $\overline d$ is the *upper natural density*, the limiting upper proportion among positive integers. For example, taking $\eta=1/2$ shows that the upper density of integers with an unusually small occupied-bin ratio is at most $C_{1/2}\sqrt\alpha$. As $\alpha$ approaches zero, this upper bound approaches zero.

Why does that disprove Erdős's conjecture? Choose $\alpha>0$ sufficiently small that $C_{1/2}\sqrt\alpha<1$. If the inequality $\tau^+(n)<\alpha\tau(n)$ held for almost all integers, its set would have density one. But the theorem says even the larger set with $\leq$ has upper density strictly below one. That is impossible.

This implication is an elementary logical deduction from Erdős and Tenenbaum's published theorem. The theorem itself is a substantial analytic-number-theory result and is credited to its original authors.

Later Hall–Tenenbaum work (*Divisors*, 1988, §4.6) studies finer distribution questions. Kevin Ford's 2008 *Annals of Mathematics* paper gives estimates for related divisor-in-interval counting functions. Those results are relevant developments, but their exact quantitative statements should be quoted only against their corresponding hypotheses.

## What remains

The mathematical status of the original conjecture is settled: **disproved**. Remaining documentary and formal work includes a complete source comparison with the sharper Hall–Tenenbaum results and an independent formal replay of the density-theoretic bridge from the 1981 theorem to the negative answer.

A useful bounded task is to encode the inequality on upper density with its strict and non-strict versions, verify that the formal `answer(False)` convention means the negation of the original density-one claim, and run the resulting proof under a recorded Lean/mathlib toolchain. That work would improve the reliability of the formal representation; it is not a new resolution of the original problem.
