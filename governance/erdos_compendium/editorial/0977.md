## The question in plain language

For each integer \(m>1\), let \(P^+(m)\) denote its **greatest prime divisor**. Erdős asked whether the number
\[
P^+(2^n-1)
\]
must eventually be arbitrarily large compared with the **exponent \(n\)**:
\[
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P^+(2^n-1)}{n}=+\infty.}
\]
The answer is **YES: this is a proved theorem**, due to Cameron L. Stewart. It is not merely known to be true for infinitely many exponents or for almost all exponents. Stewart established an explicit lower bound for **all sufficiently large \(n\)** that forces the displayed ratio to diverge. The maintained [Erdős Problem #977](https://www.erdosproblems.com/977) and protected GCL registry correctly classify it **PROVED**.

The normalization is an essential part of the claim. A prime factor exceeding \(n\), or exceeding \(2n\), does **not** by itself prove that the ratio tends to infinity; the factor must exceed **every fixed multiple** of \(n\) once \(n\) is large enough.

## Small exact examples

The following values can be obtained by exact integer factorization:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
 n&2^n-1&\text{prime factorization}&P^+(2^n-1)\\
 \hline
 3&7&7&7\\
 4&15&3\cdot5&5\\
 5&31&31&31\\
 6&63&3^2\cdot7&7\\
 8&255&3\cdot5\cdot17&17\\
 10&1023&3\cdot11\cdot31&31\\
 12&4095&3^2\cdot5\cdot7\cdot13&13
\end{array}
\]
The large-prime-factor function **is not monotone in \(n\)**. For example the value drops from \(31\) at \(n=5\) to \(7\) at \(n=6\). Thus finite computations or monotonicity heuristics cannot establish the limit.

These checks also show that the theorem's phrase **sufficiently large** cannot be deleted. Stewart's exponential factor becomes meaningful in its final asymptotic range, not as a claimed numerical inequality at all small exponents.

## The theorem that resolves the problem

Cameron L. Stewart, *On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers*, **Acta Mathematica 211** (2013), pp.291–314, DOI [10.1007/s11511-013-0105-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11511-013-0105-y), proves in his principal theorem and its integer specialization that, for fixed integers \(a>b>0\),
\[
P^+(a^n-b^n)>
n\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{104\log\log n}\right)
\]
for every sufficiently large \(n\), with a threshold depending on the fixed bases (and the relevant arithmetic parameters). Specializing **\(a=2\), \(b=1\)** gives
\[
\boxed{
P^+(2^n-1)>
n\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{104\log\log n}\right).
}
\]
The theorem is unconditional. Its proof is a deep number-theoretic result; it uses estimates for complex and \(p\)-adic linear forms in logarithms to control prime valuations of cyclotomic factors. The bound is not obtained from elementary Fermat congruences alone. [Published-author PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/pure-mathematics/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/s11511-013-0105-y.pdf); [journal bibliographic record](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11511-013-0105-y).

The implication to Erdős's question is short and complete:
\[
\frac{P^+(2^n-1)}n
>
\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{104\log\log n}\right).
\]
Since \(\log n/\log\log n\to+\infty\), the right side tends to infinity. Therefore the **full limit**, not only an unbounded subsequence, follows.

An equivalent catalogue shorthand is
\[
P^+(2^n-1)\gg
n^{1+1/(104\log\log n)}.
\]
The multiplicative implicit constant and the precise inequality in the paper should not be confused: the displayed strict version above comes directly from Stewart's result. The exponent increment \(1/(104\log\log n)\) tends to zero, but the gain over \(n\) still diverges because it is multiplied by \(\log n\) when one takes logarithms.

## What was known earlier

A. Schinzel [Sc62] proved
\[
P^+(2^n-1)>2n\qquad(n>12).
\]
This is a uniform **linear** lower bound, not Erdős's divergence conclusion. In 1974/75, Stewart had already proved the desired divergence for exponents \(n\) with fewer than
\[
\frac{\log\log n}{\log2}
\]
distinct prime factors. That result left many exponents outside its hypothesis. The 2013 theorem removed the restricted-exponent condition and handled **every sufficiently large exponent**.

The factorization of \(2^n-1\) into cyclotomic values
\[
2^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(2)
\]
is an exact identity. It explains why cyclotomic factors occur in the proof, but it is not enough by itself to show that one of these factors has a prime divisor of size \(\gg n\exp(\log n/(104\log\log n))\).

Another exact elementary identity is
\[
\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1
\quad(a,b\ge1).
\]
Its proof uses the Euclidean algorithm on the exponents: if \(a>b\), subtracting \(2^{a-b}(2^b-1)\) from \(2^a-1\) gives \(2^{a-b}-1\); repeating reduces to the greatest common divisor of the exponents. This is a useful exact check of the **divisibility-sequence** structure, but still does not yield Stewart's quantitative estimate.

## Why this is not an \(n^{1+c}\) theorem

A possible misreading is that the result gives some fixed \(c>0\) with
\[
P^+(2^n-1)\gg n^{1+c}.
\]
It does **not**. The Stewart exponent is
\[
1+\frac1{104\log\log n},
\]
whose additive term tends to zero. Thus the published theorem has the structure
\[
n^{1+o(1)}
\]
with an **unbounded** multiplicative factor beyond \(n\). This is perfectly sufficient for Erdős's limit conjecture, but it is not a fixed positive-power gain.

The factor \(104\) comes from the quality of the input estimates for \(p\)-adic logarithmic forms, not an arithmetic constant conjectured to be optimal. Optimizing this factor is distinct from solving the already resolved yes/no statement.

## The related factorial problem is still open

Erdős separately asked whether
\[
\frac{P^+(n!+1)}n\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
The same 2013 result for \(2^n-1\) **does not imply** this assertion. The integer \(n!+1\) is congruent to \(1\) modulo each integer \(2,\ldots,n\), so all its prime factors exceed \(n\), but that elementary observation gives only a lower ratio around one.

Murty and Wong [MuWo02] proved, **assuming the unproved \(abc\) conjecture**, that
\[
P^+(n!+1)>(1+o(1))n\log n,
\]
which would imply the desired limit under that hypothesis. The strongest unconditional conclusion listed in the catalogue is due to L. Lai [La21]:
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P^+(n!+1)}n
\ge1+9\log2\approx7.2383.
\]
A **finite limsup lower bound** is much weaker than divergence to infinity. Neither it nor the conditional \(abc\) statement is an unconditional resolution of Erdős's factorial question. [Maintained problem #977 and related remarks](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/977).

## Source provenance and formalization

The primary proof is a 2013 **refereed journal theorem**. Stewart's arXiv manuscript [1008.1274](https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.1274), posted in 2010, numbers the result differently from the final Acta Mathematica article. In the published version it is Theorem 1.1 with numbered equations (1.7)–(1.8); the author preprint uses Theorem 1 and equations (7)–(8). An editorial source lock must give the **version-specific theorem locator**, not transfer equation numbers silently.

The source's general Lucas–Lehmer theorem has algebraic hypotheses involving \(\alpha,\beta\), but the published paper explicitly draws a **separate direct specialization** to integers \(a>b>0\). That direct assertion authorizes \(a=2,b=1\). An attempted proof that merely substitutes a Lucas-sequence formula requiring \(\alpha\beta\ne0\) must check the specialization rather than silently violate a hypothesis. [Independent theorem comparison](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/library/arithmetic_functions/stewart_2013_divisors_lucas_lehmer).

The catalogue's public page marks the answer **PROVED**, but currently lists **no formalized statement**. The protected EC-BULK-006 task [ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0977-S01](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2248) records older statement-formalized metadata as yes and the older admitted Formal Conjectures provider as lacking the relevant file. This is a provenance discrepancy to reconcile; it is **not** evidence that the published theorem has been independently formalized or kernel-replayed by GCL.
