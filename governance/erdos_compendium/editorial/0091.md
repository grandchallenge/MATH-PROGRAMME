## The question in plain language

Among all sets of $n$ distinct points in the Euclidean plane, some use as few different pairwise distances as possible. Call such configurations **distance-minimizing**. Two configurations are similar if one can be obtained from the other by a Euclidean isometry followed by a positive uniform rescaling.

Erdős #91 asks whether, for all sufficiently large $n$, there exist at least two distance-minimizing configurations that are **not similar**. This asks about the *uniqueness of minimizers*, not about the minimum number of distances itself.

## A small example

For $n=3$, the equilateral triangle uniquely minimizes the number of distances up to similarity: it has just one nonzero distance. For $n=4$, a square and two equilateral triangles sharing an edge both determine exactly two distances. In the square their ratio is $\sqrt2$, while in the triangle-pair configuration their ratio is $\sqrt3$. The two configurations therefore cannot be similar.

These contrasting finite cases show why the statement includes the phrase “sufficiently large.” Neither uniqueness at $n=3$ nor nonuniqueness at $n=4$ decides eventual nonuniqueness.

## What is known

The eventual-nonuniqueness conjecture remains **OPEN**. The maintained [Erdős #91](https://www.erdosproblems.com/91), edited 13 April 2026, reports a unique minimizer for $n=5$ (the regular pentagon, which has two distances) and points to a published 2024 proof by Kovács. Erdős had also recorded non-similar optimal configurations for $6\le n\le9$.

The extremal **value** of the minimum number of distances is the subject of #89, and bounds for that value are insufficient to classify all equality cases. FormalConjectures' protected [ErdosProblems/91.lean](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/91.lean) defines optimality and similarity, with a conjecture about eventual failure of uniqueness. Its proof holes and finite-variant statements are not kernel certifications.

## What remains

A bounded successor is to prove that the square and the pair of equilateral triangles both minimize the number of different distances for four distinct points, and that the two are non-similar. Acceptance: exactly two distinct positive squared distances in each example, a proof that a four-point planar set cannot have all six pairwise distances equal, and distinct distance ratios. Stop at $n=4$; this cannot imply eventual nonuniqueness.
