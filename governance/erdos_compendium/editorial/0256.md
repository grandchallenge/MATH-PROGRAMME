## The question in plain language

Take positive integers $a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_n$, repetitions allowed, and form the polynomial

$$
P_a(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-z^{a_i}).
$$

On the unit circle $|z|=1$, let $M(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ be the largest value of $|P_a(z)|$. Erdős and Szekeres ask how small this largest value can be when the exponents are chosen as cleverly as possible. A safe extremal definition is

$$
f(n)=\inf_{1\leq a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_n} \max_{|z|=1}\left|\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-z^{a_i})\right|.
$$

The old wording says that $f(n)$ is “maximal” subject to being a lower bound for every tuple; Atkinson writes “greatest lower bound,” while the 1959 paper writes “minimum.” These formulations express the intended extremal quantity, but the infimum notation avoids assuming that an optimal tuple is attained.

The specific question asks whether there are constants $c>0$ and an implicit positive constant in the comparison such that

$$
\log f(n)\gg n^c
$$

as $n$ tends to infinity. In plain language: must the smallest possible worst-case size still grow faster than every fixed power of $n$ after taking logarithms? The answer to that specific question is no. That negative answer does not determine the true order of $f(n)$.

It is important not to replace the original problem by its stricter cousin. The quantity $f^*(n)$ requires $a_1<\cdots<a_n$, so it forbids repeated exponents. Results about $f^*(n)$ do not automatically answer the repeated-exponent problem for $f(n)$.

## A small example

A fully checkable example comes from the tuple $(a_1,a_2)=(1,2)$. Write $z=e^{it}$ and $x=\cos t$. Then

$$
|1-z|^2=2(1-x),\qquad |1-z^2|^2=4(1-x)(1+x),
$$

so

$$
|(1-z)(1-z^2)|^2=8(1-x)^2(1+x),\qquad -1\leq x\leq 1.
$$

Differentiating the last cubic shows that its interior maximum occurs at $x=-1/3$; both endpoints give zero. Therefore

$$
\max_{|z|=1}|(1-z)(1-z^2)|=\frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}\approx 3.079201435678004.
$$

This gives the rigorous finite conclusion

$$
f(2)\leq\frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}.
$$

It is only an upper bound because $f(2)$ is an infimum over every pair of positive integer exponents; it does not prove that $(1,2)$ is optimal. The calculation is independently reproducible from the displayed identity, and the original source records the same extremal setup. As an even simpler check, for $n=1$ and any positive $a_1$, the values $z^{a_1}$ run around the unit circle, so the maximum of $|1-z^{a_1}|$ is $2$ and $f(1)=2$.

## What is known

The classical 1959 paper of Erdős and Szekeres proves

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} f(n)^{1/n}=1
$$

and the general lower bound $f(n)\geq\sqrt{2n}$. The limit says that $f(n)$ is subexponential in $n$ on the scale of its nth root; it does not say that $f(n)$ is small in an absolute or polynomial sense.

The published upper-bound progression is substantial. Atkinson obtained $\log f(n)=O(n^{1/2}\log n)$, and Odlyzko improved this to

$$
\log f(n)=O\bigl(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{4/3}\bigr).
$$

Belov and Konyagin later proved, through their estimates for nonnegative trigonometric polynomials together with the relevant transfer, that

$$
\log f(n)=O\bigl((\log n)^4\bigr).
$$

Since $(\log n)^4=o(n^c)$ for every fixed $c>0$, this published result settles the specific question negatively. The current Erdős Problems page reports this conclusion and cites the 1996 paper.

For the lower side, the original $\sqrt{2n}$ bound stood for decades. Quanyu Tang’s 2025 preprint, listed with a 2026 *Proceedings of the AMS* publication reference, reports the improvement $f(n)\geq 2\sqrt n$. This strengthens what is known but remains far below the logarithmic-power upper bound. The broad question of the correct growth rate, and of substantially stronger lower bounds, is currently unresolved.

## What remains

The yes/no question about a lower bound of the form $\log f(n)\gg n^c$ is settled negatively by the externally published Belov–Konyagin result. The open mathematical work is finer: determine the actual scale of $f(n)$, improve the lower bound, and reconcile the best upper and lower estimates. The gap between

$$
2\sqrt n\leq f(n)\leq \exp\!\bigl(O((\log n)^4)\bigr)
$$

is enormous, and no statement here claims that either endpoint is close to the truth.

Formalization has a separate unfinished path. A future GCL task would first need a pinned formal statement with the exact infimum, positive-integer domain, nondecreasing tuple, and repeated-exponent convention. It would then need a source-faithful formal bridge for any use of the Belov–Konyagin trigonometric-polynomial theorem. A formal statement or a finite check would not, by itself, certify the deep published argument.
