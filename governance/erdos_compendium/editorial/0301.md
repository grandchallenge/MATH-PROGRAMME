## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 301 asks how large a set of small positive integers can be while avoiding all nontrivial Egyptian-fraction identities internal to the set. For a positive integer $N$, let $f(N)$ be the largest size of a set $A$ contained in ${1,\ldots,N\}$ for which there do not exist an integer $k\geq 1$ and pairwise distinct integers $a,b_1,\ldots,b_k$ in $A$ satisfying

$$
\frac{1}{a}=\frac{1}{b_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{b_k}.
$$

The case $k=1$ is automatically impossible because $a$ and $b_1$ must be distinct, so the substance begins with two or more summands. The question is to estimate $f(N)$ as $N$ grows. Its most pointed form asks whether

$$
f(N)=\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)N.
$$

Here $o(1)$ means a quantity tending to zero as $N$ tends to infinity. Thus the conjectural answer says that the largest avoiding set has asymptotic density one half: it may be a little larger or smaller than $N/2$ at any particular finite $N$, but its relative excess should disappear.

## A small example

Take $N=6$ and $A=\{4,5,6\}$. This is the upper half of the interval, and it has three elements. An exact check is short because, for each possible left-hand denominator, there are only two other elements. For $a=4$, the possible sums using at least one distinct denominator are $1/5$, $1/6$, and $1/5+1/6=11/30$; none is $1/4$. For $a=5$, the corresponding values are $1/4$, $1/6$, and $1/4+1/6=5/12$, none equal to $1/5$. For $a=6$, they are $1/4$, $1/5$, and $1/4+1/5=9/20$, none equal to $1/6$.

This is reproducible exact arithmetic rather than a decimal approximation. The complete finite check can be written as:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations

A = (4, 5, 6)
relations = []
for a in A:
    others = tuple(b for b in A if b != a)
    for k in range(1, len(others) + 1):
        for bs in combinations(others, k):
            s = sum((Fraction(1, b) for b in bs), Fraction(0))
            if s == Fraction(1, a):
                relations.append((a, bs))
print(relations)
```

The verified output is `[]`. This establishes only that this particular finite set is avoiding, and hence gives the elementary lower bound $f(6)\geq 3$. It says nothing about the asymptotic estimate, nor does it show that the upper-half construction is always optimal.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), especially the Unit Fractions discussion on printed pages 32 and 36–37. There the authors introduce families of finite sets whose elements have reciprocal sum one and ask how large a subset of ${1,\ldots,n\}$ can avoid such a configuration. The maintained Problem 301 wording is a modern normalization of that question, with the quantifiers made explicit.

One basic construction is $A=(N/2,N]\cap\mathbb{N}$, which the maintained entry records as giving $f(N)\geq N/2$ (with the harmless integer-rounding interpretation at odd $N$). Intuitively, the construction removes the small denominators that make short reciprocal identities easiest; the exact finite example above illustrates the same direction without relying on intuition.

The maintained entry also records an elementary upper bound attributed to Wouter van Doorn: $f(N)\leq (25/28+o(1))N$. Its described mechanism partitions suitable multiples into disjoint sets $S_a=\{2a,3a,4a,6a,12a\}\cap[1,N]$, where $a$ ranges over numbers of the form $8^b9^cd$ with $(d,6)=1$. An avoiding set must omit enough elements from each such block, and summing those omissions yields the stated constant. This is a live-site report of attributed progress, not a result independently certified in this article.

These bounds leave a substantial gap between one half and twenty-five twenty-eighths. The live page says the problem is open and cannot be resolved by a finite computation alone. A computation can test particular $N$ and suggest structure, but the requested asymptotic statement concerns all sufficiently large $N$.

## What remains

The central unresolved task is an asymptotic upper bound of $(1/2+o(1))N$, or a counterexample showing that the proposed constant is wrong. Before a theorem-level campaign, the smallest useful source task is to lock the exact distinctness convention and turn the block argument behind $25/28$ into a finite, checkable packing lemma. A successor worker could then test the lemma for explicit ranges of $N$, identify exactly which omitted elements are forced by each reciprocal identity, and record whether the finite inequalities support an improved constant. Such checks would be evidence for a route, not a proof of the asymptotic claim.

Any stronger claim needs a primary proof or a complete new argument. In particular, the existence of a finite avoiding set, an integer-programming optimum at selected $N$, or a formal statement in Lean would not by itself establish the limit. The semantic distinction between distinct and non-distinct $b_i$ is essential: the live page notes that allowing repetition changes the extremal problem and gives a different half-density threshold.
