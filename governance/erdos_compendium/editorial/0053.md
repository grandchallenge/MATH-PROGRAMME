## The question in plain language

Given a finite set of integers $A$, form all sums of **distinct** elements of $A$ and all products of distinct elements. Empty choices can contribute 0 to the sums and 1 to the products. [Erdős Problem #53](https://www.erdosproblems.com/53) asks whether, for every fixed positive integer $t$, any sufficiently large set $A$ generates at least $|A|^t$ different numbers in the union of these two collections.

The answer is **yes**, established in the work of Mei-Chu Chang. The theorem concerns sums and products of *different elements*, not only the usual two-term sumset and product set.

## A small example

Take the five-element set

$$
A=\{1,2,4,8,16\}.
$$

Every subset has a distinct sum, so there are 32 subset-sum values, namely 0 through 31. Every subset product is a power of two, with exponents from 0 through 10, giving 11 distinct products. Exactly five of those products already occur among the subset sums. Thus the union contains

$$
32+11-5=38
$$

distinct integers. This is one exact calculation, not the asymptotic theorem for every large set.

## What is known

Chang's *The Erdős–Szemerédi problem on sum set and product set*, *Annals of Mathematics* **157** (2003), 939–957, proves a superpolynomial lower bound for the **sum of the cardinalities** of the subset-sum and subset-product collections. The growth is much faster than $|A|^t$ for every fixed $t$. Her later paper, *On sums and products of distinct numbers* (2004), extends related superpolynomial results to complex sets.

There is a small but important counting step. If the two collections have $S$ and $P$ elements, the size of their union is at least $\max(S,P)$, hence at least $(S+P)/2$. Therefore a source theorem yielding $S+P>2|A|^t$ suffices for Erdős's union bound. Because Chang's bound is superpolynomial, that stronger inequality holds for sufficiently large $|A|$. Ignoring the factor of two would leave an unjustified implication.

## What remains

The original problem is solved. Additional work can formalize Chang's theorem as an explicit external premise, prove the cardinality transfer, and check examples that include negative numbers, zero, and empty subsets. A finite calculation alone does not establish the result for every sufficiently large set.
