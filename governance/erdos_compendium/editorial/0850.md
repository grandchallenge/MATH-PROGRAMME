## The question in plain language

For two distinct integers to have the same prime divisors at three consecutive shifts is much stronger than matching only at two shifts. Known two-shift examples do not solve the three-shift Erdős–Woods problem.

Do there exist distinct positive integers x≠y such that for each i=0,1,2, the sets of prime factors of x+i and y+i are equal? This asks equality of radicals at THREE consecutive shifts, not just equality of number of factors or two shifts. No unconditional full answer is known; Shorey–Tijdeman report a conditional NO under Baker's strong ABC.

## A small example

Exact factorization gives radical(75)=radical(1215)=15 and radical(76)=radical(1216)=38, but radical(77)=77 and radical(1217)=1217 (1217 prime). Therefore known example does not answer three-shift problem.

This finite calculation tests definitions, not a result quantified over all large N or infinite sets.

## What is known

Maintained https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/850 read 2026-10-14: x=2(2^r-1), y=x(x+2) gives a TWO-shift family; Makowski [Ma68] gives 75,1215 since 75=3·5²,1215=3^5·5 and 76=2²·19,1216=2^6·19. For shift two, 77=7·11 and 1217 is a prime with different prime factors. Source status OPEN for all three shifts. Shorey–Tijdeman [ShTi16] conditional NO depends on strong Baker ABC, which must never be stated unconditionally.

Write $\\mathrm{rad}(n)$ for the product of the DISTINCT primes dividing positive $n$. Equality of prime-factor sets is equivalent to equality of radicals. Makowski's example gives

$$

\\mathrm{rad}(75)=\\mathrm{rad}(1215)=15,\qquad
\\mathrm{rad}(76)=\\mathrm{rad}(1216)=38.

$$

The next shift fails: $77=7\cdot11$ whereas $1217$ is prime. Thus the known example is evidence for two shifts only. Shorey–Tijdeman's negative theorem assumes a strong ABC-type conjecture and is not unconditional.
