## The question in plain language

Consider a sequence of positive integers that grows by a fixed proportion at every step. Such a sequence is called *lacunary*. For instance,

$$
1,2,4,8,16,32,\ldots
$$

is lacunary because each term is twice its predecessor. More generally, an increasing sequence $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ is lacunary if there is a fixed $\varepsilon>0$ such that

$$
a_{k+1}\geq(1+\varepsilon)a_k
$$

for every $k$. Its successive terms become increasingly far apart.

For a real number $\theta$, look at the **fractional parts** of $\theta a_k$: that is, the positions of $\theta a_k$ after subtracting their integer parts. Every fractional part lies on the unit circle $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. A sequence is *dense modulo one* if it visits every nonempty open arc, however small.

Erdős asked whether **every lacunary sequence** admits an **irrational** multiplier $\theta$ for which the fractional parts $\{\theta a_k\}$ are **not** dense modulo one.

The answer is **yes**. In fact, established results allow the multiplier to keep *all* the sampled positions a fixed positive distance away from the integers. The argument is about selecting a special real multiplier for a given integer sequence. It does **not** say that every irrational multiplier has this property.

There is a subtle but consequential difference between two notations. The fractional part $\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ lies in $[0,1)$. The distance to the nearest integer,

$$
\|x\|=\min_{m\in\mathbb Z}|x-m|,
$$

always lies in $[0,1/2]$. Thus the literal claim that a collection of *nearest-integer distances* is not dense in $[0,1]$ is trivially true for **every** multiplier. That is not the substance of Erdős's historical question. The nontrivial result concerns nondensity of fractional parts modulo one, or the stronger uniform separation $\inf_k\|\theta a_k\|>0$.

## A small example

A single explicit example makes the geometry concrete. Use the lacunary sequence $a_k=2^k$. We will construct an irrational real number $\theta$ whose multiples $2^k\theta$ stay at least $1/8$ away from every integer.

Write $\theta$ in binary as

$$
\theta=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_j2^{-(j+1)}.
$$

Group the binary digits into consecutive pairs $(b_{2j},b_{2j+1})$. Choose

$$
(b_{2j},b_{2j+1})=
\begin{cases}
(1,0),&j\text{ is a perfect square},\\
(0,1),&j\text{ is not a perfect square}.
\end{cases}
$$

The beginning of the expansion is

$$
\theta=0.10100101100101010110\ldots{}_2.
$$

Every designated pair is either $10$ or $01$. Therefore **no three consecutive binary digits can all be the same**: every block of three includes at least one complete pair, which has different digits.

Multiplication by $2^k$ shifts a binary expansion left by $k$ places. The fractional part $\{2^k\theta\}$ therefore begins with a three-digit word that is neither $000$ nor $111$. Consequently,

$$
\frac18\leq\{2^k\theta\}\leq\frac78,
\qquad
\|2^k\theta\|\geq\frac18
$$

for **every** $k\geq0$.

Why is this multiplier irrational? A real number has an eventually periodic binary expansion if and only if it is rational. If our expansion became eventually periodic, the subsequence of even-position digits $b_{2j}$ would also become eventually periodic. But $b_{2j}$ is exactly the indicator of the square integers: it has infinitely many $1$ s separated by gaps of unbounded length. Such a sequence cannot be eventually periodic. Hence $\theta$ is irrational.

This is a complete construction for **one** lacunary sequence. It illustrates the conclusion but does not prove it for every possible lacunary sequence.

## What is known

The original question appears in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 92. The authors ask about an irrational $\alpha$ for which the fractional parts

$$
\{\alpha a_k-\lfloor\alpha a_k\rfloor:k\geq1\}
$$

fail to be dense, assuming a fixed ratio $a_{k+1}/a_k\geq c>1$. They report that the conjecture had recently been settled.

The historical resolution is attributed to **Andrew Pollington** (1979) and **Bernard de Mathan** (1980), who worked independently. Their results show that lacunarity provides multipliers avoiding a neighborhood of the integers modulo one. Pollington's stronger exceptional-set result gives a set of eligible multipliers of full Hausdorff dimension. Such a set contains irrationals because the rationals are countable.

A particularly accessible later quantitative theorem was proved by **Yuval Peres and Wilhelm Schlag** in *Two Erdős problems on lacunary sequences: Chromatic number and Diophantine approximation* (2010). Their Theorem 1.1 states that when

$$
a_{k+1}/a_k\geq1+\varepsilon,
\qquad 0<\varepsilon<\frac14,
$$

there is a number $\theta\in(0,1)$ for which

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\inf_{k\geq1}\|\theta a_k\|
>C\,\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log\varepsilon|}}
$$

for some absolute constant $C>0$. The proof uses a form of the **Lovász local lemma**, a probabilistic method for showing that many small “bad events” can all be avoided at once. Section 2 establishes the needed variant of that lemma; Section 3 applies it to rotation orbits sampled along lacunary sequences.

This bound provides a **uniform forbidden arc**. If $\delta$ is any positive number smaller than the displayed infimum, then no point $\{\theta a_k\}$ lies within distance $\delta$ of zero modulo one. The orbit therefore cannot be dense.

One technical limit is worth stating clearly: **the literal statement of Peres–Schlag Theorem 1.1 produces a real multiplier, not explicitly an irrational one**. The irrational requirement in the historical problem follows from stronger established witness-set results and can also be addressed by an uncountability argument. One should not silently insert the word *irrational* into the statement of a theorem whose displayed conclusion does not include it.

Lacunarity also connects this Diophantine question to graph coloring. Make a graph on $\mathbb Z$ in which two integers are adjacent whenever their absolute difference belongs to the lacunary set. A multiplier with $\inf_k\|\theta a_k\|>\delta>0$ gives a proper coloring using at most roughly $\delta^{-1}$ colors: divide the unit circle into short arcs and color each integer by the arc containing its rotation position. Adjacent integers have rotation positions separated by more than $\delta$, so they cannot receive the same color. Peres and Schlag obtain a quantitative chromatic-number bound of order $\varepsilon^{-1}|\log\varepsilon|$. This illustrates how a problem about fractional parts yields a theorem about infinite graphs.
