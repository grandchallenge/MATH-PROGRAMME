## The question in plain language

Take a finite list of distinct integers bigger than $1$, written in increasing order,

$$
1<n_1<n_2<\cdots<n_k,
$$

and suppose their reciprocals add exactly to one:

$$
\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}=1.
$$

Erdős Problem #287 asks whether every such list has a consecutive gap of at least $3$; in symbols,

$$
\max_{1\leq i<k}(n_{i+1}-n_i)\geq 3.
$$

The words “every such list” are important. A gap is the difference between neighboring denominators. Since the denominators are strictly increasing, each gap is at least $1$. The conjecture says that one cannot represent $1$ by unit fractions while keeping every gap equal to either $1$ or $2$.

This is a finite, exact-arithmetic assertion. A counterexample would be one explicit tuple $(n_1,\ldots,n_k)$ whose reciprocal sum is exactly $1$ and whose gaps are all at most $2$. That is the sense in which the pinned catalogue labels the statement **falsifiable**. The label does not assert that the statement is false, undecidable, independent of a named theory, or already settled. The ambient objects here are ordinary positive integers and rational numbers; no stronger logical status is justified by the wording.

## A small example

The tuple $(2,3,6)$ gives the exact identity

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16=\frac36+\frac26+\frac16=1.
$$

Its consecutive gaps are

$$
3-2=1,\qquad 6-3=3,
$$

so its largest gap is exactly $3$. I independently checked this calculation with Python’s `fractions.Fraction` type: the computed sum was `1`, the gap tuple was `(1, 3)`, and the maximum was `3`. This check uses exact rational arithmetic rather than floating-point rounding.

The example explains the sharpness language attached to the problem. If the universal lower bound $3$ is true, it cannot be increased to $4$, because this representation already attains maximum gap $3$. But the example is not a proof of the universal claim: it exhibits one admissible representation, not all admissible representations. It also does not decide whether there is a different tuple whose gaps are all $1$ or $2$.

## What is known

The primary historical source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 33. There they define the family of reciprocal representations of $1$ by distinct denominators and ask whether the maximum neighboring gap must be at least $3$. The same page records $(2,3,6)$ as the sharpness example and notes that the weaker lower bound $2$ follows from the impossibility of a sum of reciprocals of consecutive integers equaling $1$.

That weaker fact is supported by the primary paper P. Erdős, “Egy Kürschák-féle elemi számelméleti tétel általánosítása” (1932), printed page 6. In the special case $d=1$, Erdős identifies the result with Kürschák’s theorem: a finite sum of reciprocals taken from one consecutive arithmetic progression cannot be an integer. A representation with every gap equal to $1$ would be exactly such a consecutive block, so the theorem implies only

$$
\max_{1\leq i<k}(n_{i+1}-n_i)\geq 2.
$$

It does not rule out a mixture of gaps $1$ and $2$, which is precisely the extra difficulty in Problem #287.

The maintained problem page also records a conditional route involving primes: if every sufficiently large interval $[N,2N]$ contains a prime $p$ for which $(p+1)/2$ is prime, then the gap of $3$ conclusion would hold apart from finitely many exceptions. This is a conditional implication, not an unconditional proof of #287. The primary 1980 source itself presents the gap of $3$ question as unresolved; the current source audit found no source-authoritative proof that removes that boundary.
