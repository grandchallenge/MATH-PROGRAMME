## The question in plain language

For each point in a set of $n$ planar points, stand at that point and count the **different** distances to the other $n-1$ points. Equal distances are counted only once. Suppose **no four of the points lie on one circle**. Must there be a point from which almost all the other points are at different distances?

Write

$$
r_X(p)=\bigl|\{\lVert p-q\rVert:q\in X,\ q\ne p\}\bigr|,
\qquad
f(n)=\min_{\substack{|X|=n\\\text{no four concyclic}}}
\max_{p\in X}r_X(p).
$$

[Erdős Problem #654](https://www.erdosproblems.com/654) asks for the size of $f(n)$ and, more specifically, whether $f(n)>(1-o(1))n$. A second, weaker proposed bound is $f(n)>(1/3+c)n$ for some fixed $c>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$.

**As of October 2026, these are not equally unresolved questions:** a published 2026 construction disproves the nearly $n$ assertion, while the maintained catalogue still regards the fixed improvement over one third as open. A related *general-position* question also requires that no three points be collinear. That additional restriction changes what constructions are permitted.

## A small example

Take four points on the coordinate axes:

$$
X=\{(0,3),(0,-3),(2,0),(-2,0)\}.
$$

From $(0,3)$ the three distances to the other vertices are

$$
6,\quad \sqrt{13},\quad \sqrt{13}.
$$

From $(2,0)$ they are $4,\sqrt{13},\sqrt{13}$. By symmetry the other two vertices behave the same way. Thus every vertex has exactly **two distinct distance values**, despite having three neighbours.

These four points are not on one circle. Indeed, a circle with equation

$$
x^2+y^2+Ax+By+C=0
$$

passing through both vertical-axis points would require $C=-9$, whereas passing through both horizontal-axis points would require $C=-4$. That is impossible.

Consequently $f(4)\leq 2$. This is a small model of the published construction. A finite example by itself says nothing decisive about the question for every sufficiently large $n$.

## What is known

**The elementary lower bound.** Fix any admissible $X$ and any point $p\in X$. Each positive-radius circle centred at $p$ contains at most three other members of $X$: if it contained four, those four would be concyclic. Since there are $n-1$ other points,

$$
r_X(p)\geq \left\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rceil.
$$

In particular, $f(n)\geq(n-1)/3$. This simple counting argument does not rule out configurations where many equal-distance triples occur at most centres. Showing that the geometry of the *different* centres prevents that near-saturation is the unresolved direction.

**The published upper-bound counterexample.** In Section 3.1 of [Feng and collaborators (2026), arXiv:2601.22401v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401v3), an Aletheia-assisted construction places points on the two coordinate axes. For an integer $m\geq10$, set $n=4m$ and $K=\{10,\ldots,m+9\}$. Use

$$
P=\{(0,\pm3^k):k\in K\},
\qquad
Q=\{(\pm2^j,0):j\in K\},
\qquad X=P\cup Q.
$$

There are exactly $2m$ points on each axis. Why can no four lie on one circle? A circle can meet each axis in at most two points; any four-point circle would therefore have two points $(0,y_1),(0,y_2)$ on the vertical axis and two $(x_1,0),(x_2,0)$ on the horizontal axis. Substitution into its equation gives

$$
y_1y_2=C=x_1x_2.
$$

Taking absolute values then yields

$$
3^{k_1+k_2}=2^{j_1+j_2},
$$

which is impossible for positive integer exponents. Thus the no-four-concyclic hypothesis really is satisfied. Notice that merely saying the points lie on two axes would *not* be enough; the prime-power choice prevents circles meeting each axis twice.

At a point $(0,\pm3^{k_0})\in P$, the other $2m-1$ points on the vertical axis contribute at most $2m-1$ different distances. The distances to $Q$ have the form

$$
\sqrt{3^{2k_0}+2^{2j}}\qquad(j\in K).
$$

The two signs of each horizontal-axis coordinate produce the same distance, so the $2m$ cross-axis points contribute exactly $m$ values. We only need an upper bound on the union, hence

$$
r_X(p)\leq(2m-1)+m=3m-1.
$$

The analogous estimate holds for a point on $Q$. It follows that

$$
f(4m)\leq 3m-1<\frac34(4m)=\frac{3n}{4}
\qquad(m\geq10).
$$

This infinite family disproves any lower bound $(1-o(1))n$, because such a bound would eventually exceed $3n/4$. The argument is explained here in full but is **a published result**, not a new GCL discovery.

**What it does not prove.** The inequality $f(4m)<3n/4$ is consistent with $f(n)>(1/3+c)n$ for sufficiently small positive $c$. Moreover, the construction has many collinear triples; it cannot decide the separate question that assumes no three collinear.

The [maintained source discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/654) distinguishes the versions and keeps the residual question labelled OPEN. It attributes the stronger proposed general-position hypothesis to earlier Erdős and Erdős–Pach sources, reported as [Er87b, p.168], [ErPa90, p.267], and [Er97e, p.530]. Those original page passages were **not independently inspected** for this draft.

## What remains

The immediate source-fidelity obligation is to inspect the original Erdős passages cited as [Er87b, p.168], [ErPa90, p.267] and [Er97e, p.530] and distinguish the exact hypotheses and claims. The publication is not an adequate substitute for checking what the earlier authors actually wrote.

The mathematical problem then is whether there is a fixed $c>0$ and $n_0$ so that every admissible set of $n\geq n_0$ points has some $p$ with at least $(1/3+c)n$ distinct distances, particularly under the stronger no-three-collinear condition. Such a proof must use compatibility of distances across different centres, not merely count three points per circle. Alternatively, a genuine counterexample must satisfy *every* stated geometric restriction for arbitrarily large $n$.

Before editorial admission, the issue-bound partial return must receive critical source review, the draft must pass the relevant math and MkDocs checks, the consolidated cohort must be protected-published and the public mathematics must be inspected in a browser. A later V2 exact-hash admission is separate.
