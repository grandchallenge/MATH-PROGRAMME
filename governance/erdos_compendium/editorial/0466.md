## The question in plain language

Many distances between two points in the plane are integers, or very close to integers. Erdős asked whether it is possible to fit *arbitrarily many points* inside an increasingly large circular region while making **every pairwise distance** stay a fixed positive amount away from **all integers**.

For a real number $u$, define its distance to the nearest integer as

$$
\|u\|=\min_{m\in\mathbb Z}|u-m|.
$$

For $X>0$ and $0<\delta<1/2$, let $N(X,\delta)$ be the greatest number of points that can be placed inside a disk of radius $X$ in the plane so that

$$
\bigl\|\,|P_i-P_j|\,\bigr\|\geq\delta
\qquad\text{for every }i<j.
$$

The inner vertical bars indicate ordinary Euclidean distance between the two points. The outer double bars mean distance to the nearest integer. For example, a pair distance of $3.5$ is excellent: it stays $0.5$ away from every integer. A distance of $4.02$ is forbidden when $\delta=0.1$, because it is only $0.02$ away from the integer $4$.

**Erdős Problem #466 asks whether there exists one fixed $\delta>0$ such that $N(X,\delta)$ tends to infinity as $X$ tends to infinity.**

One cannot change $\delta$ as the desired number of points grows. It must work for every sufficiently large disk. The answer is **yes**.

The word *circle* in the original wording refers to a circular region, so points are allowed anywhere **inside the disk**, not only on its circumference. The restriction matters: insisting on the circumference would define a different extremal problem.

## A small example

Place four points at the corners of a square:

$$
P_1=(-3/4,-3/4),\quad
P_2=(-3/4,3/4),\quad
P_3=(3/4,-3/4),\quad
P_4=(3/4,3/4).
$$

Each point lies at distance $3\sqrt2/4$ from the origin, which is less than $2$, so all four lie inside a disk of radius $2$.

The square has six unordered pairs of vertices. Four correspond to its edges, each of length $3/2$; the other two are diagonals of length $3\sqrt2/2$. Their nearest-integer distances are therefore

$$
\left\|\frac32\right\|=\frac12,
\qquad
\left\|\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\right\|
=\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}-2>\frac1{10}.
$$

The final inequality can be checked without trusting a decimal approximation: squaring shows

$$
\left(\frac{21}{10}\right)^2
=\frac{441}{100}
<\frac92
=\left(\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\right)^2,
$$

so $3\sqrt2/2>2.1$. Also $3\sqrt2/2<5/2$, so the nearest integer really is $2$.

Consequently

$$
N(2,1/10)\geq4.
$$

This is an exact and reproducible finite illustration, but a four-point configuration alone says nothing about arbitrarily large configurations.

## What is known

The original question appears in Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), on printed pages 92–93.

On printed page 93, they report that **Ronald Graham** settled the question by proving that, for sufficiently large $X$,

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
N(X,1/10)>\frac{\log X}{10}.}
$$

Here $\log$ is the natural logarithm. Since $\log X$ tends to infinity, the right side does also. The same constant $1/10$ works as $X$ increases, which answers the original question affirmatively.

They then report a **substantially stronger lower bound** obtained by **András Sárközy** in 1976: for every $\varepsilon>0$, provided $\delta>0$ is sufficiently small **depending on $\varepsilon$**, one has

$$
N(X,\delta)>X^{1/2-\varepsilon}
$$

for sufficiently large $X$. The important quantifier is the dependence $\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)$. It does not mean that a single fixed $\delta$ works uniformly for all $\varepsilon>0$.

The maintained Erdős Problems page also states a more explicit exponent of $1/2-\delta^{1/7}$. That exact expression was **not located on the inspected 1980 page**, which gives only the $\delta(\varepsilon)$ formulation. The precise parameter relation in Sárközy's original 1976 papers requires a separate primary-proof audit before being promoted to a source-locked quantitative claim.

A modern primary mathematical source, *Point sets avoiding near-integer distances* by **Ritesh Goenka and Kenneth Moore**, arXiv:2605.06621v1 (May 2026), independently corroborates the historical bounds in its introduction. Its equation (1.1) gives the same pairwise-distance definition, and its summary of the planar theory states

$$
N_2(X,\delta)=\Omega_\delta(X^{1/2-\varepsilon})
$$

for sufficiently small $\delta$ depending on $\varepsilon$, along with Konyagin's complementary upper bound

$$
N_2(X,\delta)=O_\delta(X^{1/2}).
$$

The notations $N$ and $N_2$ refer to the same planar problem. These upper and lower results should not be mixed: the upper bound belongs to the neighboring Erdős Problem #465; #466 asks for *unboundedness from below*.

### Why the qualitative conclusion is plausible

There is another conceptual explanation of why $N(X,\delta)$ can grow without bound, using the classical **Kronecker density theorem**. It does not reproduce the effective logarithmic or power-law bounds of Graham and Sárközy.

Fix any desired number $n$ of points. One can choose $n$ distinct points $Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ inside the unit disk so that their finitely many pairwise distances

$$
d_{ij}=|Q_i-Q_j|\qquad(i<j)
$$

are linearly independent over the rational numbers. Such configurations exist because each nontrivial rational linear relation between these distance functions cuts out a measure-zero set of configurations, and there are only countably many rational relations.

For completeness, the distance functions really do have no rational linear identity. If a relation were identically zero, bring any selected pair of points together while keeping all the other points apart. The selected term $|Q_i-Q_j|$ has a nondifferentiable cusp at the collision; every other distance term extends differentiably. Its coefficient must therefore vanish. The same reasoning applied to every pair forces all coefficients to vanish.

Kronecker's theorem now says that the continuous one-parameter flow

$$
t\longmapsto \bigl(td_{ij}\bmod1\bigr)_{i<j}
$$

is dense in the torus with one coordinate per pair. Therefore some $t>0$ makes **every** coordinate lie between $1/4$ and $3/4$. Scale all points by this same factor $t$:

$$
P_i=tQ_i.
$$

Every pairwise distance then satisfies

$$
\bigl\|\,|P_i-P_j|\,\bigr\|>1/4.
$$

All points lie in the disk of radius $t$, so $N(X,1/4)\geq n$ for all $X\geq t$. Because $n$ was arbitrary, this establishes the **qualitative** limit statement.

Unlike Graham's construction, the argument offers no useful explicit estimate for the radius required to realize $n$ points. It is a mathematical reconstruction using established genericity and Kronecker tools, **not a claimed new result or rederivation of the historical quantitative bounds**.

## GCL's current state

GCL's [authenticated source audit for #466](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1736#issuecomment-6166375563) checked the original 1980 printed pages and compared them against Goenka–Moore's 2026 primary preprint and the maintained problem index.

The audit preserved three material distinctions. First, the points lie within a disk rather than necessarily on a circle. Second, the *same* fixed $\delta$ must support arbitrarily many points. Third, the explicitly reported modern $\delta^{1/7}$ exponent has not yet been verified against the full 1976 primary source.

The four-vertex configuration and its exact irrational-diagonal inequality have been independently checked. The generic-Kronecker argument describes the already known **qualitative** conclusion; it was not compiled in Lean or independently certified as a new mathematical theorem.

The canonical registry marks #466 as PROVED. No formal statement for it is present in GCL's pinned Formal Conjectures provider snapshot, so a later external formalization, if one exists, cannot be assumed to be an admitted GCL certificate.

## What remains

The principal mathematical question posed by Erdős is **settled**, not open. Remaining work is editorial, historical, quantitative and formal.

The first source task is to inspect the complete original Sárközy papers *On distances near integers. I, II* (1976), especially the theorem controlling the dependence of $\delta$ on the exponent. This will determine whether the modern expression $1/2-\delta^{1/7}$ is an exact historical theorem or a later reformulation.

The second source task is to locate and review Graham's original logarithmic construction. It should identify any geometric normalization and all hypotheses instead of relying solely on Erdős and Graham's 1980 summary.

A bounded formal task is to represent configurations in a closed Euclidean disk, prove that all pairwise distances meet the near-integer exclusion condition, and formalize the monotonicity of $N(X,\delta)$. The exact square configuration above is a suitable finite regression fixture. A separate and more difficult formal project could implement the generic-distance independence lemma and Kronecker's simultaneous approximation, but such verification must not be confused with a new solution.
