## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 57 concerns the distribution of odd cycle lengths in a graph whose chromatic number is infinite. If $A_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)$ denotes the set of distinct odd integers occurring as cycle lengths, the question is whether

$$
\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)}\frac1a
$$

must diverge.

This is stronger than merely asking for infinitely many odd cycle lengths. A very sparse infinite set can have a convergent reciprocal sum, so the problem asks for quantitative richness in the cycle spectrum.

The problem does not count cycles with multiplicity. For example, if a graph contains many triangles, the length $3$ contributes only once.

## A small example

Erdős and Hajnal’s 1966 Theorem 7.7 says, in modern notation, that if a finite-chromatic graph has no odd cycle of length $2i+1$ for any $i\ge j$, then its chromatic number is at most $2j$. The bound is sharp: $K_{2j}$ has chromatic number $2j$ and contains no odd cycle longer than $2j-1$.

This verifies the intended quantifier pattern and shows why “infinitely many odd lengths” is substantially weaker than reciprocal-sum divergence.

A second elementary check uses complete graphs. Since $K_n$ contains a cycle on every selected set of at least three vertices, its odd cycle lengths are precisely the odd integers from $3$ through $n$. Hence

$$
\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{odd}}(K_n)}\frac1a
=
\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\frac1{2j+1}
=
\frac12\log n+O(1).
$$

Thus the coefficient $1/2$ in the Liu–Montgomery lower bound is asymptotically natural.

## What is known

The exact reciprocal-sum formulation is visible in Erdős’s 1981 paper *Problems and Results in Graph Theory*. In its miscellaneous problems section, Erdős writes that Hajnal and he conjectured divergence of the reciprocal sum over the odd circuit lengths of an infinite-chromatic graph. He immediately adds the stronger speculation that these lengths might have positive upper density. [Erdős 1981, p. 339](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1981-20.pdf)

The frequently attached 1966 citation is historically relevant but not textually identical. Erdős and Hajnal’s 1966 paper proves that infinite chromatic number forces infinitely many odd cycle lengths and asks whether all sufficiently large odd lengths must occur. That is a predecessor problem, not the registered reciprocal-sum statement. [Erdős–Hajnal 1966, §7](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1966-07.pdf)

This distinction matters for provenance auditing: the mathematical status is settled, but the catalogue’s short citation should not be treated as proof that the exact displayed statement first appeared in 1966.

Liu and Montgomery prove a considerably stronger finite statement. For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large chromatic numbers $k$, a graph of chromatic number $k$ contains every odd cycle length in an interval

$$
[\ell,\ell k^{1-\varepsilon}]
$$

for some $\ell$ depending on the graph.

The reciprocal sum over odd integers in such an interval is approximately

$$
\frac12\log\left(\frac{\ell k^{1-\varepsilon}}{\ell}\right)
=
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\log k.
$$

Letting $\varepsilon$ tend to zero gives their quantitative conclusion

$$
\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)}\frac1a
\ge
\left(\frac12-o_k(1)\right)\log k.
$$

The theorem is stated and proved in [Liu–Montgomery](https://rhmontgomery.warwick.ac.uk/papers/22oddcycleproblem.pdf), and the published version is [JAMS 36 (2023), DOI 10.1090/jams/1018](https://doi.org/10.1090/jams/1018).

The original problem allows $G$ itself to be infinite. The relevant compactness principle is the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem: if an infinite graph has no finite subgraph of chromatic number exceeding $k$, then the whole graph is $k$‑colorable.

Therefore, if $\chi(G)$ is infinite, then for every finite $k$ there is a finite subgraph $H\subseteq G$ with chromatic number at least $k$. Applying the Liu–Montgomery estimate to $H$ gives

$$
\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{odd}}(G)}\frac1a
\ge
\sum_{a\in A_{\mathrm{odd}}(H)}\frac1a
\ge
\left(\frac12-o_k(1)\right)\log k.
$$

Since the right-hand side is unbounded as $k\to\infty$, the sum for $G$ diverges.

This also explains why the finite theorem is the correct interface for the infinite statement: no separate infinite-graph construction is needed once compactness is available.
