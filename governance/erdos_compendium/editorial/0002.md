## The question in plain language

Imagine choosing a finite collection of repeating schedules that between them cover **every integer**, even though no two schedules repeat at the same interval. Each schedule is a residue class, such as all integers leaving remainder 1 when divided by 4. The repeat interval is called its *modulus*.

Erdős asked whether it is possible to cover every integer using only very large, pairwise distinct moduli. Could the smallest modulus exceed a million—or any proposed bound?

## A small example

One covering system uses the residue classes

\[
0\pmod 2,\quad 0\pmod 3,\quad 1\pmod 4,\quad
5\pmod 6,\quad 7\pmod{12}.
\]

Every integer falls into at least one class, as checking the 12 residue possibilities confirms. The moduli are distinct, but the smallest is 2. Erdős wanted to know whether **all** small moduli could be avoided.

## What is known

**The original question was answered no.** Bob Hough proved in 2015 that any such covering system must have some modulus at most \(10^{16}\). Work by Balister, Bollobás, Morris, Sahasrabudhe, and Tiba subsequently brought the published universal upper bound to 616,000.

The largest known example of the *smallest* modulus is 42 (Owens). This leaves an enormous gap between construction and impossibility bounds. A negative answer to Erdős's original unlimited-growth question does not determine the optimal ceiling.

## What remains

The original conjecture is settled. Improving the interval between 42 and 616,000, understanding why large congruence schedules inevitably leave uncovered numbers, and formalizing the strongest proofs are legitimate open directions.
