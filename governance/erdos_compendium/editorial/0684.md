## The question in plain language

For a binomial coefficient $\binom nk$, separate its prime factors into those at most $k$ and those larger than $k$. Multiply the **complete prime powers** in the first group to obtain $u(n,k)$. How far must the index $k$ grow before this small-prime part becomes bigger than $n^2$? [Erdős #684](https://www.erdosproblems.com/684) defines $f(n)$ as the least such $k$ and asks for its growth. This is an asymptotic number-theory question; small $n$ need not have any qualifying $k$.

## A small example

Take $n=10,k=7$. Since $\binom{10}{7}=120=2^3\cdot3\cdot5$, every prime in the coefficient is at most $7$, so $u(10,7)=120>10^2=100$. Checking $k<7$ shows $f(10)=7$. But at $n=20$, exact evaluation for every $0\leq k\leq20$ gives $\max_k u(20,k)=240<20^2=400$. There is **no** value of $f(20)$ under the literal finite $n$ definition. This is not a counterexample to theorems that assert a bound only for sufficiently large $n$; it is a necessary convention for an honest explanation.

## What is known

For each prime $p$, the exponent with which it divides the binomial coefficient is exactly $v_p\!\left(\binom nk\right)=\sum_{j\geq1}(\lfloor n/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor(n-k)/p^j\rfloor)$ (Legendre's formula). Thus $u(n,k)=\prod_{p\leq k}p^{v_p(\binom nk)}$, with **all powers** included. The maintained catalogue cites Mahler's classical result implying $f(n)\to\infty$, though its proof is ineffective. A major later improvement appears in [Alexeev, Putterman, Sawhney, Sellke and Valiant, *Short proofs in combinatorics and number theory*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29961), Section 2, Theorem 2.1. The authors establish for sufficiently large $n$ a **polylogarithmic upper bound**, reported by the maintained catalogue as $f(n)\ll(\log n)^2$, and an infinite sequence on which $f(n)\geq(1/2-o(1))\log n$. The paper explicitly credits its proofs to an internal OpenAI model. Here the mathematical source remains the named author paper, not this editorial draft or any claim of GCL originality. The gap between logarithmic and squared-logarithmic growth remains of interest.
