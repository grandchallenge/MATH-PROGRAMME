## The question in plain language

Start with an increasing sequence of integers

$$
a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots
$$

whose relative gaps shrink: $a_{i+1}/a_i\to1$. A distinct subset sum means adding each selected term at most once, with only finitely many terms selected. Erdős asked whether the following implication holds: if every infinite arithmetic progression contains infinitely many such subset sums, must every sufficiently large positive integer be a subset sum?

“Sufficiently large” means that all but finitely many positive integers are represented. The quantifiers matter. The hypothesis is not merely that each progression contains one represented number, and the conclusion is not merely that there are infinitely many represented numbers. In the printed 1961 source, Problem 17 on p. 230 continues onto p. 231. Its displayed condition is $a_{i+1}/a_i\to1$; the scan does not separately state positivity, whereas the maintained page and the formal declaration use a positive sequence. That normalization is harmless for the positive counterexample below, but it should not be silently attributed to the original wording.

The answer is no. J. W. S. Cassels constructed a positive increasing sequence satisfying the progression hypothesis and the shrinking-gap condition while making its distinct subset sums arbitrarily sparse in the precise parameterized sense stated below. The maintained page summarizes this as “density 0”; the theorem’s printed quantifiers are the safer claim to use here. Thus this is a classically disproved problem, not an open conjecture.

## A small example

Here is a reproducible finite sanity check for the kind of subset-sum calculation involved. It is an irrational-rotation toy slice, not Cassels’s infinite construction and not a proof of any asymptotic assertion.

```python
from math import sqrt

alpha = (sqrt(5) - 1) / 2
epsilon = .5
C = [d for d in range(1, 20001)
     if abs(d * alpha - round(d * alpha)) < d ** (-(1 + epsilon) / 2)]

reachable = {0}
for d in C[:18]:
    reachable |= {x + d for x in tuple(reachable)}

print(len(C), C[:18], len(reachable), min(reachable), max(reachable))
print(all(n in reachable for n in range(201)))
```

The independently rerun output is `87 [1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 16, 21, 26, 29, 34, 42, 47, 55, 68, 76, 89, 110] 646 0 645` followed by `True`. So these 18 distinct summands represent every integer from $0$ through $200$ in this finite computation. The result demonstrates how a reachable-set update works; it does not show that the displayed toy sequence meets every arithmetic progression infinitely often, has the required ratio limit, or has density-zero subset sums. Those are infinite claims supplied only by a mathematical source.

A simpler finite principle is also useful for orientation: if the available summands are $1,2,\ldots,m$, their distinct subset sums include every integer from $0$ through $m(m+1)/2$ by induction. Even that exact finite coverage says nothing by itself about a prescribed infinite sequence or about eventual coverage.

## What is known

The decisive result is Cassels, “On the representation of integers as the sums of distinct summands taken from a fixed set,” *Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum* (Szeged) 21 (1960), 111–124. Theorem II is on p. 112; the construction is carried out in the proof on pp. 122–124. For each chosen $\varepsilon>0$, Cassels constructs positive integers $c_1<c_2<\cdots$ with

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{n+1}-c_n}{c_n^{1/2+\varepsilon}}=0,
$$

with infinitely many elements of the set $C=\{c_1,c_2,\ldots\}$ in every arithmetic progression, and with $S(n)<\varepsilon n$ for every $n$, where $S(n)$ counts integers at most $n$ expressible as sums of distinct elements of $C$. The first property implies $c_{n+1}/c_n\to1$ (choose any $\varepsilon<1/2$), and every element of $C$ is itself a one-term distinct sum. Hence the progression hypothesis holds for the subset sums. Taking, for example, $\varepsilon<1/2$, the estimate $S(n)<\varepsilon n$ is incompatible with cofinite representation, which would force $S(n)/n\to1$.

This is a borrowed theorem, not a GCL reconstruction of Cassels’s proof. Erdős’s “Some Unsolved Problems,” *Magyar Tud. Akad. Mat. Kutató Int. Közl.* 6 (1961), 221–254, records Problem 17 on pp. 230–231 and explicitly says there that Cassels disproved it. The theorem statement above is deliberately not used to infer that one fixed $C$ has density zero: $C$ depends on the chosen $\varepsilon$. It is already enough to refute cofinite representation by fixing one $\varepsilon<1$. The 1999 booklet *Some of Paul’s Favorite Problems*, item 1.24, revisits a weaker-looking condition requiring at least one represented integer in each residue class or progression. That later formulation should not be called logically identical to Erdős’s “infinitely many” hypothesis without a separate normalization argument; Cassels’s stronger counterexample nevertheless defeats both.

## What remains

There is no remaining classical problem to solve: Cassels’s published counterexample settles the original implication. Remaining work is evidentiary and semantic. A careful formal audit should compare every hypothesis of Cassels Theorem II with the Lean witness, including positivity, strict monotonicity, the arithmetic-progression domain, the ratio-limit expression, and the cofinite conclusion. It should also replay the external Lean source in its declared toolchain and record the exact result. Until that occurs, “formalized” and “formal proof” remain upstream reports, not GCL certification.

The source wording deserves preservation rather than repair by paraphrase. Erdős’s scan says “integers” and does not visibly add $a_1\ge1$; the current maintained page says “infinite arithmetic progression,” gives the $1\le a_1$ normalization, and summarizes Cassels’s gap as $a_{i+1}-a_i<a_i^{1/2+o(1)}$. The page was last edited 23 January 2026. The weaker 1999 formulation and any claim that it is equivalent require their own precise domain and quantifier proof.
