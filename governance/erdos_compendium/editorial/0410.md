## The question in plain language

Let $\sigma(n)$ be the sum of the positive divisors of $n$. Start with an integer $n$ and apply this operation repeatedly: $\sigma_1(n)=\sigma(n)$ and $\sigma_{k+1}(n)=\sigma(\sigma_k(n))$. Erdős Problem 410 asks whether every starting value $n\ge 2$ produces an orbit whose typical multiplicative growth per step becomes arbitrarily large:

$$
\text{for every }n\ge 2,\qquad \lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma_k(n)^{1/k}=\infty.
$$

The quantifier matters. This is not a claim about most $n$, about a subsequence of $k$, or merely about the sequence $\sigma_k(n)$ being unbounded. It asks for one asymptotic statement for each fixed starting integer. The root with exponent $k$ measures the average multiplicative scale of the first $k$ iterates; saying that it tends to infinity is much stronger than seeing occasional large values.

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/410) gives this formulation and tags it as number theory and iterated functions. The original issue for this audit is [MATH-PROGRAMME #1680](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1680).

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check for $n=2$. Direct divisor enumeration gives the following orbit prefix:

$$
2\to3\to4\to7\to8\to15\to24\to60\to168\to480\to1512.
$$

For example, the divisors of $480$ are $1,2,3,4,5,6,8,10,12,15,16,20,24,30,32,40,48,60,80,96,120,160,240,480$, whose sum is exactly $1512$. The preceding checks similarly give $\sigma(168)=480$ and $\sigma(60)=168$; enumerating all divisors verifies every displayed arrow. This is a reproducible exact-arithmetic calculation, not a floating-point estimate.

It shows that the orbit can grow substantially over a short window, and it supplies useful test data for implementations. It does not establish the limit. A finite prefix cannot decide an asymptotic statement, and even rapid early growth does not rule out later cycles, slowdowns, or other behavior. In particular, the example is not a proof for $n=2$, still less for all $n$.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-and-semantics review of the primary page-169 formulation followed by a bounded orbit investigation. First, compare its quantifiers, definition of the iterate index, and meaning of the real root with exponent $k$ with the live and Lean statements. Then choose a finite bound $N$ and depth $K$, publish an exact-arithmetic certificate for all $2\le n\le N$ and $0\le k\le K$, and separately test whether the verified values support a rigorously stated lemma such as positivity or a monotonicity property on that finite domain.

Acceptance should require the source quotation or scan location, a deterministic certificate, and an independently reproducible checker. Falsification is equally concrete: a source mismatch, a divisor-sum error, a counterexample to the proposed finite lemma, or an orbit entering a verified cycle stops that package. The stopping rule is reached at the chosen $N,K$, at the first falsifying witness, or when the primary text needed to normalize the target cannot be obtained. None of these finite outcomes settles the asymptotic conjecture.
