## The question in plain language

Fix a real number $c>0$ and a positive integer $n$. Look inside the interval of integers from $1$ through $\lfloor cn\rfloor$. We want the largest possible set $A$ with the property that no subset of $A$ has sum exactly $n$. In other words, even after choosing or discarding each member of $A$, the target $n$ must remain unreachable. Writing

$$
F_c(n)=\max\{|A|:A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\lfloor cn\rfloor\},\ n\text{ is not a subset sum of }A\},
$$

the problem asks how large $F_c(n)$ can be, and whether its value fluctuates in an irregular way as $n$ changes. The question is extremal: it asks for the maximum size, not merely for one construction.

The historical wording is slightly different. Erdős and Graham ask how many integers strictly less than $n/k$ can be chosen, with $k$ fixed and $n$ large, so that $n$ is not a subset sum. The live formulation replaces $1/k$ by an arbitrary fixed $c>0$ and uses the closed interval through $\lfloor cn\rfloor$. These are closely related normalizations, but strict versus non-strict endpoint conventions can differ for individual $n$, so they should not be silently identified.

## A small example

Take $c=3/4$ and $n=4$. Then $\lfloor cn\rfloor=3$, so the universe is $\{1,2,3\}$. The set $\{1,2\}$ is valid: its subset sums are $0,1,2,3$, and none is $4$. Thus $F_{3/4}(4)\geq2$.

There is only one candidate of size three, namely $\{1,2,3\}$. It is invalid because the subset $\{1,3\}$ sums to $4$. Hence $F_{3/4}(4)=2$. An exhaustive exact check gives the valid candidates

~~~text
(), (1), (2), (1, 2), (3), (2, 3)
~~~

and maximum cardinality 2. One reproducible verifier is:

~~~python
from itertools import combinations
U, n = (1, 2, 3), 4
def subset_sums(A):
    s = {0}
    for a in A:
        s |= {x + a for x in s}
    return s
good = [A for r in range(4) for A in combinations(U, r)
        if n not in subset_sums(A)]
print(good, max(map(len, good)))
~~~

This is an exact finite calculation over integers, not a heuristic and not a proof of the asymptotic problem. It illustrates the key semantic point: checking only the sum of the whole candidate set would incorrectly accept $\{1,2,3\}$, while the problem checks every subset.

## What is known

The primary source inspected here is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 59. It records the question; the page does not supply a solution or an asymptotic formula for this extremal function. The maintained Erdős Problems page labels the problem open and says that it cannot be resolved by a finite computation.

The live discussion contains useful but unverified exploration. One comment gives elementary constructions from multiples of the smallest prime not dividing $n$ and from intervals whose repeated use would overshoot $n$. Other comments propose residue-class constructions for particular divisibility patterns, including $c=3/4$. These are reports by contributors, not checked theorems in the source packet.

There is also a proof claim posted in July 2026. Its summary asserts, among other things, an exact formula for $c\geq1$ and non-convergence of $F_c(n)/n$ for $0<c<1$. A later comment says that the posted argument addresses only the irregular-dependence part and that a more general theorem may still be missing. A second contributor reports a note with subsequential limits. No primary paper or independently checked proof for those claims was available in this inquiry, so they remain unverified external reports rather than established coverage.

## What remains

A bounded successor task should audit the live numerical report for $c=3/4$ at $n=100,101,102,103,104$, where a discussion comment reports values 34,37,32,38,35. The input is exactly those five integers and the closed universe $\{1,\ldots,\lfloor3n/4\rfloor\}$. The target is one exact value $F_{3/4}(n)$ for each input, together with a maximizing set.

Use integer dynamic programming to enumerate every subset of the universe whose sum is $n$, then solve the resulting finite hitting-set formulation: for every forbidden subset $B$, the retained indicators must satisfy $\sum_{x\in B}x_A\leq |B|-1$. Acceptance requires an independently readable certificate consisting of the proposed set, a complete forbidden-subset constraint digest, and a checked upper-bound certificate ruling out a larger set. Falsification is the first valid larger set, invalid proposed set, missing constraint, or mismatch with a reported value. Stop after all five certificates pass or at the first discrepancy; do not infer an asymptotic theorem from the finite table.
