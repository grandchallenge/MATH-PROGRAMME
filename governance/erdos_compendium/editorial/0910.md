## The question in plain language

Connectedness says that a topological space cannot be split into two nonempty, mutually separated pieces. Erdős asked two questions about connected subsets of Euclidean space. First, must every nondegenerate connected set contain another connected subset that is neither a single point nor **homeomorphic** to the original set? Second, in dimensions at least two, must every connected set contain **more than continuum-many** distinct connected subsets?

These are the two parts of [Erdős Problem #910](https://www.erdosproblems.com/910). The maintained catalogue marks both **disproved**, crediting Mary Ellen Rudin's 1958 construction. Critically, the construction assumes the **continuum hypothesis** (CH). Thus the source establishes counterexamples in ZFC together with CH; it does not, by itself, settle the existence of such counterexamples in ZFC alone.

The two questions are distinct. A connected subset may be proper but homeomorphic to the whole, and a large total number of subsets need not imply a comparably large number of **connected** subsets.

## A small example

The interval $[0,1]$ is connected and contains proper nondegenerate intervals such as $[0,1/2]$ that are **homeomorphic** to it. It also contains connected subsets of a different homeomorphism type, such as $(0,1)$, and so it does not refute Erdős's first assertion.

On the other hand, an ordinary planar disk has very many connected subsets. Simple polygons, arcs and disks provide easy illustrations of variety, but finite examples cannot establish or refute the universal claim about **all** connected Euclidean subspaces. Rudin constructed an extraordinary planar connected set instead.

The precise published condition is unusually restrictive. Under CH there exists a nondegenerate connected set $M$ in the plane such that, whenever $N\subseteq M$ is itself nondegenerate and connected,

$$
M\setminus N\text{ is at most countable}.
$$

Thus every nontrivial connected subset occupies **all but countably many** points of $M$. There is no claim that *every* co-countable subset is connected or that *all* of these subsets are homeomorphic; those implications do not follow from the displayed condition alone.

## What is known

Mary Ellen Rudin, [*A connected subset of the plane*](https://doi.org/10.4064/fm-46-1-15-24), *Fundamenta Mathematicae* **46** (1958), pp.15–24, states the co-countable-complement theorem explicitly in the opening abstract and attributes the underlying conjecture to Erdős's 1944 remarks. The [journal issue index](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/fundamenta-mathematicae/all/46/1) independently establishes the volume, page range and DOI. An [author bibliography with the complete abstract](https://celebratio.org/Rudin_Mary/article/236/) records that Rudin starts from an **indecomposable planar continuum** and selects a subset meeting each composant in at most one point, using transfinite induction.

The hypothesis CH asserts

$$
2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1.
$$

It is used in the transfinite construction. Rudin's theorem is therefore a **conditional negative answer**. The maintained [#910 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/910) describes Rudin's paper as disproving both of Erdős's formulations, conditional on CH. The original article's abstract directly records its co-countability conclusion; independently deriving the first question's full **homeomorphism** claim from the internal construction requires further scrutiny of the complete 1958 proof.

The elementary cardinal bound for the second question is transparent. Every nondegenerate connected $N$ equals $M\setminus D$ for some **countable** subset $D$ of $M$. A planar set has at most continuum-many points, so the number of its countable subsets is at most

$$
(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}.
$$

There are also at most continuum-many singleton subsets. Consequently Rudin's $M$ has **at most continuum-many** connected subsets altogether. This implication uses ordinary cardinal arithmetic, with no additional application of CH **after** Rudin's $M$ is supplied.

The 1944 Erdős paper, [*Some remarks on connected sets*](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1944-06.pdf), explicitly asks about proper connected subsets not homeomorphic to the original. Rudin cites this historical source. The 1958 construction does not supply an unconditional ZFC-independent counterexample from these references alone.

## GCL's current state

There is a complete elementary **co-countable-family counting lemma**: for a set $M$ of cardinal at most continuum, any family of subsets each obtained by deleting a countable subset from $M$ has size at most continuum. The map taking a member $N$ to $M\setminus N$ is injective; each complement admits a finite or countably infinite enumeration, giving an injection into a family bounded by cardinal exponentiation. The identity $\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$ establishes the result.

The exact verifier governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_910_911.py checks the finite analogue for ground sets of sizes two through twelve. For all complement sizes at most three, the number of subsets obtained by removing exactly $d$ elements is the binomial coefficient $\binom nd$. This is a count-check regression, not a replacement for infinite-cardinal arithmetic, nor for Rudin's transfinite connected-set construction.

The explicit distinction between the **counting deduction** and the **topological existence theorem** is essential. A finite combinatorial test cannot certify connectedness of a noncompact planar set selected by transfinite induction.

## What remains

A bounded proof obligation is the cardinal family lemma, formally distinguishing the continuum-sized ambient plane from the countable complements and distinguishing **all connected subsets** from all subsets. Acceptance requires a complete paper proof or pinned no-placeholder Lean with literal axioms and exact finite-binomial regression. A purported injection that ignores finite complements, or a cardinal calculation suggesting continuum to the countable power exceeds continuum, falsifies this stage.

The deep source obligation is to reread Rudin's full 1958 pp.15–24 construction, including the indecomposable continuum, composants, CH enumeration and the final connectivity proof. In particular verify exactly how the first **homeomorphism-type** question is refuted, rather than infer it merely from the co-countability statement. Record which consequences genuinely require CH and whether any later ZFC result changes the status. Until then the statement is **conditionally disproved by the published source**, not independently reconstructed by GCL.
