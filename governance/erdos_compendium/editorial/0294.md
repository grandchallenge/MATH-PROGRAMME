## The question in plain language

An Egyptian-fraction representation of $1$ is a finite sum of reciprocals of distinct positive integers. For a positive integer $N$, consider representations whose denominators begin with a specified integer $t$ and never exceed $N$:

$$
1=\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k},
\qquad t=n_1<n_2<\cdots<n_k\leq N.
$$

Problem #294 asks about the first value of $t$ for which this is impossible. More precisely, $t(N)$ is the least positive integer $t$ for which no such finite sequence exists. The quantifiers are worth spelling out: $N$ is fixed first; $t$ is then the least positive integer with the failure property; and the representation, when it exists, may have any finite length $k\geq1$. The condition $t=n_1$ says that $t$ is the smallest denominator, not merely one denominator somewhere in the sum.

The answer is not expected to be a simple exact formula. The published result gives the scale of this first obstruction, up to powers of iterated logarithms:

$$
\frac{N}{(\log N)(\log\log N)^3(\log\log\log N)^{O(1)}}\ll t(N)\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.
$$

Here $f(N)\ll g(N)$ means that $f(N)$ is at most a constant multiple of $g(N)$ for all sufficiently large $N$. Thus the statement locates $t(N)$ near $N/\log N$, while leaving a polylogarithmic gap in the lower bound. It does not assert that every finite $N$ has a displayed asymptotic value.

## A small example

Take $N=6$. The denominator set is small enough that an exact enumeration can be done by considering every subset of $\{t,t+1,\ldots,6\}$ containing $t$ and summing with rational arithmetic. An independent Python replay using `fractions.Fraction` and `itertools.combinations` returned

$$
\{1\}\quad\text{and}\quad \{2,3,6\}
$$

as the only representations of $1$ with first denominator $1$ or $2$, respectively. Indeed,

$$
1=\frac11,
\qquad
1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac16.
$$

There is no representation beginning with $3$. After choosing $1/3$, the required residual is $2/3$, while even using every available larger denominator gives only

$$
\frac14+\frac15+\frac16=\frac{15+12+10}{60}=\frac{37}{60}<\frac23.
$$

Any proper subcollection is smaller. For $t\geq4$, even the sum of all available reciprocals is at most $1/4+1/5+1/6=37/60<1$. Therefore the exact finite answer is $t(6)=3$. This calculation checks the definition and its quantifiers; it is not evidence by itself for the asymptotic theorem.

## What is known

The historical source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980). On printed page 35 of the cited scan, the quantity called $k_1(n)$ is the least integer that does not occur as the first denominator $x_1$ in a representation with $x_1<\cdots<x_r\leq n$. That is the same first-denominator quantity as $t(N)$ after renaming $n$ as $N$. The same page discusses nearby quantities concerning whether an integer occurs in any denominator position; those must not be conflated with #294.

The primary modern source is Yang P. Liu and Mehtaab Sawhney, “On further questions regarding unit fractions.” Their Theorem 1.6 (arXiv version 1, printed p. 3) states exactly the definition above and proves the two-sided bound. Lemma 4.1 (arXiv version 1, p. 13) supplies the smooth-denominator construction used in the lower-bound argument, and the proof of Theorem 1.6 continues on pp. 13–14. In outline, the upper bound chooses a prime $t$ just above a constant multiple of $N/\log N$ and uses a congruence obstruction; the lower bound repeatedly builds controlled reciprocal sums from smooth integers. The paper is published in *International Mathematics Research Notices* (2026), article rnaf382, DOI [10.1093/imrn/rnaf382](https://doi.org/10.1093/imrn/rnaf382). These are claims and proofs in the cited primary paper, not a new proof by this manuscript.
