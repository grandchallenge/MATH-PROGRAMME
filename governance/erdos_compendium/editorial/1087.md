## The question in plain language

Among four distinct planar points there are six pairwise distances. A four-point set is called *degenerate* in [Erdős Problem #1087](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1087) when two of those pair distances coincide.

How many such quadruples can appear in a configuration of $n$ points? The problem asks for the maximum number $f(n)$ and, in particular, whether

$$
f(n)\le n^{3+o(1)}.
$$

This is **not** ordinary collinearity degeneracy. Two equal-length pairs might share an endpoint or be disjoint. Whether the 1971 source intends both cases is a material definition that still needs primary-source verification.

## A small example

For the four corners of a unit square, the six squared pair distances, including multiplicity, are

$$
1,1,1,1,2,2.
$$

The only four-element subset is degenerate. Because a four-point configuration contains at most one quadruple, $f(4)=1$.

Equal lengths may also appear in a collinear quadruple: positions $0,1,3,6$ on a line determine two length-three segments, $[0,3]$ and $[3,6]$, sharing one endpoint.

In contrast, the four points $(0,0),(2,0),(0,3),(4,5)$ determine six *different* squared distances. Thus repeated distances are not automatic.

## What is known

Erdős and Purdy (1971) obtained the historical bounds reported by the catalogue:

$$
n^3\log n\ \ll\ f(n)\ \ll\ n^{7/2}.
$$

The open question is whether the exponent on the upper bound can be reduced essentially to three. A small example cannot distinguish these asymptotic possibilities.
