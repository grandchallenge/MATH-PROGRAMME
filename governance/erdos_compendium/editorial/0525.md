**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #525 is **PROVED**. It asks whether almost all degree-$n$ polynomials with coefficients $\pm1$ become **smaller than one somewhere on the complex unit circle**, and more generally how small their minimum modulus typically is. Kashin answered Littlewood's stronger question in 1987, Konyagin improved the scale in 1994, and a peer-reviewed 2021 paper by Nicholas Cook and Hoi Nguyen established an **exponential limiting distribution** at scale $n^{-1/2}$.

## The question in plain language

Choose signs $\varepsilon_0,\dots,\varepsilon_n\in\{-1,1\}$ and form

$$
P_n(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon_kz^k.
$$

Define the **minimum modulus on the complex unit circle**

$$
m_n=\min_{|z|=1}|P_n(z)|.
$$

Erdős asked whether, among the $2^{n+1}$ possible sign choices, the proportion satisfying $m_n<1$ tends to one. In the original wording, all but $o(2^n)$ degree-$n$ polynomials should satisfy the inequality. Since $2^{n+1}=2\cdot2^n$, the difference in how the count is indexed does not affect the limit proportion.

Equivalently, when the coefficients are sampled independently as fair signs,

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(m_n<1)\longrightarrow1.}
$$

This is a claim about **high probability at each large degree**. It is not automatically the stronger assertion that, for one nested infinite sequence of random coefficients, the event $m_n<1$ occurs for **all sufficiently large $n$ almost surely**.

The question concerns the **minimum** over the complex unit circle. It is different from both the **maximum** over the real interval in Erdős #524 and the **maximum** over the complex unit circle in #523.

## A small example

For $n=2$, compare two sign polynomials,

$$
P(z)=1+z+z^2,\qquad Q(z)=1+z-z^2.
$$

The first vanishes at primitive cube roots of unity. Consequently, its unit-circle minimum modulus is **exactly zero**. Yet $|P(1)|=3$ and $|P(-1)|=1$. Looking only at the endpoints would miss the zero entirely.

The second polynomial has roots $(1\pm\sqrt5)/2$, whose magnitudes are not one. Since it is continuous and nonzero on the compact unit circle, its minimum modulus is **strictly positive**. Thus a small finite-degree experiment already distinguishes an exact zero from a small but positive minimum.

For exact calculations, set $z=e^{it}$ and define integer **autocorrelations**

$$
C_j=\sum_{k=0}^{n-j}\varepsilon_k\varepsilon_{k+j},
\qquad 1\le j\le n.
$$

Multiplication by the complex conjugate gives

$$
\boxed{
|P_n(e^{it})|^2=n+1+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_j\cos(jt).
}
$$

This identity turns a complex-modulus problem into a real trigonometric polynomial whose coefficients are **exact integers**.

It can be made purely algebraic. Put $u=2\cos t\in[-2,2]$ and define polynomials

$$
W_0(u)=2,\quad W_1(u)=u,\quad
W_{j+1}(u)=uW_j(u)-W_{j-1}(u).
$$

Then $W_j(2\cos t)=2\cos(jt)$, so

$$
\boxed{
m_n^2=\min_{-2\le u\le2}
\left(n+1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_jW_j(u)\right).
}
$$

The right-hand side is the minimum of an **integer polynomial on a rational closed interval**. At low degrees its minimum can be certified by exact algebraic root isolation of its derivative and checking endpoints. This gives a reproducible finite interface, not a proof of any large-$n$ probability claim.

## What is known

### From Erdős and Littlewood to Kashin and Konyagin

The original problem appears in Erdős, *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.253, equation (V.3.6)](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=33). The original scan was directly read. Erdős asks whether all but $o(2^n)$ sign polynomials have unit-circle minimum below one, and also asks for a more precise estimate.

J. E. Littlewood (1966) posed the stronger question whether the minimum modulus is $o(1)$ **in the high-probability sense**. B. S. Kashin (1987) proved this assertion. S. V. Konyagin (1994) improved the scale: for every fixed $\delta>0$,

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(m_n\ge n^{-1/2+\delta})\longrightarrow0.}
$$

The assertion is **convergence in probability**, not a deterministic bound for every sign vector. S. V. Konyagin and W. Schlag (1999) supplied the complementary small-value estimate

$$
\boxed{
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P(m_n\le\delta n^{-1/2})
\le C\delta
}
$$

for every $\delta>0$ and a universal constant $C$. Together these results indicate a typical scale of order $n^{-1/2}$, although they do not by themselves identify a limiting distribution.

These historical findings, and precise references to the 1987, 1994 and 1999 papers, appear in Cook and Nguyen's original 2021 introduction. Their older complete proofs were not independently retrieved during this tranche.

### Cook and Nguyen's 2021 exponential limit law

Nicholas A. Cook and Hoi H. Nguyen, *Universality of the minimum modulus for random trigonometric polynomials*, *Discrete Analysis* **2021:20**, 46 pages, [DOI 10.19086/da.28985](https://doi.org/10.19086/da.28985), proved that the minimum modulus has a universal exponential limiting law for a broad class of iid centered sub-Gaussian coefficients, including Rademacher signs.

The [original paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07203) was retrieved, and Theorems 1.1–1.2 and Corollary 1.5 on printed pp.2–3 were visually inspected. The authors first normalize a polynomial with $2r+1$ coefficients,

$$
G_r(e^{it})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r+1}}
\sum_{j=-r}^{r}\varepsilon_je^{ijt}.
$$

Define $\mathfrak m_r=\min_t|G_r(e^{it})|$. Their Gaussian theorem, extended by their universality result to fair signs, gives for $\tau>0$

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb P(r\mathfrak m_r>\tau)
\longrightarrow e^{-2\sqrt{\pi/3}\,\tau}.
}
$$

The normalization matters. Multiplying $G_r$ by $\sqrt{2r+1}$ and by the unimodular factor $e^{irt}$ produces a standard **unscaled** degree-$2r$ sign polynomial $P_{2r}$. Thus its minimum is $\sqrt{2r+1}\,\mathfrak m_r$. Writing $N=2r$,

$$
\sqrt N\,m_N
=\sqrt{(2r)(2r+1)}\,\mathfrak m_r
\sim 2r\,\mathfrak m_r.
$$

Consequently the unscaled polynomial has the limiting survival law

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb P(\sqrt N\,m_N>\tau)
\longrightarrow e^{-\sqrt{\pi/3}\,\tau},
\qquad \tau>0.
}
$$

The authors explain that the parity of the number of coefficients does not change the limit. The **factor of two** in the exponent is essential: copying $2\sqrt{\pi/3}$ directly from the normalized $r\mathfrak m_r$ theorem to the unscaled $\sqrt N\,m_N$ variable would be wrong.

This limiting distribution has no point mass at zero, and since $\sqrt N$ grows, it implies $m_N\to0$ **in probability**. It therefore answers Erdős's original typical-polynomial question affirmatively.

The proof develops small-ball estimates, a high-dimensional local central limit theorem and an analysis of the arithmetic structure of evaluation angles. The full forty-six-page argument was **not** independently reconstructed by GCL. The peer-reviewed published theorem is the mathematical solution.
