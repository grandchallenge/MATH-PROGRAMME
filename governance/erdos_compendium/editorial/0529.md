**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #529 remains **OPEN** in the dimensions that make it difficult. It asks how far a uniformly chosen **self-avoiding walk** travels from its starting point, on average. High-dimensional walks are now known to have the square-root displacement scale, but the precise critical exponents in dimensions two, three and four have not been established for the ordinary lattice model.

## The question in plain language

A nearest-neighbour self-avoiding walk of $n$ steps in $\mathbb Z^k$ is an ordered sequence of distinct vertices

$$
\gamma_0=0,\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_n,
\qquad \|\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_i\|_2=1.
$$

Choose such a walk **uniformly** among all $c_n(k)$ possibilities. Let

$$
d_k(n)=\mathbb E_{\mathrm{SAW}_{n,k}}\|\gamma_n\|_2
=\frac1{c_n(k)}\sum_{\gamma}\|\gamma_n\|_2
$$

be its expected **Euclidean distance** from the origin at the last step.

Erdős asks two questions:

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_2(n)}{\sqrt n}=\infty?}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{d_k(n)=O(\sqrt n)\quad\text{for all }k\ge3?}
$$

The second question, as formulated in 1961, is now expected to have a **negative answer in dimensions three and four**, though it is true in dimensions five and higher. This is a prediction from scaling theory, not an established refutation in $\mathbb Z^3$ or $\mathbb Z^4$.

The expected *position vector* is zero, by reflection symmetry. That is **not** the quantity Erdős asks about. He asks for the expectation of the *length* of the endpoint vector, which is positive. Similarly,

$$
\mathbb E\|\gamma_n\|_2
\ne\sqrt{\mathbb E\|\gamma_n\|_2^2}
$$

in general. Conflating these two statistics can create invalid conclusions about the original problem.

## A small example

On the square lattice, every one-step self-avoiding walk ends at distance one, so

$$
c_1(2)=4,\qquad d_2(1)=1.
$$

For two steps there are twelve self-avoiding walks. Four go straight in one of the four coordinate directions, ending at distance two. Eight turn once, ending at a diagonal displacement of distance $\sqrt2$.

Therefore the expected distance is

$$
\boxed{d_2(2)=\frac{4\cdot2+8\sqrt2}{12}
=\frac{2+2\sqrt2}{3}.}
$$

The expected **squared** distance is a different number,

$$
\mathbb E\|\gamma_2\|_2^2
=\frac{4\cdot4+8\cdot2}{12}=\frac83.
$$

The general Jensen inequality gives

$$
d_k(n)\le\sqrt{\mathbb E\|\gamma_n\|_2^2},
$$

but equality need not hold.

In one dimension, a self-avoiding walk cannot reverse direction. There are exactly two possible walks, ending at $+n$ and $-n$, so $d_1(n)=n$. This is a useful caution: excluding self-intersections does not automatically give the diffusive $\sqrt n$ scale.

## What is known

### Erdős's 1961 conjectures

Erdős introduced the displacement problem immediately after the connective constant in *Some unsolved problems*, [printed p.254](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=34), Section V.5. That original page was directly inspected.

The problem arose partly from models of long polymer chains. Erdős anticipated that self-avoidance would make the average displacement in the plane grow faster than the square root of the walk length, while proposing the square-root upper bound for dimensions at least three.

The maintained [Erdős #529 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/529) makes explicit a more modern set of predictions:

$$
d_2(n)\sim D_2 n^{3/4},
$$

$$
d_3(n)\approx n^\nu,\qquad \nu\approx0.59,
$$

and

$$
d_4(n)\sim D_4\sqrt n\,(\log n)^{1/8}.
$$

The first two exponents, and the logarithmic correction in four dimensions, are **conjectural for ordinary self-avoiding walks on the integer lattices**. The notation $\approx$ for the three-dimensional exponent denotes a physics-driven prediction rather than a proven exact constant or asymptotic equivalent.

These predictions imply superdiffusive movement in dimensions two and three and a logarithmic enhancement in dimension four. They explain why Erdős's all‑$k\ge3$ upper bound is not currently expected to hold in dimensions three and four. But a numerical or physical prediction is **not a rigorous counterexample**.

### Hara and Slade: the high-dimensional theorem

Takashi Hara and Gordon Slade, *Self-avoiding walk in five or more dimensions. I. The critical behaviour*, *Communications in Mathematical Physics* **147** (1992), 101–136, [DOI 10.1007/BF02099530](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02099530), established the high-dimensional theory using the **lace expansion**, together with a companion convergence paper.

Their original publication abstract states that when $k\ge5$, the mean-squared displacement is asymptotically linear in $n$, and the diffusively rescaled walk converges in distribution to Brownian motion. In particular,

$$
\mathbb E\|\gamma_n\|_2^2\sim D_k n
$$

for some positive constant $D_k$. By Jensen,

$$
d_k(n)\le \sqrt{D_kn}\,(1+o(1)).
$$

Thus **the original proposed $O(\sqrt n)$ upper bound is true for $k\ge5$**. Moreover, convergence to a nondegenerate Gaussian together with bounded rescaled second moments gives convergence of the first radial moment, hence the stronger diffusive relation $d_k(n)\sim C_k\sqrt n$ for a positive constant $C_k$.

The primary journal abstract and its assumptions were checked. The complete lace-expansion proof has not been independently reproduced here.

### Duminil-Copin and Hammond: sub-ballisticity in all dimensions

Hugo Duminil-Copin and Alan Hammond, *Self-avoiding walk is sub-ballistic*, *Communications in Mathematical Physics* **324** (2013), 401–423, [DOI 10.1007/s00220-013-1811-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00220-013-1811-1), obtained an important unconditional theorem for **every dimension $k\ge2$**.

Their complete original [27-page preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.0401) was retrieved and Theorem 1.1 read. For every fixed speed $v>0$, there is some $\eta(v,k)>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb P_{\mathrm{SAW}_{n,k}}
\left(\max_{0\le j\le n}\|\gamma_j\|_2\ge vn\right)
\le e^{-\eta n}.
}
$$

This says a uniformly chosen long self-avoiding walk is **sub-ballistic**: it is exponentially unlikely to travel a fixed positive fraction of its step count away from the origin.

There is a direct consequence for expected endpoint distance. Since $\|\gamma_n\|\le n$, split the expectation according to whether the maximum displacement exceeds $vn$. The theorem gives

$$
\frac{d_k(n)}n\le v+e^{-\eta n}.
$$

For fixed $v$, let $n\to\infty$, then let $v\downarrow0$. It follows that

$$
\boxed{d_k(n)=o(n)\quad(k\ge2).}
$$

This was a substantial advance on the 1961 state of knowledge, but it is **weaker** than determining whether $d_2(n)/\sqrt n\to\infty$, or establishing the predicted $n^{3/4}$ scale. Sub-ballistic growth still allows many behaviours between $\sqrt n$ and $n$.

## What remains

The next bounded work is to reconcile short-walk counts with the companion #528 enumeration, verify $d_2(2)$ exactly and establish the elementary consequences of the 2013 theorem.

The deeper research obstacle is to prove the low-dimensional scaling behaviour, particularly planar superdiffusion and the predicted three- and four-dimensional corrections. Larger finite enumeration is useful evidence but **not a proof** of an asymptotic critical exponent.
