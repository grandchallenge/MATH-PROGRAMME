## The question in plain language

Problem 304 asks how many distinct unit fractions are needed to write a rational number as an Egyptian fraction. For integers $1 \le a < b$, define $N(a,b)$ to be the least $k$ for which there are integers

$$
1 < n_1 < n_2 < \cdots < n_k
$$

such that

$$
\frac{a}{b}=\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}.
$$

The strict inequalities make the denominators distinct and put them in increasing order. The worst case at denominator $b$ is

$$
N(b)=\max_{1\le a<b}N(a,b).
$$

The open question is whether this worst-case number grows as slowly as a constant multiple of $\log\log b$. Here $\ll$ means “bounded above by a constant multiple of,” with an absolute constant independent of $a$ and $b$.

The wording matters. This is a uniform question over every numerator in the interval, not merely a question about one fraction or about the average number of terms. It also asks for distinct denominators greater than one.

## A small example

The pinned formal source includes the test case $N(2,15)=2$. Its two-term representation is directly checkable:

$$
\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{30}=\frac{3}{30}+\frac{1}{30}=\frac{4}{30}=\frac{2}{15}.
$$

One term cannot work. If $2/15=1/m$, then $m=15/2$, which is not an integer. Thus two terms are sufficient and one is impossible, so the minimum is exactly two. This is an exact finite calculation, not a floating-point experiment; it checks one small instance only. It does not bound $N(b)$ for general $b$, settle the asymptotic question, or certify the surrounding Lean theorems.

## What is known

The readable primary source is P. Erdős, “Az $1/x_1+1/x_2+\cdots+1/x_n=a/b$ egyenlet egész számú megoldásairól,” *Mat. Lapok* 1 (1950), 192–210. The scan states the same definition of $N(a,b)$. On printed page 195, Theorem 1 gives an absolute-constant upper bound of order $\log b/\log\log b$ for every $1\le a<b$. On printed page 196, Theorem 2 gives a lower bound of order $\log\log b$ for the average and separately proves $N(b-1,b)>\log\log b-1$. The paper’s final English summary also records these results and says that the $\log\log b$ upper bound is expected, rather than proved.

The maintained problem entry attributes the stronger uniform upper bound

$$
N(b)\ll\sqrt{\log b}
$$

to M. D. Vose, “Egyptian Fractions,” *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* 17 (1985), 21–24, DOI [10.1112/blms/17.1.21](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms/17.1.21). The citation and result are also recorded in the pinned Formal Conjectures file. This audit confirms the paper’s bibliographic record and the attribution, but does not claim a line-by-line verification of Vose’s proof from an accessible full text.

There is a small literal indexing difference worth preserving. The current web entry displays the average over $1\le a<b$; the scanned Erdős theorem writes the displayed sum through $b-2$ and treats $N(b-1,b)$ separately. Those formulations have the same coarse order for the recorded lower bound, but they should not be silently called identical. The formal definition of the envelope uses `Finset.Ico 1 b`, which includes $1$ through $b-1$.
