# Erdős #980 — Average least power nonresidues

## The question and its precise domain

Fix $k\ge2$. Modulo a prime $p$, call an integer $a$ a *power nonresidue with exponent $k$* when there is no integer $x$ with $x^k\equiv a\pmod p$. Let $n_k(p)$ be the least positive nonresidue, when one exists. Erdős asked for a positive constant $c_k$ such that

$$
\sum_{p<x}n_k(p)\sim c_k\frac{x}{\log x}.
$$

The maintained [catalogue #980](https://www.erdosproblems.com/980) says **PROVED**, citing Erdős for $k=2$ and P. D. T. A. Elliott for general $k$. That standing must retain **Elliott's convention about which primes contribute**. For composite $k$, an apparently natural all-eligible-primes reading is a different variant not covered by his published theorem. This is a source-fidelity issue, not a claim that Elliott's result is incorrect.

## When a nonresidue exists

The multiplicative group $(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times$ is cyclic of order $p-1$. The homomorphism $x\mapsto x^k$ on this group has image size

$$
\frac{p-1}{\gcd(k,p-1)}.
$$

Consequently **a nonresidue exists if and only if $\gcd(k,p-1)>1$**. When the greatest common divisor is one, the power map is bijective and even $0$ is a power of exponent $k$, so $n_k(p)$ is **undefined** unless a separate convention is supplied. This logical point is particularly important when the average runs over *all* primes.

Elliott's 1967 Theorem 1 adopts the following definition:

$$
n_k^{\mathrm{El}}(p)=
\begin{cases}
\text{least positive $k$-th power nonresidue mod $p$},&
p\equiv1\pmod k,\\
0,&p\not\equiv1\pmod k.
\end{cases}
$$

If $k$ is prime, the condition $\gcd(k,p-1)>1$ is equivalent to $p\equiv1\pmod k$. For **composite $k$**, there can be extra primes with nonresidues but $p\not\equiv1\pmod k$.

## A composite-exponent example

For $k=4$ and $p=7$, direct exponentiation gives

$$
\{x^4\bmod7:x=0,\ldots,6\}=\{0,1,2,4\}.
$$

Thus $3$ is the least fourth-power nonresidue modulo $7$, but $7\equiv3\pmod4$, so Elliott's convention gives $n_4^{\mathrm{El}}(7)=0$. This discrepancy occurs throughout additional congruence classes, not only at a finite list of primes.

For comparison, at $k=2,p=7$ the quadratic residues are also $\{0,1,2,4\}$ and $n_2(7)=3$. There is no comparable domain ambiguity for prime exponent $k=2$.

An alternate sum that includes **every** prime with $\gcd(k,p-1)>1$ contains extra terms, each nonnegative, for composite $k$. The detailed [2026 statement audit](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/integer_sequences/E0980) records **no primary theorem covering that entire additional contribution**. Calling Elliott's specific result “proved” does not, by itself, prove this extended version.

## The classical theorems

Erdős's 1961 paper [Er61e] proves the quadratic mean

$$
\sum_{p<x}n_2(p)\sim
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q_j}{2^j}\right)\frac{x}{\log x},
$$

where $q_j$ denotes the prime with index $j$, $q_1=2,q_2=3,\ldots$. The series converges absolutely, and its constant is strictly positive.

Elliott, [*A problem of Erdős concerning power residue sums*](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-13-2-131-149), *Acta Arithmetica* **13** (1967), pp.131–149, Theorem 1, proved the fixed exponent $k$ asymptotic for $n_k^{\mathrm{El}}(p)$ for every integer $k\ge2$. In the prime exponent $k$ case his paper gives explicit series expressions for the constants. The theorem's analytic proof is a published mathematical result; it is **not independently reproduced** in this editorial pass.

A [separate 1968 corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-14-4-437), *Acta Arithmetica* **14**, p.437, changes the documentary status of the original printed paper. An exact proof reconstruction must read that one-page correction before fixing theorem fingerprints or equations.

By the prime number theorem, the displayed fixed exponent $k$ result is equivalent to an average over all prime moduli,

$$
\frac1{\pi(x)}\sum_{p<x}n_k^{\mathrm{El}}(p)\longrightarrow c_k>0.
$$

It is an **average theorem**, not a bound on every individual least nonresidue. Averaging only over primes $p\equiv1\pmod{k}$ with a different denominator would alter the constant.
