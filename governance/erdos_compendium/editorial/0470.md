## The question in plain language

A positive integer is *semiperfect* when some subset of its distinct **proper** divisors sums to the number itself. It is *weird* when its divisor sum exceeds twice the number but it is not semiperfect. A weird number is *primitive* if no proper divisor is weird. [Erdős #470](https://www.erdosproblems.com/470) asks two separate questions: does an **odd** weird integer exist, and are there infinitely many primitive weird numbers?

## A small example

The divisors of 70 are 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 14, 35, 70 and their sum is 144. Its proper divisors sum to 74, exceeding 70 by four. A subset of proper divisors summing to 70 would have a complementary subset summing to four, but the only proper divisors at most four are 1 and 2, summing to at most three. Hence 70 is weird, although **even**.

The odd number 945 is abundant, with divisor sum 1920 and surplus 30. Since 27 and 3 are proper divisors summing to 30, their complement in the proper-divisor list sums to 945. Thus 945 is semiperfect, not odd weird.

## What is known

Benkoski and Erdős (1974) established foundational results on weird numbers. Fang's [2022 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12906) states that no odd weird number lies below $10^{21}$; it also excludes odd weird numbers below $10^{28}$ **only when** the abundance surplus is less than $10^{14}$. The second result is conditional, not an unconditional exclusion of the whole interval. GCL has not independently replayed the expensive computational searches.

An odd weird number of least size, if one exists, must be primitive abundant. This uses the elementary observation that multiples of semiperfect numbers remain semiperfect.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1740](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1740#issuecomment-6098593447) reconciles both questions, reproduces the arithmetic examples, and checks source qualifiers. The work is not a formal proof of either global question.

## What remains

Finite odd-number searches cannot exclude unbounded examples. A theorem about the abundance of weird numbers in general does not establish infinitely many *primitive* weird numbers. Both questions remain open in the reviewed dated record.
