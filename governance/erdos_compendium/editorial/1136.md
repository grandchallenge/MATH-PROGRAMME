## The question in plain language

Can we choose **more than one-third** of the positive integers, in the sense of lower natural density, so that the sum of **any two chosen numbers** is never a power of two?

[Erdős Problem #1136](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1136) asks for a set $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ with

$$
\underline d(A)>\frac13,\qquad a+b\ne2^k
$$

for every $a,b\in A$ and $k\ge0$. Self-sums $a+a$ are included.

The answer is **yes**: a construction of Müller has density exactly $1/2$, and the published theorem says this is optimal.

## A small example

Let $A$ consist of the positive integers whose **odd part** is congruent to $3$ modulo $4$. Equivalently, each has the form

$$
a=2^i(4m+3),\qquad i,m\ge0.
$$

If two such integers have different powers of two dividing them, their sum has an odd factor larger than one. If their powers agree, then their sum is $2^{i+1}$ times an odd number congruent to $3\bmod4$. In neither case is the sum a pure power of two.

The finite count is slightly subtler than saying exactly half: through $2^{14}=16384$ there are **8,191** members. The limiting density is nevertheless $1/2$.

## What is known

Erdős posed the question at the 1987 Berlin DMV conference, as reported by Müller (2011). The simple set of multiples of three gives the threshold density $1/3$.

**Müller (2011)** constructed the set above and established that no admissible set has lower density exceeding $1/2$. Thus the maximum possible lower density is exactly one-half.

The maintained catalogue also labels the problem **PROVED (LEAN)**, but its formalization metadata does not provide a directly verified theorem in the older GCL snapshot. An external formal-proof label is not an independent GCL kernel check.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2407](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2407#issuecomment-6181184438) contains the explicit modular sum argument. Exact enumeration through 16,384 confirms the finite count and excludes all power-of-two pair sums at that bound.

This independently verifies the **construction**, not Müller's general upper-density optimality proof.
