**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #502 is **SOLVED in its polynomial upper-bound formulation**, by Bannai, Bannai and Stanton (1983). The **exact maximum** number of points with two distinct distances is not generally known. Both facts must be stated together.

## The question in plain language

A finite set $A$ in Euclidean space $\mathbb R^d$ is a **two-distance set** if the distances between distinct pairs of points take exactly **two different positive values**.

Let $M_2(d)$ be the maximum possible size of such a set. Erdős records a question posed by Coxeter: how many points in $d$-dimensional space guarantee that **more than two distinct distances** must occur? The answer is one more than the maximum size of a set with *at most* two distances.

Erdős originally thought he could prove a polynomial bound, but corrected an error in his argument. A successful later theorem of Eiichi Bannai, Etsuko Bannai and Dennis Stanton gives

$$
\boxed{|A|\le\binom{d+2}{2}=\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}2.}
$$

In particular, a two-distance set contains at most order $d^2$ points. This answers the original question of whether a polynomial number suffices. It does **not** assert that the exact extremal number equals $\binom{d+2}{2}$ for every dimension.

The maintained [Erdős #502 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/502) accordingly classifies the **upper-bound question as SOLVED**, while recording a gap between available general lower and upper bounds.

## A small example

Take all vectors with exactly two coordinates equal to $1$ and the rest $0$ in $\mathbb R^{d+1}$. Denote this set by

$$
A_d=\{e_i+e_j:1\le i<j\le d+1\},
$$

where $e_i$ are the coordinate unit vectors. It contains

$$
|A_d|=\binom{d+1}{2}
$$

different points. Every vector lies in the affine hyperplane

$$
x_1+\cdots+x_{d+1}=2,
$$

which has dimension $d$. Therefore the construction is a valid configuration in **$d$-dimensional Euclidean space**, despite using $d+1$ coordinates to describe it.

Let $u=e_i+e_j$ and $v=e_k+e_\ell$ be different points. Their squared distance is

$$
\|u-v\|^2=4-2|\{i,j\}\cap\{k,\ell\}|.
$$

Two distinct two-element subsets intersect in either zero or one element, so the squared distances are **$4$ and $2$**, giving distances $2$ and $\sqrt2$.

When $d\ge3$, both intersection types occur. Thus

$$
\binom{d+1}{2}\le M_2(d)\le\binom{d+2}{2}
\qquad(d\ge3).
$$

For $d=3$, the six points $e_i+e_j$ in $\mathbb R^4$ lie in a three-dimensional hyperplane and realize two distinct distances. For $d=1$ or $2$, this particular weight-two construction does **not** realize two different distances, so it should not be presented as a universal witness for all dimensions.

These bounds have the same quadratic leading term but differ by $d+1$; that does not settle the exact extremum.

## What is known

### The original Coxeter question and first solution

Erdős's *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.244, beginning of Section III, records Coxeter's question about the number of points required to force three different distances. Erdős explicitly retracts his own earlier claimed polynomial argument. The [original printed page](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=24) was visually inspected.

E. Bannai, E. Bannai and D. Stanton proved the general **$s$-distance theorem** in *An upper bound for the cardinality of an $s$-distance subset in real Euclidean space. II*, *Combinatorica* **3** (1983), 147–152, [DOI 10.1007/BF02579288](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579288):

$$
|A|\le\binom{d+s}{s}
\quad\text{for every finite }s\text{-distance set }A\subset\mathbb R^d.
$$

The two-distance bound above is the specialization $s=2$. The published theorem and bibliographic record were checked; the complete 1983 original proof body was not independently obtained.

### A complete, shorter published proof

Fedor Petrov and Cosmin Pohoata, *A remark on sets with few distances in $\mathbb R^d$*, *Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society* **149** (2021), 569–571, supplied a concise alternative proof, combining a polynomial evaluation matrix with **Sylvester's law of inertia**. The [complete three-page author preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.08181) was visually inspected, including Theorem 1.1 on p.1, Theorem 1.2 on p.2 and the final deduction on p.3.

Here is the central argument. Let the $s$ positive pairwise distances determined by $A$ be $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_s$. Define

$$
p(x,y)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}
\bigl(\delta_j^2-\|x-y\|^2\bigr)
$$

and evaluate it on pairs of points of $A$ to form a square real matrix $P=(p(a,b))_{a,b\in A}$.

When $a\ne b$, their distance equals one of the $\delta_j$, and consequently $p(a,b)=0$. When $a=b$,

$$
p(a,a)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\delta_j^2>0.
$$

Thus $P$ is a **positive scalar multiple of the identity**, and its positive inertia index (the number of positive eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity) is exactly $|A|$.

The key polynomial lemma from Petrov and Pohoata says that if a real bivariate polynomial $p(x,y)$ has total degree at most $2s+1$, the positive inertia index of its evaluation quadratic form on $A$ is at most the dimension of polynomial functions of degree at most $s$ restricted to $A$. That dimension is at most

$$
\dim\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_d]_{\le s}=\binom{d+s}{s}.
$$

Why does the inertia lemma hold? Consider the subspace $W$ of coefficient functions $f:A\to\mathbb R$ whose sums against every monomial of total degree at most $s$ vanish:

$$
\sum_{a\in A}f(a)a^\nu=0
\qquad (|\nu|\le s).
$$

Its codimension is at most the polynomial-space dimension $D$. In a monomial $x^\alpha y^\beta$ of total degree at most $2s+1$, at least one of $|\alpha|,|\beta|$ is at most $s$. Hence its evaluation bilinear form vanishes whenever **both coefficient functions lie in $W$**. By linearity, the quadratic form attached to $P$ is zero on $W$.

A subspace $Y$ on which the symmetric quadratic form is positive definite cannot meet $W$ except at zero. Therefore $\dim Y+\dim W\le|A|$, so its dimension, and hence the positive inertia index, is at most $D$. Applying this to the diagonal matrix $P$ gives

$$
|A|=r_+(P)\le D\le\binom{d+s}{s},
$$

as claimed. This is a complete explanation of the mathematical mechanism in the published alternative proof, not a new independent theorem.

## What remains

The bounded next verification is to implement the weight-two-vector construction for dimensions $3$ through $8$ and check every pair of vectors using exact integer squared distances. The evaluation polynomial

$$
p(u,v)=(2-\|u-v\|^2)(4-\|u-v\|^2)
$$

should give the matrix $8I$ on the construction. This checks a concrete instance of the general proof and guards against accidentally counting ambient dimension $d+1$ instead of the affine dimension $d$.

The exact value of $M_2(d)$ in arbitrary dimensions is still a separate extremal geometry question. Protected article admission and any external Lean compilation or axiomatic audit are further tasks.
