## The question in plain language

How many distinct distances must $n$ points in the plane determine if they are in **general position** in the following strong sense: no three are collinear and no four lie on one circle? Write $h(n)$ for the largest lower bound guaranteed for every such set.

Erdős #98 asks whether

$$
\frac{h(n)}{n}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

It is not enough to exhibit individual point sets with many distances. The conjecture claims a superlinear number of distances in **every** sufficiently large configuration satisfying both restrictions.

## A small example

Take the four points $(i,i^2)$ with $i=1,2,3,4$. They are not collinear three at a time: a straight line intersects the parabola $y=x^2$ in at most two points. Nor are they concyclic: substituting $y=x^2$ into an arbitrary circle equation produces a monic quartic whose cubic coefficient is zero. Four positive distinct roots would have a positive sum, contradicting Vieta's formula.

The same proof gives, for any $n$, a concrete integer-coordinate family $(i,i^2)$, $1\le i\le n$, with neither three collinear nor four concyclic. For indices $i\ne j$, the squared distance is exactly

$$
d_{ij}^2=(i-j)^2\bigl[1+(i+j)^2\bigr].
$$

This is a useful exact test family, not a sharp extremal construction.

## What is known

The conjecture remains **OPEN**. The maintained [Erdős #98](https://www.erdosproblems.com/98) attributes a construction with fewer than $n^{\log_2 3}$ distinct distances to Pach, and a stronger upper construction of order

$$
n\exp\bigl(C\sqrt{\log n}\bigr)
$$

to Erdős, Füredi, Pach and Ruzsa. Their primary paper [*The grid revisited*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0012-365X(93)90155-M), *Discrete Mathematics* 111 (1993), 189–196, explicitly states in its published abstract that planar general-position sets can have $o(n^{1+\varepsilon})$ distances for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$. These are **upper examples** for the minimum $h(n)$, not lower bounds proving superlinearity.

A simple elementary lower bound comes from the no-four-concyclic hypothesis. For any fixed distance, each point has at most three others at that distance, so its multiplicity among unordered pairs is at most $3n/2$. As there are $\binom n2$ pairs, at least $(n-1)/3$ distinct distances are needed. This is still only linear.

## GCL's current state

The pinned 2025 catalogue and maintained #98 classify it as **OPEN**. The original issue is [#1367](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1367). The original Erdős–Füredi–Pach–Ruzsa abstract and exact general-position assumptions were checked; the full 1993 construction has not been independently rebuilt.

The original issue contains an authenticated [RESULT/1 source report](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1367#issuecomment-6161022683). This is unadjudicated source evidence only, not independent mathematical certification or editorial admission.

## What remains

A bounded successor is to formalize the Vieta argument for the parabola example and the degree-three equal-distance incidence bound. Acceptance: for each $n\le10$, enumerate the exact squared distances $d_{ij}^2$, verify no collinear triples or concyclic quadruples, and check the lower bound $(n-1)/3$ for every input. Stop without inferring $h(n)/n\to\infty$ from finite samples.
