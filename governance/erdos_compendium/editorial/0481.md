The modern nonnegative-coefficient theorem is established, and its elementary argument can be reconstructed directly. The older historical formulation needs an additional sign-domain audit; there is no independently certified GCL Lean proof.

## The question in plain language

Choose several rules for transforming positive integers. Rule $i$ takes a number $x$ to

$$
f_i(x)=a_ix+b_i,
$$

where $a_i$ is a positive integer and $b_i$ is a nonnegative integer.

Begin with the list $A_1=(1)$. Apply **every** rule to **every** entry of that list, keeping all outputs even if some coincide. Call the resulting list $A_2$, and repeat to make $A_3,A_4,\ldots$.

Suppose the multipliers obey

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle \frac1{a_1}+\frac1{a_2}+\cdots+\frac1{a_r}>1.}
$$

Must we eventually obtain **the same number in two different list positions**?

**Yes.** This is Erdős Problem #481, in its modern positive/nonnegative-parameter form. Although the lists branch exponentially, they cannot keep their integer values distinct forever.

The word *list* matters. If two different routes reach the same integer, both occurrences remain and constitute a collision. Converting each generation to a mathematical set and deleting duplicates would erase exactly what the problem asks us to find.

## A small example

Take three rules:

$$
f_1(x)=2x,\qquad f_2(x)=2x+1,\qquad f_3(x)=3x+1.
$$

The reciprocal sum is

$$
\frac12+\frac12+\frac13=\frac43>1.
$$

Starting with $A_1=(1)$ gives $A_2=(2,3,4)$.

At the next generation, two different routes produce $7$:

$$
f_3(f_1(1))=f_3(2)=7,
$$

and

$$
f_2(f_2(1))=f_2(3)=7.
$$

Thus $A_3$ already contains a repeated integer. This is a complete finite witness for these particular rules, not the reason the theorem works for **every** allowed collection.

There is a useful edge case. Let $f_1(x)=x$ be the identity map and $f_2(x)=2x$. Then the reciprocal sum is $1+1/2>1$, but the smallest element of every generation is still $1$, because repeatedly applying the identity does nothing.

Nevertheless a collision appears: applying the identity and then doubling gives the same number as doubling and then applying the identity.

This corrects a potential shortcut in statements of the problem: the minimum of the lists does **not** necessarily grow by one at each step when an identity map is permitted. Such a claim is valid only after handling the identity case or imposing a stronger positivity condition.

## What is known

### The historical result

The original problem appears in Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 96. The authors considered affine transformations $x\mapsto a_ix+b_i$ and the condition $\sum_i1/a_i>1$. They asked whether repeated application forces collisions under a stated lower-bound condition on the resulting integers.

The 1980 page says the $a_i$ and $b_i$ are **integers** without explicitly spelling out the modern assumptions $a_i>0$, $b_i\ge0$. The original phrasing also refers to a bound $B$ on the values reached. The modern catalogue instead states the cleaner theorem for nonnegative integer parameters and finite lists. It would be inaccurate to claim these formulations have been proved equivalent for every possible signed choice without a separate audit.

The first solution is attributed to **David A. Klarner**, *A Sufficient Condition for Certain Semigroups to Be Free*, *Journal of Algebra* **74** (1982), 140–148.

A complete later primary mathematical proof appears in **Alexander Kolpakov and Alexey Talambutsa**, *On free semigroups of affine maps on the real line*, *Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society* **150** (2022), 2301–2307. Their Theorem 3 establishes a stronger statement: when $a_i$ are positive integers, $b_i$ are **rational numbers**, and $\sum_i1/a_i>1$, the resulting affine maps cannot generate a free semigroup.

Informally, *non-free* means that two different strings of transformations give the **same affine function**. Their proof produces a collision of words of a common length. Evaluating those two functions at the starting value $1$ then gives two identical values within the same generation. This implies the modern Erdős problem.

The reverse inference is invalid: two different affine maps may agree at $x=1$ without being the same function. The semigroup theorem is the stronger assertion.

### A direct proof for the modern theorem

There is also an instructive elementary proof, based on harmonic sums, that does not need the full semigroup theorem.

Assume for contradiction that **every generation contains only distinct positive integers**. Define

$$
C=\sum_{i=1}^r\frac1{a_i}>1,
\qquad
D=\sum_{i=1}^r\frac{b_i}{a_i^2}\ge0.
$$

Write $S_k$ for the sum of the reciprocals of the numbers appearing in the list $A_k$:

$$
S_k=\sum_{x\in A_k}\frac1x.
$$

If an identity map $f_i(x)=x$ is present, then $r\ge2$, and composing it with any differently labelled rule in both orders gives a collision within the third generation. So under our contradiction hypothesis **no rule is the identity**.

For $x\ge1$, every remaining rule satisfies

$$
f_i(x)=a_ix+b_i\ge x+1.
$$

Therefore the smallest value in $A_k$ is at least $k$. Every entry $x$ of $A_k$ obeys $x\ge k$.

Now use the exact identity

$$
\frac1{a_ix+b_i}
=\frac1{a_ix}
-\frac{b_i}{a_ix(a_ix+b_i)}.
$$

Because $a_i\ge1$ and $b_i\ge0$, its last term is bounded by

$$
0\le
\frac{b_i}{a_ix(a_ix+b_i)}
\le\frac{b_i}{a_i^2x^2}.
$$

Summing over the transformations and over every input in $A_k$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{k+1}
&\ge C S_k-D\sum_{x\in A_k}\frac1{x^2}\\
&\ge\left(C-\frac Dk\right)S_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second inequality uses $x\ge k$, so $1/x^2\le1/(kx)$.

Since $C>1$, for all sufficiently large $k$ we have

$$
C-\frac Dk>q
$$

for some fixed number $q>1$. Iterating the previous inequality forces **exponential** growth:

$$
S_k\ge q^{k-K}S_K\qquad(k\ge K).
$$

But this is impossible if every entry remains distinct. At generation $k$, the list has exactly $r^{k-1}$ positions. Among all collections of that many distinct positive integers, the largest reciprocal sum is attained by the smallest ones, $1,2,\ldots,r^{k-1}$. Hence

$$
S_k\le H_{r^{k-1}}
=\sum_{j=1}^{r^{k-1}}\frac1j
\le1+(k-1)\log r.
$$

The right side grows only **linearly** in $k$.

A positive quantity cannot grow at least exponentially and at most linearly for arbitrarily large $k$. This contradiction proves that some generation must contain a repeated integer.

This is a reconstruction of known mathematics, consistent with an independently posted harmonic-weighting argument in the maintained problem discussion. It is not a novel solution or a formal machine-checked theorem.

### Why the minimum-element condition matters

The lower bound $\min A_k\ge k$ is what makes the error term $D/k$ vanish as the generations advance. Without that estimate, the coefficient in the harmonic recurrence need not approach $C>1$.

The identity-map example $f_1(x)=x$, $f_2(x)=2x$ shows why a careless proof would be wrong. The correct argument separates identity maps first, proves they cause an immediate collision, and uses minimum growth only when none exists.

More generally, if all offsets satisfy $b_i\ge1$, the minimum-growth estimate holds automatically without an identity exception.

## What remains

The first bounded source task is to obtain Klarner's complete 1982 *Journal of Algebra* proof and compare its hypotheses directly with the 1980 page and the newer 2022 Theorem 3. The sign restrictions on $a_i$ and $b_i$ must be explicit. A theorem for $a_i>0$, $b_i\ge0$ must not be silently advertised as a theorem for arbitrary signed affine transformations.

The second task is formalization of the harmonic proof under a pinned Lean environment. Its reusable components are straightforward to state: the identity-map collision, the lower bound on the minimum when no identity occurs, the exact reciprocal identity, the harmonic upper bound for distinct lists, and the contradiction between exponential and linear growth. Source-level declarations with unresolved placeholders do not pass this gate.

The third task is independent replay of sample affine systems, with each list entry retaining its **transformation-word provenance** so two equal outputs from different histories are not accidentally collapsed. The criterion $C>1$ is sufficient but not necessary: examples with $C=1$ can still collide.

Completion of these tasks would strengthen source fidelity and formal proof confidence. It would not constitute a new mathematical resolution of an already proved result.
