## The question in plain language

Consider a trigonometric polynomial of the special form

$$
f(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_k e^{ik\theta},
\qquad \max_{0\le\theta\le2\pi}|f(\theta)|=1.
$$

Assume **all zeros**, interpreted through the corresponding algebraic polynomial $P(z)=\sum c_kz^k$, lie on the unit circle: their arguments $\theta$ are real. Erdős asked whether the integral of the modulus satisfies

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|f(\theta)|\,d\theta\le4.
$$

The normalization matters. Replacing the maximum by the average, or allowing algebraic zeros off the unit circle, changes the inequality.

## A small example

Set $P(z)=(1+z)/2$. Then $f(\theta)=e^{i\theta/2}\cos(\theta/2)$, its unique algebraic root is $z=-1$, and its maximum modulus is one. Direct integration gives

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|f(\theta)|\,d\theta
=\int_0^{2\pi}|\cos(\theta/2)|\,d\theta=4.
$$

Therefore the constant four **cannot be reduced**. The polynomial $P(z)=(1+z)^2/4$ has the same zero on the circle with multiplicity two, but its integral is $\pi$, demonstrating that the constant is not attained by every admissible polynomial.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Saff and Sheil-Small, *Coefficient and integral mean estimates for algebraic and trigonometric polynomials with restricted zeros*, *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* (1974/75), proved the statement for **complex coefficients**. Kristiansen's independent 1974 proof addressed real coefficients; a flaw in an original argument was corrected in his 1976 erratum. It is important to cite the erratum rather than treating all versions of the early proof as equally sound.

The geometric root constraint is the substantial hypothesis. Without it, the trivial normalization gives only $\int |f|\le2\pi$. In particular, the sharp bound four is not merely the triangle inequality applied coefficient by coefficient.

## What remains

A bounded task evaluates normalized $P(z)=(1+z)^m/2^m$ for $m=1,\ldots,8$, integrates its modulus numerically against exact beta-function values, and checks every root lies on $|z|=1$. Acceptance: the case $m=1$ returns **exactly four**, $m=2$ returns $\pi$, and any statement-capture ambiguity about “real roots” versus unit-circle roots is resolved against the primary theorem.
