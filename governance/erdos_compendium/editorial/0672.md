## The question in plain language

Can the product of four or more **positive integers in an arithmetic progression** be a perfect square, cube or higher integer power, when the initial term and the common difference are coprime?

[Erdős Problem #672](https://www.erdosproblems.com/672) asks whether there exist integers $n,d,k,q,\ell$ with

$$

n\geq1,\quad d\geq1,\quad\gcd(n,d)=1,\quad k\geq4,\quad\ell\geq2,

$$

such that

$$

\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(n+id)=q^\ell.

$$

The maintained catalogue calls this **VERIFIABLE**: one genuine counterexample with all of the above hypotheses would answer yes. In the other direction, proving that none exist for any length $k\geq4$ requires a general theorem, not a finite computation.

## A small example

The bound $k\geq4$ is essential. The **three-term** positive progression $1,25,49$ has common difference $24$ and coprime initial term, yet

$$

1\cdot25\cdot49=1225=35^2.

$$

So replacing $k\geq4$ by $k\geq3$ immediately changes the answer. Another three-term example is $18,25,32$, whose product is $14400=120^2$ and whose first term and difference $7$ are coprime.

Similarly, allowing **negative** terms changes the question. The four integers $-6,-1,4,9$ form an arithmetic progression of difference $5$, and their product is $216=6^3$. This does **not** satisfy the requirement that all progression terms be positive. A correct source audit must preserve both the length threshold and the positivity condition.

## What is known

The classical theorem of Erdős and Selfridge says the product of consecutive positive integers is never a perfect power; that corresponds to common difference $d=1$. Arbitrary positive $d$ with $\gcd(n,d)=1$ is a strictly broader problem.

The primary 2006 paper of [Bennett, Bruin, Győry and Hajdu](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0024611505015625), *Powers from Products of Consecutive Terms in Arithmetic Progression*, *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society* **92**, 273–306, states in its publisher abstract that there are **no** such perfect-power products for $4\leq k\leq11$.

The later [Győry, Hajdu and Pintér article](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0010437X09004114), *Perfect powers from products of consecutive terms in arithmetic progression*, *Compositio Mathematica* **145** (2009), 845–864, extends the excluded range to **$4\leq k\leq34$** in its author abstract. These are substantial published partial negative results, but they do not settle all arbitrarily large $k$. The maintained catalogue reports additional results for sufficiently large $k$ under restrictions on prime divisors of $d$, and for large prime exponents subject to explicit bounds. Those extra hypotheses must not be dropped.

Therefore this snapshot is **not** a complete Lean proof of the parent assertion or even a replayed GCL certificate for those special cases.

Checking the formal translation must cover the meaning of the arithmetic progression, the positive starting term and difference, the coprimality condition, the length of the finite set, the exponent and whether a purported answer asserts existence or nonexistence. A Lean source file's presence does not resolve its theorem's proof obligations.

## What remains

A bounded successor would compare the exact $4\leq k\leq34$ publisher theorem with the pinned formal predicate and verify its hypotheses one by one. A further task could independently formalize one Euler or Obláth special case without a proof hole, but only if the precise source and dependencies are available.

Finite enumerations may discover and certify a **positive** example, if one exists, but failure to find an example cannot establish nonexistence for all integers. Any subsequent mathematical claim must distinguish the published finite range from the unrestricted infinite question.

The source-fidelity audit, accountable critical review, protected production merge, browser MathJax verification and V2 exact-hash editorial admission remain separate requirements.
