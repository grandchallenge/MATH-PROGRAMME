## The question in plain language

Can **every sufficiently large positive integer** be written as a multiple of a **prime square**, plus a remainder smaller than that prime?

[Erdős Problem #676](https://www.erdosproblems.com/676) asks whether all integers beyond some cutoff admit

$$
N=ap^2+b,\qquad p\text{ prime},\qquad a\geq1,\quad0\leq b<p.
$$

This asks for **every** sufficiently large $N$, not just almost all. The maintained catalogue says **OPEN**, although sieve arguments show the desired representation is common.

## A small example

Ten is representable: $10=1\cdot3^2+1$, with $p=3$ and remainder $1<3$. Twenty-four is also representable: $24=6\cdot2^2+0$.

But $N=6$ is not. The only eligible prime is $2$ (because $p^2\leq N$), and the remainder when dividing 6 by $4$ is 2, not strictly less than 2. Similarly, $N=14$ fails for both eligible primes: its remainders modulo $4$ and $9$ are 2 and 5, respectively. These are small finite counterexamples to an assertion about **every** positive integer; they do not refute **eventual** representability.

## What is known

There is an exact and efficient modular reformulation. Given a prime $p$ with $p^2\leq N$, the division algorithm gives

$$
N=\left\lfloor\frac N{p^2}\right\rfloor p^2+(N\bmod p^2).
$$

The coefficient is at least one, and the desired remainder restriction is exactly

$$
N\bmod p^2<p.
$$

Therefore a positive integer $N$ is an exception if and only if

$$
N\bmod p^2\geq p
\quad\text{for every prime }p\leq\sqrt N.
$$

This is the complete finite **witness test**, with no approximations. It also clarifies why a single modulus cannot settle the global existence statement.

An exact elementary enumeration using this criterion finds 86 exceptions among the integers from 1 through 400, beginning

$$
1,2,3,6,7,14,15,22,23,30,31,34,35,\ldots.
$$

These finite data can test code or refute a proposed small cutoff. They cannot show that exceptions continue indefinitely.

**The sieve boundary.** The maintained catalogue attributes to the sieve of Eratosthenes that almost all integers have the representation. It also quotes a Brun–Selberg sieve estimate: the number of exceptions in $[1,X]$ is at most

$$
O\left(\frac X{(\log X)^c}\right)
$$

for some positive constant $c$. Even if rigorously established, that upper bound allows an unbounded number of exceptions. It is **not** a proof that all sufficiently large integers are representable.

The source records Erdős's suspicion that infinitely many exceptions exist. It discusses two different questions: dropping the requirement that $p$ is prime and allowing a larger remainder of size $c_Np$. Results for those variants do not automatically resolve this exact prime-and-remainder statement.

## What remains

An efficient bounded mathematical investigation is to reproduce the exact exception count for a finite range with a verified prime sieve, then rigorously derive a source-backed quantitative upper bound on the number of exceptions. A different, potentially decisive route would construct **infinitely many** integers for which every eligible prime fails the remainder test, proving the eventual-representation claim false.

The hard step is simultaneous control across **all** primes $p\leq\sqrt N$, not the easy modular equivalence for one prime. Finite computation can falsify attempted lemmas but cannot establish infinitude or eventual absence.

The 1979–1980 source passages and original sieve bound need precise theorem/page review. Protected publication, deployed mathematical rendering and a separate V2 admission also remain outstanding.
