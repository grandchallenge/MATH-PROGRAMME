## The question in plain language

Take a **monic** polynomial with $n$ complex roots, counting multiplicities, all in the closed unit disk:

$$
p(z)=\prod_{j=1}^n(z-z_j),\qquad |z_j|\le1.
$$

Look at the planar area of the strict sublevel set $L_p=\{z\in\mathbb C:|p(z)|<1\}$. Erdős, Herzog and Piranian asked how small that area can be. Their first proposed safeguard was a lower bound by an inverse power of $n$; a stronger question asked for an inverse power of $\log n$.

The symbol $|L_p|$ here denotes **Euclidean area**, not curve length. That distinction separates this problem from #114.

## A small example

If every root equals zero, then $p(z)=z^n$. The condition $|z^n|<1$ is equivalent to $|z|<1$, so $L_p$ is the unit disk and has area $\pi$ for all $n$. This gives an example with relatively large area, **not** a minimizing construction.

The zero-location hypothesis matters. The polynomial's roots must all lie inside the unit disk, though $L_p$ itself need not be a disk or connected.

## What is known

**PROVED in its inverse-power and inverse-logarithm forms.** A classical theorem of Pommerenke supplies an inverse polynomial lower bound, historically recorded as $\gg n^{-4}$.

Manjunath Krishnapur, Erik Lundberg and Koushik Ramachandran's [*On the area of polynomial lemniscates*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18270), posted March 2025, proves the sharper universal estimate

$$
\frac{c}{\log n}\le\inf_p |L_p|
\le\frac{C}{\log\log n}
$$

for sufficiently large $n$ and absolute positive constants. The **upper** bound is an example construction, not an upper bound required for every $p$; the **lower** bound holds uniformly over all admissible polynomials.

Thus the first two historical formulations are answered affirmatively. The precise minimizing asymptotic between $1/\log n$ and $1/\log\log n$, and which polynomials attain the exact finite-degree minimum, remain questions beyond those results. The 2025 primary abstract also establishes complementary inradius estimates. GCL did not independently reproduce the analytic proof.

## What remains

A bounded successor is to verify exactly that for $p(z)=z^n$ the strict lemniscate has area $\pi$, and prove that for every fixed exponent $K>0$ the function $1/\log n$ eventually exceeds $n^{-K}$. Acceptance: strict versus non-strict boundary has zero area in this example, all roots satisfy the unit-disk condition, and the implication's quantifier is correctly stated. The universal lower bound remains an imported published theorem.
