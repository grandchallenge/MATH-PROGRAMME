## The question in plain language

Let \(\tau(n)\) count the positive divisors of \(n\). Erdős and Mirsky asked whether there are infinitely many positive integers \(n\) such that
\[
\tau(n)=\tau(n+1).
\]
The answer is **yes**: Heath-Brown proved this in 1984. The original question is therefore solved, even though the precise asymptotic frequency of such \(n\) remains an active and subtler question. [Erdős Problem #946](https://www.erdosproblems.com/946).

## Small exact example

The consecutive integers \(2\) and \(3\) each have two positive divisors, as do \(14=2\cdot7\) and \(15=3\cdot5\), each with four divisors. Thus \(n=2\) and \(n=14\) are explicit solutions. There are infinitely many such pairs, but these examples alone do not establish that.

For any prime factorization \(n=\prod p^{e_p}\), the exact formula
\[
\tau(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}(e_p+1)
\]
provides an inexpensive independent finite verifier. Coprimality of neighbouring integers does not force their divisor counts to differ.

## What is known

Spiro [Sp81] first proved infinitude of solutions to the shifted equation \(\tau(n)=\tau(n+5040)\). Heath-Brown [He84] proved the actual consecutive-integer case, obtaining
\[
\#\{n\le x:\tau(n)=\tau(n+1)\}\gg\frac{x}{(\log x)^7}.
\]
Hildebrand [Hi87] strengthened the lower bound to
\[
\gg \frac{x}{(\log\log x)^3}.
\]
Erdős, Pomerance and Sárközy [EPS87] established
\[
\#\{n\le x:\tau(n)=\tau(n+1)\}\ll\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}.
\]
These two estimates leave a gap. The anticipated principal term is \(c x/\sqrt{\log\log x}\), not a theorem holding for **all** \(x\).

Tao and Teräväinen [TaTe25] established that anticipated asymptotic for almost all \(x\), where the exceptional-set statement uses **logarithmic density** rather than ordinary density. A 2026 catalogue discussion identifies \(c=1/(2\sqrt{\pi})\). These qualifications are necessary: "almost all" is not "every sufficiently large." Pinner [Pi97] also proved infinitely many solutions to \(\tau(n)=\tau(n+k)\) for each fixed positive \(k\). [Catalogue and references](https://www.erdosproblems.com/946).

## Why the stronger question is hard

Although consecutive integers are coprime, their numbers of divisors depend on unrelated factorization patterns. Establishing isolated equalities is easy; controlling the distribution of equalities in every large interval requires uniform correlations of multiplicative functions. Lower bounds of one order of magnitude and upper bounds of another do not determine an asymptotic constant.
