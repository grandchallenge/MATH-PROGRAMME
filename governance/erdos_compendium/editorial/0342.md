## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 342 concerns the classical $(1,2)$ Ulam sequence. Start with $a_1=1$ and $a_2=2$. Once the first $n$ terms are known, choose $a_{n+1}$ to be the smallest integer larger than $a_n$ that has exactly one representation as $a_i+a_j$ with $i<j\leq n$. “Exactly one” counts unordered pairs of distinct earlier terms: the same summands in the opposite order do not count as a second representation.

The page asks four related questions. Do infinitely many pairs of terms differ by $2$? Do the successive differences $a_{n+1}-a_n$ eventually repeat periodically? Does the sequence have density zero among the positive integers? More generally, what can be said about its global structure? The last question is broad; the first three are concrete targets.

This is a greedy definition, but its long-term behaviour is not controlled by the first few terms. A finite computation can show exactly what the rule does for a specified range. It cannot by itself settle an assertion about infinitely many pairs, eventual periodicity, or an asymptotic density.

## A small example

The first four terms are $1,2,3,4$. At the next stage, the unordered sums from these four terms include $1+4=5$ and $2+3=5$, so $5$ is not unique. The value $6$ has the single representation $2+4$ among distinct earlier terms: the other pairs give $3,4,5,5,$ and $7$. Therefore the rule selects $a_5=6$.

Continuing the same exact integer test gives the reproducible prefix

$$
1,2,3,4,6,8,11,13,16,18,26,28,36,38,47.
$$

For an additional check, at the stage with earlier terms $1,2,3,4,6$, the value $8$ has the unique distinct-pair representation $2+6$; candidates between the previous selected value $6$ and $8$ do not pass the uniqueness test. An exact-arithmetic enumeration that counts every pair $i<j$ produces these values and verifies the uniqueness/minimality conditions term by term. This confirms a finite prefix only. It does not estimate a limiting density and does not provide evidence strong enough to infer periodicity.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/342) gives the definition, the same initial values, and the four questions. It currently labels the problem open. It cites Guy’s *Unsolved Problems in Number Theory* as problem C4 and identifies the source location as page 53 of the cited collection.

Ben Green’s [100 Open Problems](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/greenbj/papers/open-problems.pdf) gives a closely related formulation as Problem 7 on PDF page 4 (printed page 5). Green asks whether Ulam’s sequence has positive density and whether its unusual Fourier behaviour can be explained. The comments distinguish the density question from computational observations about a frequency near $2.5714474995$. Green presents these as open questions and points to earlier computations; this is not an external proof of any answer to Problem 342.

The primary paper [“A Hidden Signal in the Ulam sequence”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.00267) reports numerical evidence for that Fourier phenomenon, including a computation on a large finite prefix. Its abstract and introduction explicitly describe the observation as empirical. It is useful evidence about the Fourier subquestion, but it does not establish the density, infinitely-many-pairs, or eventual-periodicity claims. No primary source read in this audit supplies a theorem resolving those claims.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a pinned finite certificate: take the exact external recurrence and the pinned `IsUlamSequence` definition as inputs, generate a specified prefix such as the first 10,000 terms, and record for every selected term its unique pair and the rejection of every smaller candidate. The certificate should also state the one-based/zero-based index translation and carry a content hash. Acceptance means an independent rerun reproduces every term and every uniqueness/minimality check; the first mismatch is a falsification. Stop after the bound is certified or at the first mismatch.

This task would test semantic alignment and provide auditable finite evidence without pretending to settle an asymptotic question. Any later work on density, pair gaps, periodic differences, or Fourier structure needs a separate mathematical argument and source review.
