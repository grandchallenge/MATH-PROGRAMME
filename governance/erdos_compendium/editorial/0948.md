## The question in plain language

Erdős and Galvin ask whether there exist a fixed number of colors $k$ and a function $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ so that, **for every $k$‐coloring of the positive integers**, some increasing sequence $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ satisfies both:

1. $a_n<f(n)$ for infinitely many $n$;
2. the set of **all nonempty finite sums of distinct terms**,

$$
\mathrm{FS}(a_i)=\left\{\sum_{i\in F}a_i:\varnothing\ne F\subset\mathbb N\text{ finite}\right\},
$$

omits at least one of the $k$ colors.

The full question is **open**. The requirement is not that the finite-sums set be monochromatic, and the growth restriction holds infinitely often, not necessarily for every index. [Erdős Problem #948](https://www.erdosproblems.com/948).

## A small example

*Elementary examples and quantifier checks*

For an increasing sequence $a_i=2^{i-1}$, the finite sums contain **every positive integer**, by binary expansion. Thus this sequence fails the color-omission requirement for any coloring using all $k$ colors. This demonstrates why merely finding a fast-growing sequence is insufficient.

For $k=2$, omitting a color is exactly the same as being monochromatic. Galvin supplied a two-color counterexample for any prescribed bound $f$, so the proposed uniform growth property is impossible with two colors. For $k\ge3$, omitting at least one color allows the finite-sums set to use several other colors; this distinction is the substance of the open problem.

A coloring means a map $c:\mathbb N\to\{1,\ldots,k\}$. A finite-sums witness omits a color $j$ if

$$
\forall F\subset\mathbb N\ \text{finite nonempty},\qquad c\left(\sum_{i\in F}a_i\right)\ne j.
$$

A finite experiment checking sums from the first $N$ terms cannot prove this universal quantifier over all finite subsets of an infinite sequence.

## What is known

*Established partial result*

Erdős and Galvin [ErGa91] proved a weaker but striking theorem: in any finite $k$‐coloring, an increasing sequence can be chosen with

$$
a_n<2^{\,2^{O(n)}}
$$

at every index such that **sums over finite intervals of indices** use at most two colors. The interval sums are

$$
\left\{\sum_{i=u}^{v}a_i:1\le u\le v<\infty\right\}.
$$

This is a smaller family than all finite sums, since arbitrary finite subsets need not be consecutive. Controlling interval sums does **not** prove the open claim for all finite sums.

The original monochromatic version was refuted by Galvin using a coloring based on the 2-adic valuation and odd part of each integer. The maintained catalogue reports that the general question remains open even when the color palette is countably infinite. The possibility $k=3$ was explicitly unresolved in [ErGa91]. These are source-attributed claims, not original GCL results. [Catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/948).

## Why the problem is difficult

Hindman's theorem guarantees an infinite sequence with monochromatic finite sums in every finite coloring, but supplies no universally prescribed rate of growth for the sequence. A sparse monochromatic sequence may grow arbitrarily rapidly to accommodate an adversarial coloring. Allowing two or more colors among the finite sums relaxes the target; the question is whether that relaxation buys a single growth envelope met infinitely often.

The order of the quantifiers is crucial: the function $f$ and number $k$ are chosen *before* the adversarial coloring. Choosing $f$ after seeing the coloring would ask a much weaker question.

## GCL's current state

The binary example and the distinction between interval sums and arbitrary finite sums are elementary and verified by definitions. The historical two-color obstruction and the interval-sum theorem are literature claims from Erdős–Galvin; their complete proofs have not been independently reconstructed here. Comments on the site include unadjudicated proposed disproofs and formalization claims, which must not be promoted to certified resolution without theorem-equivalence and proof replay.
