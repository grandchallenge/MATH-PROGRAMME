## The question in plain language

For an integer $N\geq 1$, let $k(N)$ be the largest number of pairwise disjoint subsets $A_1,\ldots,A_{k(N)}$ of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ for which every reciprocal sum is exactly $1$:

$$
\sum_{n\in A_i}\frac{1}{n}=1.
$$

“Pairwise disjoint” means that no integer from $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ may be used in two different sets. The original question asks how large $k(N)$ can be and, specifically, whether $k(N)=o(\log N)$. The latter would mean that $k(N)/\log N$ tends to $0$ as $N$ grows.

The resolved asymptotic is instead

$$
k(N)=(1+o(1))\log N.
$$

Equivalently, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently large $N$ satisfy

$$
(1-\varepsilon)\log N\leq k(N)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\log N.
$$

Thus Problem 296 is proved in the mathematical source record, and the proposed $o(\log N)$ answer is false. The lower bound comes from extracting many disjoint unit-fraction sets; the upper bound is simply the total reciprocal mass available in $\{1,\ldots,N\}$.

## A small example

At $N=6$, two disjoint qualifying sets are

$$
A_1=\{1\},\qquad A_2=\{2,3,6\},
$$

because

$$
\frac{1}{1}=1,\qquad \frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{3+2+1}{6}=1.
$$

There cannot be three. If three disjoint sets each had reciprocal sum $1$, their combined reciprocal mass would be $3$. But exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
H_6=1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac16=\frac{49}{20}<3.
$$

Therefore $k(6)=2$. This finite calculation does not prove the asymptotic theorem, but it makes the disjointness upper bound concrete. The exact reproducible check recorded in the authenticated audit is:

```python
from fractions import Fraction

H6 = sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in range(1, 7)), Fraction(0))
assert H6 == Fraction(49, 20)
assert Fraction(1, 1) == 1
assert sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in (2, 3, 6)), Fraction(0)) == 1
assert H6 < 3
```

The calculation and the conclusion $k(6)=2$ are also stated in the exact [RESULT/1 audit comment](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1566#issuecomment-6137973854), which is the reproducibility reference for this fixture.

## What is known

The upper estimate is immediate. Every qualifying set consumes reciprocal mass $1$, and disjoint sets consume different terms, so

$$
k(N)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1n=H_N=\log N+\gamma+o(1).
$$

In particular, $k(N)\leq(1+o(1))\log N$.

The lower estimate is the substantive part. Bloom’s Theorem 3 states that there is an absolute constant $C>0$ such that if $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ satisfies

$$
\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\geq C\frac{\log\log\log N}{\log\log N}\log N,
$$

then $A$ contains a subset $S$ with reciprocal sum $1$. This is a quantitative extraction theorem, not merely an assertion that one unit-fraction set exists.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Start with $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ and repeatedly remove a unit-sum subset while Bloom’s threshold is still met. After $m=\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)\log N\rfloor$ removals, the remaining reciprocal mass is

$$
H_N-m=\varepsilon\log N+O(1).
$$

For sufficiently large $N$, this dominates Bloom’s threshold because $\log\log\log N/\log\log N$ tends to $0$. Hence at least $m$ pairwise disjoint sets can be removed. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, the lower bound is $k(N)\geq(1-o(1))\log N$, and the two estimates combine to give the displayed asymptotic.

I inspected Bloom, “On a density conjecture about unit fractions,” arXiv:2112.03726v2: Theorem 3 is on PDF p. 2 and its deduction is in §2.1, pp. 6–7. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/296) gives the same statement and attributes the greedy consequence to Hunter and Sawhney. The original Erdős–Graham monograph is the historical citation, but its relevant page was not obtained in this audit; no page-specific claim from that unavailable source is presented as directly inspected.
