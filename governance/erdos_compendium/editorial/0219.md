## The question in plain language

Are there **arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions made entirely of prime numbers**? For every positive integer $k$, the question asks whether some prime $p$ and positive integer step $d$ satisfy

$$
p,\ p+d,\ldots,p+(k-1)d
$$

with all $k$ terms prime. The primes forming such a progression need **not** be consecutive in the ordered list of all primes.

## A small example

The primes $5,11,17,23$ form a four-term arithmetic progression with common difference six. The primes $3,5,7$ form a three-term progression and are also consecutive primes, but consecutive-prime progressions are a much stronger requirement.

If a progression has more terms than a small prime modulus $q$, then either its common difference is divisible by $q$, or one of its terms is divisible by $q$. For sufficiently large terms, the latter is impossible unless that term equals $q$. This elementary modular obstruction explains why steps in long prime progressions have many small prime divisors. It does **not** make long progressions impossible.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Ben Green and Terence Tao, [*The primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions*](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2008.167.481), *Annals of Mathematics* 167 (2008), 481–547, established exactly the requested statement. Their argument combines a transference principle for pseudorandom majorants with Szemerédi-type density methods adapted to the primes.

The theorem is existential for every fixed length $k$. It does not guarantee that those primes are consecutive, does not supply the optimal smallest terminal prime for each $k$, and does not establish any particular rate for the longest progression below $N$.

The stronger problem of **arbitrarily long progressions of consecutive primes**, stated in #141 and related to #218, remains open. The maintained [Erdős #219](https://www.erdosproblems.com/219) explicitly separates these formulations.

## What remains

A bounded successor enumerates primes to 10,000 and finds maximal example progressions of lengths three through five. Acceptance: every proposed term passes an exact primality test and all successive differences are identical; whether terms are consecutive is recorded separately. Stop at finite examples, not an independent proof of arbitrary lengths.
