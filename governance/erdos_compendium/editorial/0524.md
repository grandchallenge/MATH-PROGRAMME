The maintained catalogue still labels it **OPEN**, but an April 2026 preprint by **Brayden Letwin and Mehtaab Sawhney** claims an exact lower-envelope theorem. ## The question in plain language

Choose one infinite sequence $\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_1,\ldots$ of independent fair signs, each equal to $-1$ or $+1$. Form its nested Littlewood polynomials

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon_kx^k
$$

and take the maximum on the **real** interval,

$$
M_n=\max_{-1\le x\le1}|P_n(x)|.
$$

What is the correct order of $M_n$, for almost every infinite sign sequence? The original Erdős statement encodes the signs by the binary digits of a uniformly chosen $t\in(0,1)$, with $\varepsilon_k=(-1)^{\text{digit}_k}$. Except for the countable dyadic-expansion ambiguity, this is precisely the iid sign model.

There are really two aspects to the order-of-magnitude question. How **large** is $M_n$ along its recurrent peaks? How **small** can it be along arbitrarily large degrees for the **same** coefficient sequence? The answers have different scales.

One must not confuse this norm with the **complex unit-circle maximum** from Erdős #523. On the circle, the maximum typically has order $\sqrt{n\log n}$. On the real interval, its almost-sure upper envelope is only of order $\sqrt{n\log\log n}$, and the lower envelope is even smaller than $\sqrt n$ by a subpolynomial factor.

## A small example

For the all-positive signs, $P_n(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^n$. The maximum on $[-1,1]$ is $n+1$ at $x=1$. This is an exceptional deterministic sign sequence, not a contradiction of an almost-sure theorem.

To understand why the interval maximum is related to random walks, define two partial-sum sequences,

$$
S_j=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\varepsilon_k,
\qquad
T_j=\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^k\varepsilon_k.
$$

For $0\le x\le1$, **summation by parts** gives the exact polynomial identity

$$
\boxed{
P_n(x)=(1-x)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}S_jx^j+S_nx^n.
}
$$

The coefficients $(1-x)x^j$ and $x^n$ are nonnegative and sum to one. Consequently, $P_n(x)$ is a convex combination of the partial sums $S_0,\ldots,S_n$. For negative $x=-u$, the same argument applies to the alternating partial sums $T_j$.

Thus we obtain the deterministic **two-walk sandwich**

$$
\boxed{
\max\{|S_n|,|T_n|\}
\le M_n
\le\max_{0\le j\le n}\{|S_j|,|T_j|\}.
}
$$

This is a useful finite result. It explains the relationship to random-walk maxima without invoking the advanced Gaussian-process machinery.

It is nevertheless far weaker than the 2026 lower-envelope theorem. In particular, bounding $M_n$ above by random-walk maxima does not determine the probability that the entire polynomial remains unusually small over **every** point of the interval.

## What is known

### Erdős and the earlier upper and lower bounds

Erdős, *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.253, Section V.3](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=33), asks for the precise lower envelope of the interval maximum. The original page was inspected. He states an unpublished result asserting that, for every $\delta>0$, $M_n/n^{1/2-\delta}\to\infty$ almost surely, while a theorem of **Chung** implies that infinitely many $n$ satisfy

$$
M_n\ll\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log\log n}}.
$$

The original source of this problem is Salem and Zygmund, *Some properties of trigonometric series whose terms have random signs*, *Acta Mathematica* **91** (1954), 245–301. The 2026 authors identify their Theorem (6.1.1) as giving the complementary exact upper envelope,

$$
\boxed{\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\frac{M_n}{\sqrt{n\log\log n}}=\sqrt2
\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

This limit superior permits infinitely many degrees with much smaller maxima. It is not an ordinary asymptotic formula $M_n\sim\sqrt{2n\log\log n}$ for **every** large $n$.

### Letwin and Sawhney's April 2026 lower envelope

Brayden Letwin and Mehtaab Sawhney, *On the maxima of Littlewood polynomials on* $[-1,1]$, [arXiv:2604.19294v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19294), April 21, 2026, present a twenty-nine-page proof of

$$
\boxed{
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\frac{\log(M_n/\sqrt n)}{(\log\log n)^{1/3}}
=-\left(\frac{3\pi^2}{4}\right)^{1/3}
\quad\text{almost surely}.
}
$$

The constant is **$(3\pi^2/4)^{1/3}$**, not $3\pi/4^{1/3}$. Their original paper's abstract and Theorems 1.1–1.2 on its first two pages were visually inspected; the source's longer explanation was also reviewed.

This result says that the unusually low recurrent maxima have logarithmic scale

$$
M_n\approx
\sqrt n\,
\exp\left(
-\left(\frac{3\pi^2}{4}\right)^{1/3}
(\log\log n)^{1/3}
\right),
$$

where this expression is an interpretation of the **limit inferior** on a logarithmic scale. It is not a $1+o(1)$ formula for every sufficiently large $n$.

In fact, their Theorem 1.1 gives a finer implicit normalization using a Brownian-motion process. Let $B_s$ be standard Brownian motion and define

$$
Y_t=\int_0^1e^{-st}\,dB_s,\qquad
F(\eta)=\mathbb P\left(\sup_{t\ge0}|Y_t|\le\eta\right).
$$

The theorem states, almost surely,

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\frac{M_n}{\sqrt n\,F^{-1}((\log n)^{-1/2})}=1.
$$

Their Theorem 1.2 calculates the leading **small-ball** asymptotic

$$
\boxed{\log F(\eta)\sim
-\frac{2}{3\pi^2}\log^3\frac1\eta
\quad(\eta\downarrow0).}
$$

If $F(\eta)=(\log n)^{-1/2}$, the leading terms give
$\log^3(1/\eta)\sim(3\pi^2/4)\log\log n$.
This recovers exactly the constant in the theorem's logarithmic lower envelope.

The authors' proof uses the Komlós–Major–Tusnády coupling, two related Gaussian processes, spectral estimates for weighted $L^2$ small-ball probabilities, Gaussian correlation, and a sparse-scale argument. A proof of the deterministic two-walk bound above is **not a replacement** for any of those steps.

The paper openly credits ChatGPT Pro for drawing attention to a Gaussian $B$‑inequality and notes Codex assistance with writing. The mathematical authorship and priority of the 2026 result remain Letwin and Sawhney; this editorial audit asserts no new result.
