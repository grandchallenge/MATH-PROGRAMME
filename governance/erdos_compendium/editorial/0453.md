## The question in plain language

Prime numbers do not arrive at regular intervals. Erdős and Straus asked whether the primes on both sides of a sufficiently late prime must sometimes be far enough apart, in a *multiplicative* sense, to outweigh the square of the middle prime.

Write $p_n$ for the $n$th prime, starting with $p_1=2$. Their proposed property was that there is an index $N$ such that, for **every** $n\ge N$, one can find an integer $i$ with $1\le i<n$ and

$$
p_n^2<p_{n-i}p_{n+i}.
$$

The two neighboring primes here need not be immediately adjacent to $p_n$. We can move $i$ positions in both directions, but we must use the **same** distance in the list of primes. The condition $i<n$ keeps the left-hand index positive.

Taking logarithms reveals the geometric meaning:

$$
2\log p_n<\log p_{n-i}+\log p_{n+i}.
$$

The conjecture therefore asked whether every sufficiently late point of the sequence $\log p_n$ lies **strictly below** at least one symmetric average of its neighbors. In 1979, Carl Pomerance showed that this is **false**. There are infinitely many indices where the middle logarithm is instead strictly **above every** such average.

This is not a statement that all primes behave one way. It is a statement about an infinite collection of exceptional indices, which is enough to refute a claim about *every sufficiently large* index.

## A small example

The third prime is $p_3=5$. There are exactly two allowable offsets, $i=1$ and $i=2$. Both give the opposite of the conjectured inequality:

$$
5^2=25>3\cdot7=21,\qquad 5^2=25>2\cdot11=22.
$$

The fifth prime, $p_5=11$, is another example. For $i=1,2,3,4$, the symmetric products are respectively

$$
7\cdot13=91,\quad 5\cdot17=85,\quad 3\cdot19=57,\quad 2\cdot23=46,
$$

all strictly smaller than $11^2=121$. But the second prime behaves differently: $p_2^2=9<2\cdot5=10$, so $i=1$ **does** witness the proposed inequality at $n=2$.

These calculations can be reproduced with integer multiplication; no floating-point logarithms are needed. They are tests of the indexing and inequality direction, not a proof that the exceptional indices continue forever. An eventual claim allows finitely many exceptions. The theorem needs **infinitely many**.

## What is known

The original Erdős–Graham collection (1980, printed p. 90) already reported that Pomerance had disproved the Erdős–Straus expectation. The resolving publication is Pomerance's *The Prime Number Graph* (1979), printed pp. 399–400. Its equation (1.1) states the strict multiplicative inequality and its Theorem 2.2 supplies a general geometric reason why such exceptions occur infinitely often.

Here is the precise reusable theorem, in the notation of that paper. Let

$$
0<a_1<a_2<\cdots,\qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{n}=0.
$$

Then **infinitely many** indices $n$ have the simultaneous property

$$
2a_n>a_{n-i}+a_{n+i}\quad\text{for every integer }1\le i<n.
$$

Pomerance's argument considers the convex hull of the points $(n,a_n)$ in the plane. Because the sequence is increasing but grows more slowly than linearly, the nonhorizontal boundary on the upper side is concave and has infinitely many vertices. At each of the relevant vertices, the point is strictly above the midpoint of any symmetric pair of other points. This geometric statement is the difficult step; merely drawing a finite picture would not establish infinitely many strict vertices.

Apply the theorem to $a_n=\log p_n$. The logarithms are positive and strictly increasing. The growth condition is mild: elementary estimates for the $n$th prime give $p_n\le Cn\log n$ for sufficiently large $n$, so

$$
0\le\frac{\log p_n}{n}\le\frac{\log C+\log n+\log\log n}{n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Consequently there are infinitely many $n$ for which

$$
2\log p_n>\log p_{n-i}+\log p_{n+i}
$$

for **all** $1\le i<n$. Since exponentiation is strictly increasing, this becomes

$$
p_n^2>p_{n-i}p_{n+i}\qquad(1\le i<n).
$$

That is stronger than merely saying there is no offset with $p_n^2<p_{n-i}p_{n+i}$. Equality is excluded as well. The quantifier order is crucial: Pomerance constructs infinitely many $n$, each of which defeats **every** allowable offset. Thus no finite threshold $N$ can make the original proposed property hold for every $n\ge N$.

One related question must remain separate. On the same 1980 page, Erdős and Graham suggest that the indices satisfying the reverse inequality might have natural density zero. Infinitude alone says nothing about whether their proportion among $1,\ldots,N$ tends to zero. Pomerance's theorem does **not** settle that density assertion.

## What remains

The historical Erdős–Straus conjecture is settled in the negative. Remaining work concerns **verification and explanation**, not a fresh attempt to solve it. The most useful bounded formal task is to replay Pomerance's strict midpoint-maxima lemma for increasing sublinear sequences under a pinned Lean/mathlib environment, specialize it to the logarithms of primes, and establish the precise equivalence between the paper's one-based indices and the provider's zero-based `Nat.nth Nat.Prime` indices.
