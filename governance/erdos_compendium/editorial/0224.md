## The question in plain language

Erdős asked whether every set of **$2^d+1$ distinct points in $\mathbb R^d$** contains three of its points forming a **strictly obtuse angle**. At a vertex $y$, the angle between vectors $x-y$ and $z-y$ is obtuse precisely when

$$
(x-y)\cdot(z-y)<0.
$$

Strict inequality matters: right angles with dot product zero are **not** obtuse. Equivalently, a set of points in which all angles are at most $90$ degrees has at most $2^d$ members.

## A small example

In the plane, the four vertices of a square form only acute or right angles: no triple determines a strictly obtuse angle. Adding a fifth distinct planar point necessarily creates one. Thus the threshold $2^2+1=5$ is sharp.

In $\mathbb R^d$, the $2^d$ vertices of the unit cube $\{0,1\}^d$ also have no strictly obtuse angle. Indeed, in each coordinate the two differences from a fixed cube vertex point in the **same direction or one is zero**, so each term of their dot product is nonnegative. Summing gives a nonnegative dot product, including possible right angles.

## What is known

**PROVED**, with a sharp cardinality. Ludwig Danzer and Branko Grünbaum, [*Über zwei Probleme bezüglich konvexer Körper von P. Erdős und von V. L. Klee*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/224), 1962, proved that a set of points in $\mathbb R^d$ with **no obtuse angle** has at most $2^d$ members. The hypercube construction attains the bound.

This is related to but distinct from problems that forbid **right or obtuse** angles, and from Danzer–Grünbaum's antipodal-set theorem. Changing the condition from $<0$ to $\le0$ excludes right angles and changes the extremal examples.

The maintained [Erdős #224](https://www.erdosproblems.com/224) also attributes an unpublished dimension-three argument to Kuiper and Boerdijk; the Danzer–Grünbaum theorem handles arbitrary finite dimension.

## What remains

A bounded task enumerates the cube vertices for $d=1,2,3,4$ and checks every ordered choice of an angle vertex and two other vertices using exact integer dot products. Acceptance: all such dot products are nonnegative, each cube has exactly $2^d$ distinct points, and the difference between zero and strictly negative dot products is explicit.
