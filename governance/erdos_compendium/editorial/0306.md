## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #306 asks whether every positive rational number can be written as a finite sum of distinct unit fractions whose denominators are semiprimes. In the normalized formulation, write $q=a/b$ in lowest terms, require $b$ to be squarefree, and ask whether there are integers

$$
1<n_1<\cdots<n_k
$$

such that each $n_i$ is the product of two distinct primes and

$$
q=\frac{1}{n_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}.
$$

“Distinct primes” excludes squares such as $9$; the allowed denominators include $6=2\cdot3$, $10=2\cdot5$, and $15=3\cdot5$. The squarefree condition belongs to the reduced denominator of $q$, not to an arbitrary unreduced way of writing the same rational. The question is therefore an existence statement with several important quantifiers: for every eligible $q$, there must exist some finite length $k$ and some increasing list of eligible denominators.

The restriction is on the denominators, not on the number of prime factors appearing across the whole list: different terms may reuse a prime, as the example below does. Also, the problem asks for a finite sum, so an argument based only on an infinite convergent series would not be enough. These distinctions explain why a verified example for $q=1$ is informative without being a solution of the universal question. They also give a practical checklist for reading a claimed proof: identify the exact domain of $q$, check whether the denominator is canonical and squarefree, and check that every selected denominator is a product of two different primes.

## A small example

The primary source [Watanabe, arXiv:2009.03275v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03275) gives the following 47-term example for $q=1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1={}&\frac1{6}+\frac1{10}+\frac1{14}+\frac1{15}+\frac1{21}+\frac1{22}+\frac1{26}+\frac1{33}+\frac1{34}+\frac1{35}+\frac1{38}+\frac1{39}\\
&+\frac1{46}+\frac1{51}+\frac1{55}+\frac1{57}+\frac1{58}+\frac1{62}+\frac1{65}+\frac1{69}+\frac1{74}+\frac1{82}+\frac1{85}+\frac1{86}\\
&+\frac1{87}+\frac1{91}+\frac1{93}+\frac1{95}+\frac1{106}+\frac1{111}+\frac1{123}+\frac1{133}+\frac1{145}+\frac1{155}+\frac1{159}\\
&+\frac1{185}+\frac1{203}+\frac1{215}+\frac1{253}+\frac1{265}+\frac1{287}+\frac1{319}+\frac1{493}+\frac1{583}+\frac1{731}+\frac1{851}+\frac1{1073}.
\end{aligned}
$$

An exact-arithmetic check using rational fractions returned `count = 47`, `strictly_increasing = True`, `all_distinct_prime_products = True`, and `sum = 1`; it also found no denominator failing the two-distinct-prime test. For example, $159=3\cdot53$ and $1073=29\cdot37$. This verifies one finite representation and makes no claim about arbitrary $a/b$, about a minimum number of terms, or about the truth of the general problem. Watanabe reports 17 such 47-term examples and says that no shorter example had been found; that is a computational report, not a proof that 47 is minimal.

The calculation is reproducible without floating-point rounding: construct an exact rational `1/n` for each listed integer, add the 47 fractions, sort the denominators, remove duplicates, and factor each integer. The resulting equality is exact, rather than a decimal approximation. That distinction matters for Egyptian fractions, where a tiny numerical residual could conceal an incorrect identity. It still checks only the certificate printed in the paper; it does not validate the paper’s search procedure or prove that no other, shorter certificate exists.

## What is known

The source page [Erdős Problems #306](https://www.erdosproblems.com/306) gives the same general question. It records that when denominators are products of three distinct primes, the integer case $b=1$ was proved by Butler, Erdős, and Graham in “Egyptian fractions with each denominator having three distinct prime divisors,” *Integers* 15 (2015), A51; the authors’ [primary paper](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/pre_tres_egyptian.pdf) states that every natural number has such a representation. That theorem changes “two” to “three” and does not settle #306.

For the semiprime case, Watanabe’s 2020 paper is primary evidence for explicit decompositions of $1$, not a proof for every squarefree-denominator rational. The maintained page was last edited on 21 June 2026 and now displays one proof claim linking to 2026 arXiv work. The page itself warns that a proof claim is not a guarantee of correctness. The claim and the associated preprints are therefore recorded here as unverified external reports, not as accepted coverage.

## What remains

The immediate missing work is a source-semantic review of the recent proof claims: compare their exact hypotheses, quantifiers, denominator distinctness conditions, and dependency assumptions with the normalized statement above. A report that proves all integers, or rationals above a threshold, is materially weaker than the registered all-positive-rationals statement. A proof of the three-prime analogue is a different theorem even when its method is relevant.

A bounded successor task can independently reproduce Watanabe’s displayed list with exact rational arithmetic, factor every denominator, and compare the resulting finite certificate with the formal file’s indexing convention. Acceptance would require exact sum $1$, 47 distinct increasing denominators, and two different prime factors for every denominator. Falsification is immediate at the first bad factor, duplicate, ordering failure, or nonzero residual. This finite check is useful evidence and a regression test, but it cannot establish the infinite quantified statement.
