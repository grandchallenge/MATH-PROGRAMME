## The question in plain language

Let

$$
f(n)=\sum_{\substack{p<n\\p\ {\rm prime}}}\frac1{n-p}.
$$

Erdős asked three pointwise questions:

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(n)=1,\qquad
\limsup_{n\to\infty}f(n)=+\infty,
$$

and whether $f(n)=o(\log\log n)$ as $n\to\infty$. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/950) records all these questions as **open**. The last is a uniform pointwise little-o assertion, not a statement about averages or almost every integer.

## A small example

*Small calculations and a structural identity*

For $n=6$, the primes below six are $2,3,5$, and

$$
f(6)=\frac14+\frac13+1=\frac{19}{12}.
$$

For $n=7$, they are $2,3,5$, giving

$$
f(7)=\frac15+\frac14+\frac12=\frac{19}{20}.
$$

Thus even the finite values fluctuate on both sides of one.

There is a useful exact reindexing:

$$
f(n)=\sum_{h=1}^{n-2}\frac{\mathbf1_{\mathbb P}(n-h)}{h}.
$$

The terms at small $h$ measure how many primes lie immediately below $n$, and the terms at large $h$ sample a much wider portion of the primes with smaller weights. Both local irregularity and global prime distribution matter.

If $p<p'$ are consecutive primes, then for integers $p+1\le n<p'$, each individual term $1/(n-q)$ for primes $q\le p$ decreases as $n$ increases, and no new prime term appears. Hence

$$
f(p+1)>f(p+2)>\cdots>f(p')
$$

when the interval has at least two members. This reduces the study of local maxima and minima to specified points adjacent to consecutive primes, but does not solve the limiting questions.

## What is known

De Bruijn, Erdős and Turán established the mean and second-moment asymptotics

$$
\sum_{n<x}f(n)\sim x,\qquad
\sum_{n<x}f(n)^2\sim x.
$$

These are global averaged claims. They neither imply the proposed exact liminf nor rule out occasional very large spikes. The catalogue cites a subsequent proof of the more difficult second-moment statement by Gorodetsky; source and hypotheses should be pinned before GCL certification.

The maintained source also notes that certain strong assumptions on the abundance of primes in short intervals would imply $\liminf f(n)>0$; this is weaker than the proposed equality to one. A sufficiently uniform prime-counting estimate in intervals of lengths beyond polylogarithmic scales would imply $f(n)\ll\log\log\log n$, but the catalogue presents that as a **conditional implication**, not an unconditional proof.

The behavior of $f(p)$ along prime arguments is harder still; Erdős did not prove the analogue $\sum_{p<x}f(p)^2\sim\pi(x)$. The source discussion cautions that reconstructing the full historical second-moment proof is nontrivial.

## Why the question is hard

A very short cluster of primes just below $n$ can produce large harmonic contributions, but the frequency and strength of such clusters are constrained by subtle prime-gap results. Averaged control allows sparse exceptional values and thus does not determine a limsup or uniform little-o bound.

## GCL's current state

The formulas, small values, and between-prime monotonicity are elementary. The historical moment asymptotics are attributed literature, not independently re-proved here. The three stated pointwise questions remain open. No finite numerical cutoff proves any limiting assertion.
