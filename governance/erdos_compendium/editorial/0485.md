## The question in plain language

Polynomials sometimes have very few nonzero coefficients, even when their degree is enormous. Such a polynomial is called *sparse* or *lacunary*. For example,

$$
P(x)=2+5x^{100}-x^{10\,000}
$$

has **three terms**, irrespective of the huge gaps between its exponents.

When two polynomials are multiplied, different products of terms can contribute to the same power of $x$. Their coefficients may cancel. This makes an apparently elementary question surprisingly difficult: can a polynomial with arbitrarily many nonzero terms have a square with only a fixed number of nonzero terms?

Let $\tau(P)$ denote the number of nonzero coefficients of a polynomial $P$, and define

$$
f(k)=\min_{\substack{P\in\mathbb Q[x]\\\tau(P)=k}}\tau(P^2).
$$

The minimization is over **all rational polynomials with exactly $k$ terms**, with no bound on their degrees, gaps between exponents, or coefficient sizes.

Erdős and Rényi conjectured that

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}f(k)=+\infty.}
$$

In plain language, a square cannot stay uniformly sparse while the original rational polynomial becomes arbitrarily dense in its *number of terms*.

The answer is **yes**. Andrzej Schinzel proved the conjecture in 1987, and in 2009 Schinzel and Umberto Zannier gave a stronger quantitative lower bound:

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
f(k)\ge 2+\frac{\log(k-1)}{\log8}\qquad(k\ge2).}
$$

The logarithm grows slowly, but it increases without bound. That is all that is needed to settle the original question.

## A small example

Cancellations are real, so simply counting all formal products gives a misleading answer.

Consider

$$
P(x)=1+x-\frac{x^2}{2}.
$$

The polynomial has three nonzero terms, but direct multiplication gives

$$
P(x)^2=1+2x-x^3+\frac{x^4}{4}.
$$

The coefficient of $x^2$ vanishes because

$$
1+2\left(-\frac12\right)=0.
$$

Thus this three-term polynomial produces only **four** terms in its square, rather than the five that a generic quadratic square has. In particular,

$$
f(3)\le4.
$$

For comparison, set

$$
G_k(x)=1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{k-1}.
$$

This polynomial has $k$ terms. All coefficients of $G_k(x)^2$ are positive, and its exponents cover every integer from $0$ to $2k-2$, so

$$
\tau(G_k^2)=2k-1.
$$

Consequently $f(k)\le2k-1$. This is an **upper bound** obtained by constructing one polynomial; it cannot prove the conjectured lower bound.

For a two-term polynomial

$$
P(x)=ax^r+bx^s,\qquad r<s,\quad ab\ne0,
$$

the square has exactly three terms in characteristic zero, because the exponents $2r,r+s,2s$ are distinct and all three coefficients $a^2,2ab,b^2$ are nonzero. Hence

$$
f(1)=1,\qquad f(2)=3.
$$

These elementary examples clarify what the conjecture does and does not assert. It is about the **minimum number of surviving terms after all cancellations**, not the number of formal pairwise products.

## What is known

### Erdős and Rényi's conjecture

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed page 234, Problem I.23, Paul Erdős defines the minimum number of terms in the square of a $k$‑term polynomial and attributes the conjecture $f(k)\to\infty$ to himself and Alfréd Rényi.

The problem had been investigated by Rényi and László Rédei in 1947 and by Erdős in 1949. Erdős reports an earlier construction showing

$$
f(k)<k^{1-c}
$$

for some positive constant $c$. There is no contradiction: it is possible for $f(k)$ to grow without bound while growing much more slowly than a positive power of $k$.

The original challenge was a **lower** bound that applies uniformly to *every* polynomial, irrespective of how its coefficients are chosen to cancel terms.

### Schinzel's 1987 proof

Andrzej Schinzel, *On the number of terms of a power of a polynomial*, *Acta Arithmetica* **49** (1987), 55–70, proved the qualitative conjecture and extended the study from squares to higher powers.

In the language used by the subsequent 2009 paper, for a fixed exponent $\ell$ and characteristic-zero coefficients, the 1987 work implies lower bounds of order

$$
\tau(P^\ell)\gg_\ell \log\log\tau(P).
$$

This tends to infinity when $\tau(P)\to\infty$, proving the original square conjecture on setting $\ell=2$.

The 1987 archive's original scanned article was retrieved. Its proof is image-based, however, and has **not** been independently checked page by page in this audit. The result and attribution are also explicitly described in the 2009 published primary paper.

### The stronger 2009 theorem

Schinzel and Zannier, *On the number of terms of a power of a polynomial*, *Rendiconti Lincei Matematica e Applicazioni* **20** (2009), 95–98, Theorem 1, prove the following precise inequality.

Let $F$ be a field, $P\in F[x]$ have $T\ge2$ nonzero terms, and let $\ell\ge1$ be an integer. Write $t=\tau(P^\ell)$. If

$$
\mathrm{char}F=0
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathrm{char}F>\ell\deg P,
$$

then

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
t\ge2+\frac{\log(T-1)}{\log(4\ell)}.}
$$

For the original problem, take $F=\mathbb Q$, $\ell=2$, $T=k$. Since $\mathbb Q$ has characteristic zero, the theorem applies without any restriction on the polynomial's degree, yielding

$$
f(k)\ge2+\frac{\log(k-1)}{\log8}.
$$

The right side tends to infinity. This provides a complete implication from the published theorem to the conjecture.

The denominator is **$\log(4\ell)$**, not $\ell\log4$, and the numerator contains **$T-1$**, not $T$. These details matter when checking statements against the primary paper and any machine formalization.

The published proof, on printed pages 96–98, is not merely a numerical estimate. It assumes a counterexample of **minimal polynomial degree**, uses Dirichlet's approximation of exponent ratios to build a suitable two-variable polynomial, and constructs a lower-degree univariate counterexample, contradicting minimality.

Two technical lemmas are imported from Schinzel's 1987 paper, one concerning powers in subrings $F[x^d]$ and one controlling support under a bivariate transformation. The article states those lemmas explicitly, but does not reproduce their original complete proofs. Source-level verification of the 2009 argument is therefore not the same as an independent reconstruction of all of its dependencies.

The characteristic hypothesis is important. The theorem is not claimed here for **every field of arbitrary positive characteristic and every exponent**; in positive characteristic, Frobenius identities alter how polynomial powers behave.

### Why polynomial degree does not resolve the problem

Suppose $Q(x)=cx^rP(x^d)$ with $c\ne0$, $r\ge0$ and $d\ge1$. Distinct exponents of $P$ remain distinct when multiplied by $d$ and shifted by $r$, so

$$
\tau(Q)=\tau(P),\qquad
\tau(Q^2)=\tau(P^2).
$$

The degree of $Q$ can be arbitrarily large without changing either count.

This is why checking polynomials only up to some degree can be useful for finding finite examples, but cannot establish the conjecture over all rational polynomials. The proof must control possible cancellations at arbitrary exponent gaps.
