## The question in plain language

For sets of positive integers $A$ and $B$, write

$$
A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\}.
$$

When the limit exists, their natural density is

$$
d(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|}{N}.
$$

Erdős Problem 335 asks for a characterization of all pairs with $d(A)>0$, $d(B)>0$, and

$$
d(A+B)=d(A)+d(B).
$$

The equality says that adding the two sets creates exactly the amount of large-scale coverage suggested by their separate densities. The problem is an inverse question: instead of estimating a sumset from information about $A$ and $B$, it asks what the sets must look like when the estimate is sharp. The quantifiers matter. This is a question about subsets of the positive integers, ordinary natural density, and the unrestricted pair of sets; it is not a question about finite sets, Schnirelmann density, or merely lower density.

## A small example

A completely checkable equality comes from periodic residue classes. Let $A$ contain precisely the positive integers congruent to $0$ or $3$ modulo $6$, and let $B$ contain precisely those congruent to $0$ or $1$ modulo $6$. In one period the supports are

$$
P_A=\{0,3\},\qquad P_B=\{0,1\}.
$$

Their residue sums are

$$
P_A+P_B=\{0+0,0+1,3+0,3+1\}\pmod 6=\{0,1,3,4\}.
$$

Thus $A$ and $B$ have densities $2/6=1/3$ each, while $A+B$ is eventually, in fact periodically apart from the harmless positive-integer boundary, supported on four residue classes and has density $4/6=2/3$. The exact identity is

$$
\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{6}.
$$

This was verified by enumerating the four residue sums, with no floating-point approximation. It illustrates why finite cyclic groups and modular obstructions naturally enter the subject. It does not classify all equality cases: periodic examples are only one family, and a finite residue calculation says nothing about arbitrary nonperiodic sets.

The frozen catalogue entry at [teorth/erdosproblems commit `536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89`](https://github.com/teorth/erdosproblems/tree/536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89), whose `data/problems.yaml` entry has blob `4e60263a80af6207aca494d2e1388e44fa372889`, records Problem 335 as open, unformalized, and last updated 2025-08-31. That snapshot is the programme's source lock; its status is not a proof that no later mathematics exists.

A major partial result is the primary paper by Ethan Ackelsberg and Florian K. Richter, *An inverse theorem for sumsets of sets of positive density in the integers*, arXiv:2604.12864v1 (submitted 14 April 2026). Their Theorem 1.4 assumes $d(A)>0$, $d(A)+d(B)<1$, that $B$ meets every residue class in the sense that $B$ intersects every set of the form $a\mathbb N+b$, and equality along a sequence tending to infinity. It concludes that, after a finite-modulus decomposition, either the pair is described up to zero density by parallel Bohr intervals, or a second modular and translation-invariant alternative occurs. The paper explicitly identifies this as a partial answer to the Erdős–Graham question, not a solution of the unrestricted problem.

The maintained [live Problem 335 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/335) gives the same core question and an open status in the accessible indexed record. It also records the rotation or Bohr-interval construction and the obstruction from sets supported on even integers. ## What remains

The missing mathematical object is an unrestricted inverse theorem. Ackelsberg–Richter remove the difficulty when one summand meets every residue class, but Problem 335 permits both summands to have modular obstructions. A complete result would need to control equality after decomposing the sets into residue sections, including overlaps between different section-sums and sparse or exceptional sections whose translates can still affect a positive-density sumset.

A useful bounded successor would first capture the exact statement and conventions in a source-locked record, then verify a finite-modulus subclass: enumerate nonempty residue supports $P,Q\subseteq\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z$, retain those with $|P+Q|=|P|+|Q|$, and prove the corresponding ultimately periodic density identity. That would be a genuine finite lemma and a regression test for notation, but not evidence for the arbitrary-set classification. Any broader claim should wait for specialist mathematical review of the primary theorem and the unrestricted modular case.
