## The question in plain language

Take $n$ distinct points in the plane. At any one point $x$, count the **different** distances from $x$ to the other $n-1$ points. Call that number $R(x)$. Several neighbours may be equally far away, so $R(x)$ can be smaller than $n-1$.

Order the points so that

$$
R(x_1)\leq R(x_2)\leq\cdots\leq R(x_n).
$$

[Erdős Problem #652](https://www.erdosproblems.com/652) asks how small $R(x_k)$ can be when we insist on $k$ points with relatively few distinct distances. The maintained formulation calls $\alpha_k$ the least threshold (or infimum if the threshold is not attained) such that, **for all sufficiently large $n$**, there exists a configuration of $n$ points with

$$
R(x_k)<\alpha_k\sqrt n.
$$

Must $\alpha_k$ grow without bound as $k\to\infty$? According to the maintained catalogue and published work, the answer is **yes**. Crucially, $k$ is fixed first and $n$ then grows.

## A small example

Place five points equally spaced around a circle of radius $1$. From any vertex of the resulting regular pentagon, the other four vertices occur at only **two** distances: the side length and the diagonal length. Their exact squared values are

$$
d_{\mathrm{side}}^2=\frac{5-\sqrt5}{2},
\qquad
d_{\mathrm{diagonal}}^2=\frac{5+\sqrt5}{2}.
$$

Thus, after ordering,

$$
(R(x_1),R(x_2),R(x_3),R(x_4),R(x_5))=(2,2,2,2,2).
$$

This calculation was independently checked with numerical coordinates $(\cos(2\pi j/5),\sin(2\pi j/5))$ for $j=0,\ldots,4$: every vertex has precisely two squared distances, approximately $1.381966$ and $3.618034$.

The example distinguishes *different radii around one point* from *all pairs of points*. It does not contradict the question's asymptotic lower bound, which concerns arbitrarily large $n$ for each fixed $k$.

## What is known

A particularly useful published theorem appears in Surya Mathialagan's [*On Bipartite Distinct Distances in the Plane*](https://doi.org/10.37236/9687), *Electronic Journal of Combinatorics* **28** (2021), P4.33. Its **Theorem 14, printed page 7**, says that if $P$ and $Q$ are planar point sets with $|P|=m$, $|Q|=N$, and

$$
2\leq m\leq N^{1/3},
$$

then **some point in $P$** determines at least $c\sqrt{mN}$ different distances to the points of $Q$, for a universal $c>0$. This *pointwise* assertion is stronger than saying the union of all distances between $P$ and $Q$ is large. The published proof uses a graph crossing-number argument.

Apply this theorem to the first $k$ ordered points and their complement:

$$
P=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\},
\qquad Q=\{x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n\}.
$$

For fixed $k\geq2$ and sufficiently large $n$, the condition $k\leq(n-k)^{1/3}$ holds. The theorem gives some $p\in P$ at least $c\sqrt{k(n-k)}$ different distances to $Q$. But every $p\in P$ has **at most** $R(x_k)$ distances even when all other points are counted. Hence

$$
R(x_k)\geq c\sqrt{k(n-k)}.
$$

For $n\geq2k$, divide by $\sqrt n$:

$$
\frac{R(x_k)}{\sqrt n}
\geq c\sqrt{k}\sqrt{1-\frac{k}{n}}
\geq \frac{c}{\sqrt2}\sqrt k.
$$

The bound applies to *every* configuration. It therefore also constrains the least possible threshold in the maintained definition:

$$
\boxed{\alpha_k=\Omega(\sqrt k)}.
$$

In particular $\alpha_k\to\infty$. This consequence uses a **published theorem** and elementary quantifier manipulation. It is not a GCL mathematical discovery or an independently certified proof of Mathialagan's deeper theorem.

The maintained page also credits Elekes with constructions satisfying $R(x_k)=O_k(\sqrt n)$ for each **fixed** $k$. There is no contradiction: the hidden constant in $O_k$ may grow with $k$. A distinct [2026 preprint by Feng and collaborators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401) gives an $\Omega(k^{1/4})$ conclusion using Pach–Sharir incidence estimates and discusses corrections to earlier incorrectly cited exponents. That weaker exponent does not supersede the published Mathialagan theorem's pointwise consequence.
