## The question in plain language

Take an integer $n$, write all its positive divisors in increasing order, and compare each divisor with the next one. Do these successive ratios typically add up to something large?

[Erdős Problem #673](https://www.erdosproblems.com/673) concerns

$$
1=d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_{\tau(n)}=n,\qquad
G(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau(n)-1}\frac{d_i}{d_{i+1}}.
$$

The original questions ask whether $G(n)$ tends to infinity for **almost all** integers $n$, and about the asymptotic behavior of its **summatory function**. The maintained catalogue lists the density-one assertion as **PROVED**. These questions must not be collapsed: proving a typical-growth statement does not produce a sharp mean-value formula.

## A small example

For $n=12$ the divisors are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Their successive ratios sum to

$$
G(12)=\frac12+\frac23+\frac34+\frac46+\frac6{12}
=\frac{37}{12}>3.
$$

For a prime $p$, however, there are only two divisors, $1,p$, so $G(p)=1/p$. Therefore $G(n)$ does **not** tend to infinity along *every* sequence of integers. The important phrase is **almost all**, meaning the exceptional integers form a set of natural density zero.

## What is known

There is a useful exact inequality. Choose any divisor $m>1$ of $n$. For every $t\mid n/m$, both $t$ and $mt$ divide $n$, and $t<mt$. If $t^+$ denotes the next divisor of $n$ after $t$, then

$$
t<t^+\leq mt,\qquad \frac t{t^+}\geq\frac1m.
$$

Different divisors $t\mid n/m$ correspond to different summands, so

$$
\frac{\tau(n/m)}m\leq G(n)<\tau(n),\qquad m>1,\ m\mid n.
$$

The upper bound follows because every successive ratio is strictly less than one. For even $n$, taking $m=2$ and noting $\tau(n/2)\geq\tau(n)/2$ gives

$$
G(n)\geq\frac14\tau(n).
$$

**A source-precision correction.** The maintained catalogue attributes the lower inequality to Terence Tao and prints it for 'any divisor $m\mid n$'. The restriction $m>1$ is essential: at $m=1$ and $n=5$, the displayed inequality would falsely claim $\tau(5)=2\leq G(5)=1/5$. The [GCL audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1944#issuecomment-6156972845) flags the literal discrepancy and gives the valid elementary argument. This is a qualification to an auxiliary statement, not evidence that the main proved density-one result is false.

**Why the sum usually grows.** If a prime $p$ divides $n$, the same argument gives

$$
G(n)\geq\frac{\tau(n/p)}p\geq\frac{\tau(n)}{2p}.
$$

For any fixed large cutoff $M$, the proportion of integers divisible by at least one prime $p\leq M$ is

$$
1-\prod_{p\leq M}\left(1-\frac1p\right),
$$

which approaches one as $M$ increases. Classical number theory also shows that $\tau(n)$ tends to infinity in **natural density one**. Thus, among the overwhelming majority of integers, one can choose a small prime factor $p\leq M$ and infer a large lower bound for $G(n)$.

More formally, for every fixed threshold $T$, the upper density of integers with $G(n)\leq T$ tends to zero. This establishes the almost-all conclusion, without claiming any convergence for every single integer.

The maintained record also attributes to Erdős and Tenenbaum the existence of a continuous distribution function for the normalized ratio $G(n)/\tau(n)$. That assertion is historical attribution pending full original-paper verification; it should not be rephrased as a sharp formula for $\sum_{n\leq X}G(n)$ without a separate theorem.
