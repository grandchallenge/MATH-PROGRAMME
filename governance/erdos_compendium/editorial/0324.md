## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 324 asks whether there is one integer-coefficient polynomial whose values behave like a particularly collision-resistant list of integers. More precisely, seek a polynomial $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ such that whenever $a<b$ and $c<d$ are nonnegative integers, the equality

$$
f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d)
$$

forces $(a,b)=(c,d)$. Thus every sum of two different entries is unique: one can recover the two inputs from their sum. A set with this property is called a Sidon set. The question is existential and global: it concerns every pair of nonnegative inputs, not merely a large finite range or a tail of the sequence.

The maintained Erdős Problems page paraphrases the object as the set of values for $n\ge 1$. The catalogue statement and the pinned formal statement use nonnegative integers, including $0$. That indexing difference should not be silently erased when comparing results; a tail theorem and the full problem are different assertions.

## A small example

The candidate quartic family discussed in the live forum is $f_t(x)=x^4+t^3x$. It has an exact collision for every positive integer $t$:

$$
f_t(23t)+f_t(27t)=f_t(6t)+f_t(30t)=811332t^4.
$$

For a fully concrete check, take $t=1$. Then $f_1(23)=279864$, $f_1(27)=531468$, $f_1(6)=1302$, and $f_1(30)=810030$. Hence both sums equal $811332$, while $(23,27)\ne(6,30)$ and both pairs have increasing, nonnegative entries. This is a reproducible exact-arithmetic disproof of the proposed family, not a disproof of every quartic polynomial. It also illustrates the standard obstruction: one nontrivial equal-sum quadruple is enough to destroy the Sidon property.

## What is known

The original source is Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, p. 53, as identified by the maintained entry. It calls the problem difficult and suggests $x^5$ as a likely candidate. Ruzsa's primary paper, “An almost polynomial Sidon sequence,” proves a nearby result: for some real $\xi\in[0,1]$ and a suitable $n_0$, the tail set

$$
\{n^5+\lfloor\xi n^4\rfloor:n\ge n_0\}
$$

is Sidon. This is strong evidence for the shape of a construction, but it is not an integer polynomial and does not cover the initial values.

The strongest directly relevant obstruction located in this audit is Dubickas and Novikas, “No cubic integer polynomial generates a Sidon sequence,” *Mathematische Nachrichten* 294 (2021), 1859–1865. The publisher's primary-paper abstract states that no integer-coefficient polynomial of degree at most three generates a Sidon tail, and that the proof constructs infinitely many solutions of $f(m)+f(n)=f(r)+f(s)$ in pairwise distinct positive integers. The publisher record dates first publication to 3 October 2021. The live entry also reports that linear and quadratic cases are elementary and that $x^4$ fails by a classical result of Swinnerton-Dyer, “$A^4+B^4=C^4+D^4$ Revisited,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 43 (1968), 149–151.

These results leave the central construction question unresolved. In particular, the cited sources do not establish that $x^5$ works, nor do they settle whether some quartic polynomial works. The live forum's geometric discussion is explicitly speculative, and the finite quartic-family collision above is a check of a particular family rather than a published classification.

## What remains

The next useful bounded question is narrower than the full problem: decide a specified candidate quartic or quintic polynomial by producing either a symbolic collision family or a proof that its equal-sum equation has only the trivial pair symmetries. A computational search may find a collision and can falsify a candidate, but a finite collision-free range cannot prove an infinite Sidon property. Any proposed successor should fix the domain, distinguish ordered from unordered pairs, and state whether it targets the full sequence or only a tail.

For $x^5$, the missing object is an all-input proof that $a^5+b^5=c^5+d^5$ with $a<b$ and $c<d$ implies equality of pairs. For general quartics, the missing object is either one explicit polynomial with a proof of that implication or a theorem ruling out all integer quartics. Neither is supplied here.
