## The question in plain language

For an integer $n\geq2$, take the factorial $n!=1\cdot2\cdots n$. Erdős asks how close together the factors can be when $n!$ is written as a product of distinct increasing integers. In the maintained wording, $f(n)$ is the least integer $m\geq1$ for which

$$
n!=a_1a_2\cdots a_t,\qquad a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_t=a_1+m.
$$

The natural working convention is that the $a_i$ are positive integers. The displayed live statement does not spell out the sign domain or say separately that $t\geq2$; the condition $m\geq1$ already rules out a one-term representation, while positivity is a normalization that should be checked against the printed source. For fixed $m$, every factor lies in the short interval from $a_1$ to $a_1+m$, and the endpoints must both occur. The problem is to understand the resulting function as $n$ grows, not merely to find a few values.

The original source attribution is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), p. 76, recorded as [ErGr80] by the maintained page. The exact source-audit issue is [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1663](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1663); its original SOURCE RESULT/1 citation is **[authenticated original RESULT/1](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1663#issuecomment-6143411875)**.

## A small example

For $n=4$, exact arithmetic gives $4!=24$. Two valid factorizations with endpoint gap $2$ are

$$
24=4\cdot6=2\cdot3\cdot4.
$$

There is no gap $1$ factorization: with strictly increasing integer factors and largest factor one more than the smallest, the only possible tuple is $(a,a+1)$, so one would need $a(a+1)=24$. The quadratic $a^2+a-24=0$ has discriminant $97$, which is not a square. An exhaustive exact enumeration of all increasing positive tuples whose product is $24$ returned minimum gap $2$, gap $2$ witnesses $(4,6)$ and $(2,3,4)$, and no gap $1$ witness. Thus $f(4)=2$ under the positive-factor normalization.

This is a finite certificate only. It checks one small input and says nothing about whether $f(n)$ is bounded, tends to infinity, or follows a regular asymptotic law. It also does not settle whether allowing negative factors would change the intended definition; that sign convention belongs to the source-normalization record.

## What is known

The maintained entry says that Erdős and Graham did not even know whether $f(n)=1$ for infinitely many $n$. The condition $f(n)=1$ means that $n!$ is a product of two consecutive positive integers, because there is no integer strictly between the endpoints. This remains an open subquestion, not a numerical pattern that finite computation can settle.

There are rigorous sparsity results for each fixed gap. Let $F_m(N)$ count the integers $n\leq N$ with $f(n)=m$. Once $m$ is fixed, choose which of the internal points $a_1+1,\ldots,a_1+m-1$ occur. Each choice gives a fixed polynomial in $a_1$, namely the product of the selected terms from $a_1$ through $a_1+m$, and $f(n)=m$ is contained in the union of the corresponding equations $P(a_1)=n!$. Berend and Osgood proved that this kind of polynomial-factorial equation occurs for a density-zero set of $n$; consequently the maintained page records $F_m(N)=o(N)$ for every fixed $m$. Their paper is Daniel Berend and Charles F. Osgood, “On the equation $P(x)=n!$ and a question of Erdős,” *Journal of Number Theory* 42 (1992), 189–193, [doi:10.1016/0022-314X(92)90020-P](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-314X(92)90020-P).

Bui, Pratt, and Zaharescu give the stronger fixed-polynomial estimate that leads here to $F_m(N)\ll_m N^{33/34}$. Their primary paper is “Power savings for counting solutions to polynomial-factorial equations,” *Advances in Mathematics* 422 (2023), article 109021, [doi:10.1016/j.aim.2023.109021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aim.2023.109021). These results control every fixed gap separately; they do not provide a uniform bound in $m$ and therefore do not determine the behavior of $f(n)$ itself.

Finally, Luca showed that the ABC conjecture implies $f(n)\to\infty$. The implication uses the same finite family of polynomial-factorial equations for each fixed $m$: ABC gives finiteness for each such family, and hence no fixed gap can occur infinitely often. This is conditional, not an unconditional solution. See Florian Luca, “The Diophantine equation $P(x)=n!$ and a result of M. Overholt,” *Glasnik Matematički* 37 (2002), 269–273, [open primary PDF](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/file/7833).

## What remains

The main unresolved question is the global behavior of $f(n)$: in particular, whether fixed gaps recur infinitely often, whether $f(n)$ tends to infinity unconditionally, and whether a sharper asymptotic description exists. The estimates for a fixed $m$ leave a genuine quantifier gap: they say what happens after $m$ is held constant, while the minimizing $m=f(n)$ may grow with $n$.

A bounded successor task is to construct an exact-arithmetic certificate for a chosen limit $N$: enumerate all strictly increasing positive tuples with product $n!$ for every $2\leq n\leq N$, record $f(n)$ and one witness, and independently verify the enumeration by prime-exponent vectors. Acceptance requires equal products, strict ordering, endpoint gap, and a proof that every smaller gap has been excluded for each tested $n$. Falsification is a single missed tuple, an incorrect minimum, or a discrepancy between integer products and prime exponents. Stop at the declared $N$ or at resource exhaustion; do not infer an infinite theorem from the table. A separate theorem task would need to preserve the distinction between fixed and growing gaps and explicitly resolve the sign convention from [ErGr80, p. 76].
