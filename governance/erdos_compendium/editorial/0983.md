# Erdős Problem #983 — Prime supports of large integer subsets

## The exact problem and its strict inequality

Fix integers $n\ge2$ and $\pi(n)<k\le n$, where $\pi(x)$ counts primes at most $x$. For an integer $a\ge1$, let $P(a)$ be its set of prime divisors (so $P(1)=\varnothing$). For a prime set $S$ and a subset $A$ of the first $n$ positive integers, write

$$
A[S]=\{a\in A:P(a)\subseteq S\}.
$$

Erdős and Straus considered the least integer $f(k,n)$ such that, for **every** subset of size $k$ $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, **some** set of $r=f(k,n)$ primes has

$$
|A[S]|>r.
$$

In more operational terms, $r$ primes must capture **strictly more than $r$ members** of $A$ whose **every** prime divisor belongs to the selected set. A member having even one unselected prime factor is not captured. This is not the same as requiring only one of its prime factors to belong to $S$.

The conjecture in the [maintained #983 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/983) asks whether

$$
2\pi(\sqrt n)-f(\pi(n)+1,n)\longrightarrow+\infty
$$

as $n\to\infty$, and more generally seeks the behavior of $f(k,n)$ when $\pi(n)+1<k=o(n)$.

**Status exception:** the catalogue is **OPEN**, but a 30 April 2026 construction by Liam Price, attributed partly to GPT-5.5 Pro, claims a **negative answer to the first question**. A mathematical explanation by Nat Sothanaphan and a related independent published 1979 result of Carl Pomerance support this claim. Under the *literal quantifier order currently printed on the website*, the combination gives a complete conditional-on-source-fidelity disproof, reconstructed below. Whether the website's quantifiers exactly match the original Erdős–Straus paper is **under active dispute** in the forum. GCL has not issued independent source adjudication or a formal proof receipt, and this article does not retroactively update the protected register.

## The minimal Hall-deficiency formulation

For a nonempty subset $B\subseteq A$, let

$$
P(B)=\bigcup_{a\in B}P(a).
$$

Because $|A|=k>\pi(n)$, there are more elements of $A$ than there are primes up to $n$. Construct the bipartite graph whose left vertices are elements of $A$, right vertices are primes at most $n$, and connect an integer $a$ to each of its prime divisors. Hall's marriage theorem implies that there exists a subset $B\subseteq A$ with

$$
|B|>|P(B)|.
$$

Alternatively, the full set $A$ itself has this strict inequality; Hall's theorem is conceptually useful because a **minimal such** $B$ has rigid incidence structure.

For a given $A$, define the least deficiency-support size as

$$
r(A)=\min_{\substack{B\subseteq A\\|B|>|P(B)|}}
|P(B)|.
$$

This is **equivalent** to the earlier definition using selected prime sets: if $|B|>|P(B)|$ take $S=P(B)$; conversely, if $|A[S]|>|S|$, take $B=A[S]$ and note $P(B)\subseteq S$, which gives a deficiency witness of no larger support size. Hence the displayed catalogue quantifiers give

$$
f(k,n)=\max_{\substack{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\|A|=k}}r(A).
$$

This maximum over choices of $A$ form is *not* equivalent to reversing the order of choices and demanding **one universal prime set $S$ that works for every $A$**. The original source [Er70b, p.138] must be checked directly to resolve the disputed quantifier order. The special element $1$, which has empty prime support, introduces a harmless zero-prime witness if empty prime sets are permitted; it does not affect the **worst-case lower-bound constructions** here, which contain no $1$.

## A complete upper bound

Select a subset $B\subseteq A$ **minimal by inclusion** with $|B|>|P(B)|$. Put $r=|P(B)|$. If $b\in B$ is removed, minimality gives

$$
|B|-1\le|P(B\setminus\{b\})|\le r.
$$

Combining this with $|B|>r$ yields

$$
|B|=r+1.
$$

Every prime in $P(B)$ must divide **at least two elements** of $B$. Otherwise, removing its unique incident integer would produce a smaller deficiency witness, contradicting minimality.

Let $t=\pi(\sqrt n)$. There are at most $t$ prime divisors in $P(B)$ at most $\sqrt n$. Each integer $a\le n$ has **at most one distinct prime divisor larger than $\sqrt n$**, since two such primes would have product exceeding $n$. Each large prime of $P(B)$ divides at least two members of $B$, so counting incidences gives at most $|B|/2$ distinct large primes. Hence

$$
r\le t+\frac{|B|}{2}
=t+\frac{r+1}{2},
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{f(k,n)\le2\pi(\sqrt n)+1.}
$$

This is a **complete elementary proof** of the catalogue's uniform upper bound, using only finite counting. It is valid for every $k>\pi(n)$ and every $A$ under the website's quantifier convention.

## An exact extremal certificate at $n=24$

Here $\pi(24)=9$ and $\pi(\sqrt{24})=2$. Take the ten-element set

$$
A=\{5,11,13,14,15,17,19,21,22,23\}.
$$

Its prime supports are

$$
\begin{array}{c|cccccccccc}
a&5&11&13&14&15&17&19&21&22&23\\
P(a)&5&11&13&2,7&3,5&17&19&3,7&2,11&23
\end{array}.
$$

In the table, comma-separated entries indicate a **set of prime divisors**, not an ordered sequence or integer multiplication.

Put

$$
C=\{5,11,14,15,21,22\},\qquad
P(C)=\{2,3,5,7,11\}.
$$

Then $|C|=6>|P(C)|=5$, so five selected primes capture six elements. The remaining four elements of $A$ are the singleton primes $13,17,19,23$.

A second, independent exact computation exhaustively checked the $2^9=512$ candidate prime subsets of $\{2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,23\}$. **No prime subset with fewer than five members captures more elements than its size**, while the one set $P(C)$ of size five succeeds. Thus

$$
r(A)=5.
$$

Since the proved universal upper bound is $2\pi(\sqrt{24})+1=5$, it follows **without any untested exhaustive search over all ten-element sets** that

$$
f(10,24)=5,\qquad
2\pi(\sqrt{24})-f(10,24)=-1.
$$

This is a **finite verified counterexample to positivity of the gap at one $n$**, but by itself it does not disprove a limit tending to $+\infty$.

## The infinite counterexample mechanism

Let $q_1<q_2<\cdots$ denote the primes. Pomerance's [1979 theorem on good primes](https://www.erdosproblems.com/453) asserts that there are **infinitely many indices $N$** such that

$$
q_{N-i}\,q_{N+i}<q_N^2
\qquad(1\le i<N).
$$

This is a **published result**, not an unproved conjecture. It settles another Erdős problem, [#453](https://www.erdosproblems.com/453), and Pomerance's primary reference is *The prime number graph*, *Mathematics of Computation* **33** (1979), pp.399–408.

For one such $N$, let $n=q_N^2-1$. Then

$$
\pi(\sqrt n)=N-1.
$$

Form a path whose **$2N-1$ distinct vertices** are the primes in this order:

$$
q_{2N-1},\ q_1,\ q_{2N-2},\ q_2,\ldots,
q_{N+1},\ q_{N-1},\ q_N.
$$

For every adjacent pair of vertices put their **product** in a set $C$, and also put in the **two endpoint primes** $q_{2N-1}$ and $q_N$ themselves. Thus $C$ has $2N-2$ edge-products and two endpoint primes, for a total of **$2N$ distinct integers**; its set $V=P(C)$ of prime divisors has **$2N-1$ elements**.

The Pomerance inequalities ensure every edge-product is at most $n$: the edges corresponding to $q_jq_{2N-j}$ are strictly below $q_N^2$, and the interleaved edges have no larger products than those. The singleton endpoint primes are also below $n$ for all sufficiently large $N$. Thus $C\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$.

For every **proper** subset $B\subsetneq C$,

$$
|B|\le|P(B)|.
$$

This is a finite graph fact: interpret the two endpoint singletons as **loops** at the endpoints of an otherwise ordinary path. A proper subset of the path edges and these two loops has at most one loop in each connected component; its number of chosen edges-plus-loops does not exceed its number of incident vertices. Only the **full path with both endpoint loops** has one more element than support vertices.

Now adjoin to $C$ **every prime $p\le n$ not belonging to the vertex set $V$**:

$$
A=C\cup\{p\le n:p\text{ prime},\ p\notin V\}.
$$

This precise distinction matters: adjoining primes merely "not in $C$" would accidentally include some **internal path-vertex primes** and give the wrong cardinality. With the support-exclusion rule above,

$$
|A|=2N+\bigl(\pi(n)-(2N-1)\bigr)=\pi(n)+1.
$$

Each newly adjoined prime is an isolated support element, so it cannot create a smaller Hall-deficient subset. The **unique minimal prime-support deficiency** is the complete $C$, of support size $2N-1$. Consequently

$$
r(A)=2N-1.
$$

The universal upper bound then yields the **exact equality**

$$
f(\pi(n)+1,n)=2N-1=2\pi(\sqrt n)+1
$$

for these arbitrarily large $n=q_N^2-1$. Therefore

$$
2\pi(\sqrt n)-f(\pi(n)+1,n)=-1
$$

on an infinite sequence, and the conjectured divergence to $+\infty$ **fails under the currently displayed definition**.

This proof is a detailed reconstruction of **Price's 2026 construction** combined with **Pomerance's established 1979 theorem**; it is not a GCL novelty claim. The **remaining source-adjudication issue is the original quantifier order in [Er70b]**, not the already verified $n=24$ witness or the logical implication from the published good-prime theorem.

## Earlier estimates and the wider second question

Erdős and Straus [Er70b] established, in the notation of the catalogue,

$$
f(\pi(n)+1,n)=2\pi(\sqrt n)+
o_A\left(\frac{\sqrt n}{(\log n)^A}\right)
\quad\text{for every fixed }A>0.
$$

This *very sharp relative asymptotic* still allows a bounded gap of $-1$ on infinitely many cutoffs because the error $o_A(\sqrt n/(\log n)^A)$ can diverge in absolute size. No contradiction exists.

They also proved, for any fixed $0<c<1$ and integer $k$ near $cn$,

$$
f(k,n)=\log\log n+
(c_1+o(1))\sqrt{2\log\log n},
$$

where the real $c_1$ is specified by the Gaussian quantile condition

$$
c=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}
\int_{-\infty}^{c_1}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx.
$$

This settles the **fixed positive-density** regime $k\asymp n$, not the broad intermediate interval $\pi(n)+1<k=o(n)$. The 2026 discussion suggests a plateau near $2\pi(\sqrt n)$ over part of that intermediate interval, but the displayed extensions depend on additional set constructions and have **not been independently audited**. The second, general estimation request remains substantially open.

A wording revision on 20 October 2025 changed "at least $r$" to **strictly more than $r$**. This is not cosmetic: a singleton prime can satisfy a weak $|A[S]|\ge|S|$ condition without any Hall deficiency. The exact value $2\pi(\sqrt n)+1$ and the path construction are sensitive to this distinction.
