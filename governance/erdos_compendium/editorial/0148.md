## The question in plain language

How many ways can one express the number one as the sum of **exactly $k$ distinct unit fractions**? Let $F(k)$ count solutions of

$$
1=\frac1{n_1}+\cdots+\frac1{n_k},
\qquad 1\le n_1<\cdots<n_k,
$$

where the denominators are positive integers. The strict ordering prevents counting permutations of the same representation multiple times and prohibits repeated denominators.

Erdős and Graham asked for good upper and lower estimates on $F(k)$ as $k$ becomes large. This is a counting problem, not merely an existence question.

## A small example

For $k=1$, the unique representation is $1=1/1$, so $F(1)=1$. For $k=2$ there is no representation with distinct positive denominators: if the first denominator is at least two, the sum of two strictly decreasing unit fractions is below one, and if it is one, any second term overshoots. Hence $F(2)=0$.

For $k=3$, the representation

$$
1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac16
$$

is valid. Exact enumeration establishes $F(3)=1$; one should not substitute unrestricted Egyptian fractions in which denominator repetition is allowed.

## What is known

The sharp growth of $F(k)$ remains **OPEN**. The maintained [Erdős #148](https://www.erdosproblems.com/148) records the double-exponential-type bounds

$$
2^{c^{\,k/\log k}}\le F(k)
\le c_0^{(1/5+o(1))2^k},
$$

where $c>1$ is an absolute constant (with a base adjustment absorbed into the notation) and $c_0\approx1.26408$ is the Vardi constant. The lower bound is attributed to S. V. Konyagin, [*Double exponential lower bound for the number of representations of unity by Egyptian fractions*](https://oeis.org/A006585), *Mathematical Notes* 95 (2014), 277–281. The upper bound is attributed to Christian Elsholtz and Stefan Planitzer (2021).

OEIS [A006585](https://oeis.org/A006585) independently records the small values $F(1),\ldots,F(8)$ as $1,0,1,6,72,2320,245765,151182379$. Its values are useful finite checks, not independent certification of the large-parameter analytic estimates. The strict-ordering condition is material: counting ordered tuples multiplies solutions by $k!$, while permitting repeated denominators changes the count in another way.

## What remains

A bounded exact-rational search enumerates all strict $k$ term Egyptian fraction representations of one for $k=1,2,3,4$. Acceptance: $F(1)=1$, $F(2)=0$, $F(3)=1$, $F(4)=6$, no duplicates from permutations, and every rational sum checked using integer cross multiplication. A terminating search needs mathematically justified denominator bounds, not an arbitrary cutoff. This finite fixture cannot establish double-exponential asymptotics.
