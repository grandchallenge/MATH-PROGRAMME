## The question in plain language

Start with a positive integer $n$. Put $a_1=n$, and having chosen the increasing sequence $a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k$, choose $a_{k+1}$ to be the smallest integer that is not a sum of a nonempty consecutive block from the already chosen terms. Erdős Problem 359 asks how densely the resulting sequence occupies the positive integers. The especially studied case is $n=1$.

For $n=1$, the first question is whether the terms eventually become sparse enough that $a_k/k$ tends to infinity. The second asks whether they remain far below every power strictly larger than linear: for each $c>0$, does $a_k/k^{1+c}$ tend to zero? Andrews proposed the sharper prediction

$$
a_k\sim \frac{k\log k}{\log\log k}.
$$

Here “consecutive” refers to consecutive positions in the sequence, not consecutive integers. Thus $a_3$ is tested against $a_1$, $a_2$, and $a_1+a_2$, while the order and the least-unrepresented rule matter at every later step. The general parameter $n$ is part of the original problem, even though the two displayed limit questions concern $n=1$.

## A small example

For $n=1$, exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
1,2,4,5,8,10,14,15,16,21,22,25,26,28,33.
$$

The first nontrivial choices show the rule. From the prefix $1,2$, the consecutive sums are $1$, $2$, and $3$, so the least integer greater than $2$ not represented is $4$. From $1,2,4$, the consecutive sums are $1,2,3,4,6,7$; hence $5$ is next. From $1,2,4,5$, the values $6$ and $7$ are represented, but $8$ is not, so the next term is $8$.

The following finite checker was run with exact Python integers. It constructs every consecutive sum from the current prefix, searches upward from the previous term, and verifies the leastness condition through the fifteenth term:

```python
A = [1]
for _ in range(14):
    sums = {sum(A[i:j+1]) for i in range(len(A))
            for j in range(i, len(A))}
    x = A[-1] + 1
    while x in sums:
        x += 1
    A.append(x)
print(A)
print(all(A[k] > A[k-1] and
          all(y in {sum(A[i:j+1]) for i in range(k)
                    for j in range(i, k)}
              for y in range(A[k-1] + 1, A[k])) and
          A[k] not in {sum(A[i:j+1]) for i in range(k)
                       for j in range(i, k)}
          for k in range(1, len(A))))
```

The observed output was the displayed list followed by `True`. This is a reproducible finite check of the recurrence for these fifteen terms. It does not establish that an infinite sequence exists for every parameter in the original wording, prove either limit, establish the asymptotic conjecture, or settle any density statement.

## What is known

The readable primary source is Paul Erdős, “Problems and results on combinatorial number theory III,” *Number Theory Day*, Lecture Notes in Mathematics 626 (1977), pp. 43–72. On printed page 70, in the section on unconventional problems, Erdős describes the sequence beginning at $1$ and says that Andrews conjectured its growth, while noting that he did not know even whether $x_n/n$ tends to infinity. This is direct historical evidence that the lower-growth question was open in that source, not a modern proof-status certificate.

The current Erdős Problems entry, accessed 2026-10-13, still labels #359 open and says that it cannot be resolved by a finite computation. It identifies the sequence with OEIS A002048 and reports the initial terms above. It attributes the asymptotic prediction to George E. Andrews, “Mac Mahon’s Prime Numbers of Measurement,” *American Mathematical Monthly* 82 (1975), 922–923, and reports a result of Š. Porubský, “On MacMahon’s segmented numbers and related sequences,” *Nieuw Arch. Wisk.* (3) (1977), 403–408: for every $epsilon>0$, infinitely many $k$ satisfy

$$
a_k< (\log k)^\epsilon\frac{k\log k}{\log\log k},
$$

and, for the counting function $A(x)$, the page reports $\limsup A(x)/\pi(x)\ge 1/\log 2$. These are externally reported results here; the full Porubský paper was not available in the bounded source pass, so this article does not certify their proof details or translate them into a solved status.
