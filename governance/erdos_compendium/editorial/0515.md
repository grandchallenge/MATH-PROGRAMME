**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #515 is **PROVED**. It asks whether every transcendental entire function admits a **single path to infinity** along which every negative power of the function is integrable with respect to arc length. Lewis, Rossi and Weitsman proved a stronger subharmonic-function theorem in 1984. The complete original paper was retrieved and its main theorem checked directly.

## The question in plain language

Let $f$ be an entire function that is **not a polynomial**. Can one find a locally rectifiable path $\Gamma$ tending to infinity for which

$$
\boxed{\int_\Gamma |f(z)|^{-\lambda}|dz|<\infty
\quad\text{for every real }\lambda>0?}
$$

A *locally rectifiable* path has finite length on each bounded portion. Its total length may be infinite. The integration symbol $|dz|$ means **arc length**, not an oriented complex contour integral. A path can be parametrized by $\gamma(t)$, in which case the integrand is $|f(\gamma(t))|^{-\lambda}|\gamma'(t)|\,dt$ wherever the path has a derivative.

The quantifiers are the central point. We need

$$
\exists\,\Gamma\quad\forall\,\lambda>0,
$$

**one and the same path for all exponents**. Proving that each individual exponent admits a possibly different path,

$$
\forall\,\lambda>0\quad\exists\,\Gamma_\lambda,
$$

would not resolve Erdős's question.

When $f$ has zeros on the path, the inverse power can become infinite. A successful path must handle such points so that the asserted arc-length integrals still converge. The problem is not merely to find an escape direction on which $f$ is occasionally large.

## A small example

For $f(z)=e^z$, choose the positive real ray $\Gamma(t)=t$, $t\ge0$. Then

$$
|f(\Gamma(t))|=e^t,\qquad |d\Gamma|=dt.
$$

For every $\lambda>0$,

$$
\int_\Gamma |f(z)|^{-\lambda}|dz|
=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt
=\frac1\lambda<\infty.
$$

This is one **explicit common path** for all exponents. No path adjustment as $\lambda$ varies is needed.

The same elementary mechanism works for $f(z)=e^{z^m}$ with a positive integer $m$, along the positive real ray from $t=1$ onward. Since $t^m\ge t$ when $t\ge1$,

$$
\int_1^\infty e^{-\lambda t^m}\,dt
\le\int_1^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt
=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda}.
$$

This is an inequality that holds **for every** $\lambda>0$ with the path fixed. It does not imply that the same simple ray works for an arbitrary transcendental entire function.

Why require a non-polynomial? For the polynomial $f(z)=z^d$ on the positive ray from one,

$$
\int_1^\infty t^{-d\lambda}\,dt
$$

converges precisely when $d\lambda>1$. The polynomial example already shows why a superpolynomial-growth mechanism is relevant. The main theorem is much stronger: it constructs a suitable path even for functions with highly irregular growth and zeros.

## What is known

### Erdős's formulation and Huber's earlier theorem

The original *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.249](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=29), was visually inspected. After the separate question on superpolynomial growth along paths, Erdős cites a theorem of **Alfred Huber** establishing that for every $\lambda>0$ there is a locally rectifiable path $\Gamma_\lambda$ tending to infinity with the requisite finite integral. He then explicitly asks whether the same path can be chosen **independently of $\lambda$**.

Huber's *On subharmonic functions and differential geometry in the large*, *Commentarii Mathematici Helvetici* **32** (1957), 13–72, is the earlier source. Its complete original proof has not been re-examined in this tranche. A finite-order version of the common-path assertion is attributed to Guang Hou Zhang, *Asymptotic values of entire and meromorphic functions*, *Kexue Tongbao* (1977), 480, 486.

### The complete 1984 resolution

John Lewis, John Rossi and Allen Weitsman, *On the growth of subharmonic functions along paths*, *Arkiv för Matematik* **22** (1984), 109–119, [DOI 10.1007/BF02384375](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02384375), establish a stronger statement. The [complete original eleven-page journal PDF](https://archive.ymsc.tsinghua.edu.cn/pacm_download/116/7409-11512_2006_Article_BF02384375.pdf) was obtained and its opening theorem and first proof sections directly read.

For a subharmonic function $u$ on the plane, define

$$
M_u(r)=\max_{|z|=r}u(z).
$$

Their **Theorem 1**, printed on pp.109–110, assumes that

$$
\frac{M_u(r)}{\log r}\longrightarrow\infty
\quad (r\to\infty).
$$

It then gives a single path $\Gamma$ tending to infinity satisfying *both*

$$
\int_\Gamma e^{-\lambda u(z)}|dz|<\infty
\quad\text{for every }\lambda>0,
$$

and

$$
\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}\longrightarrow\infty
\quad\text{as }z\to\infty\text{ along }\Gamma.
$$

The authors explicitly point out the important feature: **the path is independent of $\lambda$**. This is the precise historical improvement Erdős requested, not merely a collection of separate paths.

The theorem is about general **subharmonic** functions, rather than only logarithms of entire functions. To recover Erdős #515, take

$$
u(z)=\log|f(z)|.
$$

The logarithm of the modulus of an entire function is subharmonic, with the standard value $-\infty$ at its zeros. Moreover,

$$
M_u(r)=\log M_f(r),\qquad
M_f(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.
$$

Because a transcendental entire function grows faster in maximum modulus than **every fixed polynomial**, $\log M_f(r)/\log r\to\infty$. Thus the hypotheses of Theorem 1 hold, and

$$
e^{-\lambda u(z)}=|f(z)|^{-\lambda}.
$$

This directly proves the asserted integral convergence using one path for all positive exponents.

The proof in the original paper employs a version of **Hall's lemma** for subharmonic functions, conformal mapping of simply connected domains and an inductive construction of paths through sublevel components. The theorem and proof architecture have been checked against the primary text; the entire transitive Hall-lemma argument has **not** been independently formalized or reproved by GCL.
