## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 316 asks whether a finite set of distinct positive integers, none equal to $1$, can always be split into two groups whose reciprocal sums are both strictly below $1$, provided the reciprocal sum of the whole set is below $2$. In symbols, for a finite set $A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}$, the question is whether

$$
\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}<2
$$

forces a partition $A=A_1\sqcup A_2$ with

$$
\sum_{n\in A_i}\frac{1}{n}<1\qquad(i=1,2).
$$

The word “set” matters: an integer may occur only once. A multiset version is easier to refute because repeated small denominators can put too much weight into either part. The strict inequalities also matter. A partition with one side exactly $1$ is not a successful partition.

The original source is Erdős and Graham’s *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), where the question appears on printed page 41. The source asks about distinct denominators and displays the two complementary reciprocal sums explicitly. This fixes the ambient object and the quantifier order more reliably than a short catalogue label.

## A small example

The maintained catalogue records the counterexample

$$
A=\{2,3,4,5,6,7,10,11,13,14,15\}.
$$

Its total reciprocal sum is

$$
\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}=\frac{120047}{60060}=1.998784548\ldots<2.
$$

That inequality alone does not show failure: in principle the terms might still be arranged into two groups below $1$. A finite exact check tests every possibility. The following calculation uses rational arithmetic, not floating-point comparisons.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

A = [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15]
v = [Fraction(1, n) for n in A]
total = sum(v, Fraction())
successes = []
for mask in range(1 << len(A)):
    left = sum((v[i] for i in range(len(A)) if mask >> i & 1), Fraction())
    right = total - left
    if left < 1 and right < 1:
        successes.append((left, right))
print(total, 1 << len(A), len(successes))
```

The actual output is `120047/60060 2048 0`. Thus this particular set satisfies the hypothesis, while no one of its $2048$ subset/complement pairs satisfies both strict upper bounds. The check establishes a concrete finite counterexample; it does not, by itself, establish that the set is minimal, prove Sándor’s general theorem, or certify any formal development.

## What is known

The live Erdős Problems entry reports that Csaba Sándor resolved the question negatively in “On a problem of Erdős,” *Journal of Number Theory* 63 (1997), 203–210. It says that the proper divisors of $120$, excluding $1$, already form a counterexample, and that Sándor proved a stronger statement: for every integer $n\geq2$, some finite set with reciprocal sum below $n$ cannot be partitioned into $n$ parts all having reciprocal sum below $1$.

The proper-divisor claim also has a short exact check. For

$$
B=\{2,3,4,5,6,8,10,12,15,20,24,30,40,60\},
$$

the sum is $239/120<2$. Enumerating its $2^{14}=16384$ subsets gives zero successful subset/complement pairs. This agrees with the reported counterexample, but it is not a substitute for reading Sándor’s proof. The publisher metadata identifies the article and its open-access status, but the article’s full text was not retrievable in this audit environment; theorem and page locations for the general result therefore remain unverified here.

The source evidence supports a careful status distinction. The two-part problem is disproved by an explicit finite set, and the finite arithmetic is reproducible. The stronger statement for all values of $n$ is reported by the maintained page and formal source comments, but its published proof has not been inspected in full during this audit. “Minimal counterexample” is likewise a maintained-site assertion, not a claim established by the small enumeration above.

## What remains

The most useful bounded successor is source closure for Sándor’s result. It should obtain a readable copy of the 1997 article, record its exact hash, identify the theorem and page proving the two-part counterexample and the extension to all values of $n$, and compare its hypotheses with the finite-set Lean theorem. In particular, it should check whether “partition into $n$ parts” means exactly $n$ nonempty parts, permits empty parts, or uses another convention, and whether the published construction has the same exclusion of $1$ and the same strict inequality.

A second, narrower task is a formal-source review: mark the completed two-part theorem separately from the generalized declaration with `sorry`, then test whether the generalized statement is definitionally or merely informally aligned with Sándor’s formulation. No Lean replay is needed to identify the visible `sorry` boundary. The finite rational checks are reusable regression tests, not a replacement for the missing source reading.
