## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 322 asks how often an integer can be written as a sum of exactly $k$ many kth powers, when $k\geq 3$. For a fixed $k$, let

$$
r_k(n)=\#\{(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\in\mathbb N_{>0}^k:x_1^k+\cdots+x_k^k=n\}.
$$

The tuples are ordered: changing the order of the same summands counts again. The problem asks for the order of growth of $r_k(n)$ and, more specifically, whether there is a constant $c>0$ such that $r_k(n)>n^c$ for infinitely many $n$. This is an asymptotic question for each fixed exponent, not a request to find one unusually representable integer.

The [original issue](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1592) identifies the problem as an open, source-audit assignment. Its pinned packet points to the maintained [Problem 322 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/322), and to `teorth/erdosproblems` commit [`536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89`](https://github.com/teorth/erdosproblems/tree/536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89). In that commit, the relevant `data/problems.yaml` entry has Git blob `4e60263a80af6207aca494d2e1388e44fa372889`, status `open`, and last update `2025-08-31`.

## A small example

Take $k=3$ and $n=10$. Any positive base $x$ occurring in a representation satisfies $x^3\leq 10$, so $x$ is either $1$ or $2$. The exact possibilities are therefore

$$
1^3+1^3+2^3=10,
$$

with the larger summand in any one of the three positions. Thus

$$
r_3(10)=3,
$$

and the ordered triples are $(1,1,2)$, $(1,2,1)$, and $(2,1,1)$. The following exact-arithmetic enumeration returns precisely those three triples:

```python
from itertools import product
sol = [x for x in product(range(1, 11), repeat=3) if sum(t**3 for t in x) == 10]
print(sol, len(sol))
```

Its output is `[(1, 1, 2), (1, 2, 1), (2, 1, 1)] 3`; no floating-point approximation is involved. This verifies the definition at one finite input. It says nothing about the order of $r_3(n)$ as $n$ grows, nor about infinitely many $n$ with a fixed positive-power lower bound.

The example also makes the convention important. The 1936 primary paper defines its function using nonnegative bases, so zero-containing triples are included there. The positive-base convention above is the one used in the current formal file. These conventions agree only after an explicit comparison, not by notation alone.

## What is known

The primary source is Paul Erdős, “On the Representation of an Integer as the Sum of k k-th Powers,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 11 (1936), 133–136. An [accessible scan](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1936-09.pdf) shows on its first page that $f(m)$ counts representations by kth powers of nonnegative integers. It recalls Hardy and Littlewood’s Hypothesis K, namely an upper bound of the form $f(m)=O_\epsilon(m^\epsilon)$ for every $\epsilon>0$, and states the opposite-direction result

$$
f(m)>\exp\left(\frac{c_1\log m}{\log\log m}\right)
$$

for infinitely many $m$, with a positive constant depending on $k$. The proof occupies the remainder of the paper, including separate odd- and even $k$ arguments. In the normalization $\exp(c_1\log m/\log\log m)=m^{c_1/\log\log m}$, this grows faster than every fixed power of $\log m$ but slower than $m^\delta$ for any fixed $\delta>0$.

The same paper records that Mahler had just shown Hypothesis K to be false for $k=3$, with many representations along a sequence of twelfth powers. The maintained page also attributes the corresponding lower-bound result independently to Erdős and Chowla, and records Mahler’s $k=3$ result, the weaker Hardy–Littlewood Hypothesis K-star, and Erdős’s later unpublished positive-density variant. Those are useful literature leads, but the page itself warns that its status reflects the owner’s current belief and may omit literature. No claim here promotes the unpublished argument to a proved result.

Thus a verified lower bound is known, but it does not settle the page’s stronger question about a fixed $c>0$ and infinitely many $n$. The maintained page continues to label the problem open. The order of growth in the broad sense is likewise not supplied by the 1936 lower bound alone.

## What remains

A useful bounded successor task is a source-normalization certificate for $k=3$. Let $p_j(n)$ count ordered j-tuples of positive integers whose cubes sum to $n$, and let $f_3(n)$ count ordered triples of nonnegative integers as in Erdős’s paper. For every $n>0$, partition a triple by its number of zero coordinates. The exact identity to prove is

$$
f_3(n)=p_3(n)+3p_2(n)+3\,\mathbf 1_{\{n=x^3\text{ for some }x>0\}}.
$$

The input is the scanned 1936 definition and the pinned or explicitly labelled upstream formal definition. The target is a written bijection for the three cases, together with exact integer enumeration for $1\leq n\leq 1000$ as a regression check. Acceptance requires the symbolic partition proof, the script, and a statement of which convention the resulting lower-bound interface uses. A counterexample to the identity, or an ambiguity about whether representations are ordered, falsifies the package. Stop after this bridge is checked; do not infer the open asymptotic question from the finite table and do not launch a Lean build.
