## The question in plain language

Can one name a *very sparse* set of cycle lengths that must nevertheless occur in any sufficiently dense graph? Erdős #72 asks for a set $A\subset\mathbb N$ of natural density zero and a constant $c>0$ such that every sufficiently large graph with average degree at least $c$ contains a cycle whose length lies in $A$.

A set has density zero when the fraction of integers up to $N$ belonging to it tends to zero. This makes the question striking: although almost every positive integer is excluded, one of the few permitted lengths is unavoidable.

## A small example

Consider the powers of two $A=\{4,8,16,\ldots\}$. At most $\lfloor\log_2N\rfloor$ elements of $A$ are at most $N$. Hence

$$
\frac{|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|}{N}\le\frac{\log_2N}{N}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The complete bipartite graph $K_{4,4}$ has cycles of lengths $4$ and $8$, both in $A$. This is an illustration only; finite examples do not prove a statement about *every* graph above a density threshold.

## What is known

The answer is **YES**. Verstraëte proved that some density-zero set is unavoidable, using a nonconstructive existence argument. More strongly, Liu and Montgomery showed that even the explicit powers-of-two sequence works. Their [Corollary 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.15802v2) states that an increasing sequence of even integers $\sigma_i$ satisfying

$$
\sigma_{i+1}\le\exp(\sigma_i^{1/10})
$$

at every index is unavoidable once the average degree reaches a fixed threshold depending on the first term and a universal constant. A sufficiently late *tail* of the powers of two satisfies this inequality; the first few powers do **not**. For $\sigma_i=2^{J+i}$ and sufficiently large $J$, the condition is fulfilled because $2\sigma_i\le\exp(\sigma_i^{1/10})$ eventually. The tail is still density zero and supplies one fixed $A$ and one fixed $c$.

The deep cycle-length theorem supplies the universal graph implication, not the elementary density calculation.

## What remains

An explicit choice is $J=60$: $2^{6}=64>61\log2$, equivalently $2\cdot2^{60}<\exp((2^{60})^{1/10})$. Moreover, $2^{j/10}/(j+1)$ increases for integers $j\ge60$, so every later power satisfies the same inequality. Then instantiate the published corollary with the tail and prove the density-zero bound. Do not silently apply the corollary to the sequence starting at $4$, where its quantified hypothesis fails.
