## The question in plain language

The Hofstadter consecutive-sum sequence starts with $a_1=1$, $a_2=2$. For each $k\geq3$, its next term is the smallest integer **strictly greater than the preceding term** that can be expressed as a sum of at least two consecutive earlier terms of the selected sequence. This is a greedy construction: earlier selected numbers determine which larger numbers may be chosen.

Erdős Problem 423 asks how the sequence grows as $k$ tends to infinity. Does it eventually select almost every positive integer? How many integers are never selected? How large is $a_k-k$? These are distinct questions: even infinitely many omitted integers could form a set of density zero.

The word *consecutive* refers to adjacent indices in the selected sequence, not necessarily adjacent positive integers. A permitted block must have at least two members, and only already selected terms are available at each step.

## A small example

Start with $1,2$. Their sum gives $a_3=3$. Among block sums of $1,2,3$, the smallest one above $3$ is $2+3=5$, so $a_4=5$. The integer $4$ is skipped. The next entries include $6=1+2+3$, $8=3+5$, $10=2+3+5$, and $11=5+6$. Direct exact-integer enumeration gives

$$
1,2,3,5,6,8,10,11,14,16,17,18,19,21,22,24,25,29,30,32,\ldots.
$$

The result agrees with [OEIS A005243](https://oeis.org/A005243). Some small numbers absent from the displayed prefix are $4,7,9,12,13,15,20$. An observed finite omission cannot by itself prove that infinitely many omissions occur.

There is an exact counting identity. Put $b_k=a_k-k$. The first $k$ selected terms are precisely the $k$ selected positive integers not exceeding $a_k$. Hence

$$
b_k=\#\bigl([1,a_k]\cap\mathbb N\setminus\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}\bigr).
$$

The excess $b_k$ therefore counts the omitted integers up to $a_k$. Since $a_{k+1}\geq a_k+1$, we have $b_{k+1}\geq b_k$. This proves monotonicity but **not** unboundedness. A nondecreasing integer sequence could become constant.

## What is known

Erdős recorded Hofstadter's question in *Problems and results on combinatorial number theory, III* (1977), printed p. 71, and again with Graham in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed p. 83 of the [scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). Hofstadter was inspired by a related question of Ulam.

Quanyu Tang's 2026 preprint, [*The Hofstadter consecutive-sum sequence omits infinitely many positive integers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09939), makes a substantial advance. **Theorem 1.3** proves that $b_k=a_k-k$ is nondecreasing and unbounded; consequently infinitely many positive integers are never selected. The maintained [Erdős Problems #423](https://www.erdosproblems.com/423) entry credits Matthew Bolan with an independent proof of this qualitative assertion.

Tang's Theorem 1.4 gives a quantitative lower bound of order $\log\log k$ for the excess, and Theorem 1.5 provides a polynomial upper bound on $a_k$. The maintained problem page reports a stronger upper bound obtained using later estimates for difference sets of finite convex sets:

$$
a_k\ll k^{688/413+o(1)}.
$$

This is not an asymptotic equality and not proof that $a_k/k$ tends to one. The maintained status remains **OPEN** for the original question of precise asymptotic growth. Infinitely many omissions have been proved; complete asymptotics have not been settled.

## What remains

A bounded successor can calculate the first 100 selected terms in two independent ways. One algorithm enumerates all sums of at least two consecutive previously selected terms before each greedy choice. Another maintains an incrementally updated set of eligible block sums. Both should reproduce the same terms, and the identity $b_k=a_k-k$ should match a direct count of omitted positive integers to $a_k$ at every stage.

A source audit should transcribe the precise hypotheses and conclusions of Tang's Theorems 1.3–1.5, and identify the convex-set difference estimate behind the maintained improved exponent. The successor is accepted if both integer algorithms agree, each selected number has a valid block witness, and the published lower and upper bounds remain distinct from the unsolved exact asymptotics. A mismatched greedy term, a false block sum, or a claim that infinitely many omissions means positive-density omissions falsifies the proposed result. Stop at this finite fixture and source-concordance boundary.
