## The question in plain language

For an integer parameter satisfying $k\geq 2$, look at tuples of length $k$ made from nonnegative integers whose factorials multiply to a divisor of $n!$. The quantity

$$
g_k(n)=\max\{a_1+\cdots+a_k-n:a_1!\cdots a_k!\mid n!\}
$$

measures how much larger the sum of the factorial indices can be than $n$, while the factorial divisibility constraint still holds. Erdős Problem 400 asks two typical-size questions. First, does the sum of $g_k(n)$ up to $x$ have an asymptotic constant $c_k$?

$$
\sum_{n\leq x}g_k(n)\sim c_kx\log x.
$$

Second, for almost every $n<x$, does $g_k(n)$ itself behave like $c_k\log x$, up to a smaller-order error? The same symbol $c_k$ is used in both questions in the original wording. The problem is recorded in Erdős and Graham’s 1980 monograph, p. 77, and in the maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/400).

The quantifiers matter: $k$ is fixed when discussing the growth in $n$, and the issue packet asks that this be checked rather than inferred from a numerical experiment. The source says “integers”; factorials conventionally force nonnegative indices, but the exact ambient domain should remain explicit.

## A small example

Take $n=5$ and $k=2$. Since $5!=120$ and $6!>120$, any admissible index is in $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$. Exact enumeration gives the feasible pairs with nonnegative excess as

$$
(0,5),(1,4),(1,5),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1),(5,0),(5,1).
$$

Their excesses $a+b-5$ are respectively $0,0,1,0,0,0,0,1$. For example, $(1,5)$ and $(5,1)$ work because $1!5!=120\mid120$, while $(4,2)$ fails because $4!2!=48$ does not divide $120$. Thus the exact finite value is $g_2(5)=1$.

This calculation is reproducible using only integer factorials and divisibility; it checks the definition and the boundary convention, not the asymptotics. A finite maximum cannot establish the existence of $c_k$, a density-one statement, or even the eventual order of magnitude.

## What is known

The maintained page attributes the elementary pointwise estimate $g_k(n)\ll_k\log n$ to Erdős and Graham and says that the best possible constant was unknown in the cited source. The original reference is [Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Old_and_New_Problems_and_Results_in_Comb.html?id=E-sZAQAAIAAJ), Monographie 28, 1980, p. 77; the live page identifies it as `[ErGr80,p.77]`. A bibliographic record and the author’s publication list are also available from [Ron Graham’s publications page](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/).

A relevant primary result is Carl Pomerance’s [“Remarks on the Middle Binomial Coefficient”](https://math.colgate.edu/~integers/aa47/aa47.pdf), *Integers* 26 (2026), article A47, Theorem 1 on p. 1. For every fixed $\eta<1/\log 4$, it proves that for a density-one set of $m$, every positive integer $r\leq\eta\log m$ satisfies

$$
\frac{(m+r)!}{m!}\mid\binom{2m}{m}.
$$

Consequently, for the Problem 400 quantity, choose $n=2m$, indices $m+r$, $m$, and $k-2$ copies of $1$. This gives $g_k(2m)\geq r+k-2$ on that density-one set. It is a real lower-bound interface, but it does not identify the asymptotic constant or prove either question. The PDF retrieved for this audit has SHA-256 `b28e39128d5fe30f0c54bdde172ecff621ac74eb97a6b420186c1077f7be7724`.

The live discussion also contains a 2026 preprint report by Eric Li claiming stronger bounds. It is useful evidence to locate, but this article does not independently certify that report or any claimed resolution.

The distinction between a lower bound and an asymptotic is especially important here. Pomerance’s theorem supplies many admissible tuples and therefore forces $g_k(n)$ to be large on a controlled set of inputs. It says nothing by itself about whether other admissible tuples are larger, whether the normalized values concentrate, or whether the summatory ratio converges. Even a sharp pointwise upper bound would leave the existence and value of a limiting average unresolved unless the two bounds meet at the right scale.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is to reconcile the exact source semantics before attempting a theorem: positive versus nonnegative indices, ordinary integer subtraction versus truncated natural subtraction, and the formal `∀ᵉ (k ≥ 2)` binder versus the prose “for any $k\geq2$”. Then record Pomerance’s divisibility theorem as a conditional lower-bound lemma for fixed $k$, and separately test the finite values of $g_2(n)$ by exact prime-valuation arithmetic. Acceptance should require the stated hypotheses, exact divisibility, and a falsifiable comparison with the formal `g`; a failed comparison stops the task and opens a semantic correction, not a claim about the original problem.
