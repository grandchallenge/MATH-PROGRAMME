## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 355 asks whether one can choose an infinite, rapidly growing sequence of positive integers

$$
a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots
$$

with a fixed growth factor greater than $1$, while still making finite sums of distinct reciprocals unusually complete. More precisely, if there is a number $\lambda>1$ such that $a_{n+1}/a_n\geq\lambda$ for every $n$, does there exist a non-empty open interval $(u,v)$ such that every rational number in that interval is a sum of finitely many distinct terms $1/a_n$?

This is a question about Egyptian fractions: sums such as $1/4+1/6+1/12$. The tension is that lacunarity makes the available reciprocals sparse, whereas representing every rational in an interval requires a great deal of arithmetic flexibility. The older formulation in Bleicher and Erdős uses a strict inequality $a_{n+1}/a_n>c>1$. For an existence question this agrees with the displayed $\geq\lambda$ normalization: one may move slightly below or above the chosen constant without changing the underlying existential issue.

The original issue for this article is [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1625](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1625), assignment `ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0355-S01`. The issue’s source packet identifies the problem as proved, but that catalogue metadata is evidence to audit, not GCL mathematical certification.

## A small example

The primary paper gives an explicit beginning of a possible construction with lacunarity constant $3/2$:

$$
1,2,4,6,12,24,48,72,120,180,360,720,\ldots
$$

The first five terms provide a useful exact finite check. Put all fractions over the common denominator $12$. The reciprocals correspond to integer weights

$$
12,6,3,2,1.
$$

Taking subset sums of the same weights in increasing order gives the following exact interval calculation. The weight $1$ gives all integers from $0$ through $1$; adding $2$ gives $0$ through $3$; adding $3$ gives $0$ through $6$; adding $6$ gives $0$ through $12$; and adding $12$ gives $0$ through $24$. At each step the new weight is at most one more than the previous maximum, so there is no gap. Thus the five reciprocals represent exactly the grid points $k/12$ for $0\leq k\leq24$. For example, $7/12=1/2+1/12$.

This is reproducible exact integer arithmetic, not a floating-point experiment. It verifies the finite-prefix claim recorded by the authors: the first five terms cover the fractions in $[0,2]$ whose reduced denominators divide $12$. It does not prove that all rationals in $[0,2]$ are represented, and it says nothing by itself about the infinitely continued sequence. The infinite argument must arrange divisibility milestones and enough intervening terms at every stage.

## What is known

The primary resolution is Wouter van Doorn and Vjekoslav Kovač, “Lacunary sequences whose reciprocal sums represent all rational numbers in an interval,” arXiv:2509.24971v3, especially Theorem 1 and Proposition 8; the work is also published in *Acta Arithmetica* 223 (2026), 275–295, DOI [10.4064/aa251001-13-1](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa251001-13-1). The arXiv record identifies v3 as the 3 December 2025 revision, while the online-first publication supplies the later journal record.

Theorem 1(a) proves the stronger statement that for every $\lambda\in(1,2)$ there is a sequence of positive integers with lacunarity constant $\lambda$ whose finite reciprocal sums contain every rational in $[0,2]$. The construction is organized in blocks. Distinguished block endpoints are divisible by all previous terms and, over time, by every positive integer. A finite-sum filling lemma then turns the controlled ratio bound into complete rational coverage at each compatible denominator.

The same theorem says more: the sequence may have consecutive ratios tending to $2$, and every rational in $(0,2]$ can have infinitely many finite representations. The boundary is sharp in the relevant sense. Theorem 1(c), proved through the paper’s necessary-condition argument, says that no sequence with lacunarity constant $2$ has finite reciprocal sums containing all rationals from a non-empty open interval. Thus the answer to the original existence question is yes, with the admissible uniform constants below $2$, while the exact endpoint $2$ is excluded.

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/355) reports this same conclusion and says it was last edited 18 November 2025. Its displayed references include Bleicher–Erdős and Erdős–Graham, and it links the van Doorn–Kovač work. The page is a useful maintained index, but the primary paper is the evidence for the theorem’s scope and proof.

## What remains

The substantive theorem is externally resolved, so the useful bounded follow-up is semantic and documentary rather than a search for a new solution. A reviewer can compare the paper’s Theorem 1(a) and Proposition 8 line by line with the pinned Lean statement: index origin, positivity, strict increase, the ratio inequality, whether the interval is open or closed, whether $0$ is included, and whether finite subsets are represented through indices or values. The endpoint distinction matters: the paper proves coverage of $[0,2]\cap\mathbb{Q}$ for every fixed $\lambda<2$, but separately excludes sequences with lacunarity constant $2$.

A second bounded task is to transcribe the paper’s first four explicit construction blocks and verify the finite-prefix denominator claims with exact rational arithmetic, retaining the script and its input sequence as evidence. Acceptance should require matching the published list and all claimed finite grid points; falsification should be an omitted point, an incorrect ratio bound, or a mismatch in the divisibility milestone. Stop once the stated finite claims and the formal statement comparison are recorded; do not infer a formal proof from successful finite checks.
