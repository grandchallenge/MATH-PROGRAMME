## The question in plain language

Suppose we place $n$ distinct points in the plane, with one restriction: **from any point, no three of the other points may be equally distant**. Erdős Problem [#655](https://www.erdosproblems.com/655) asks whether this restriction forces the point set to determine at least

$$
(1+c)\frac n2
$$

different *pairwise distances*, for some fixed constant $c>0$ whenever $n$ is sufficiently large. Here a distance is counted once even if many pairs attain it. The statement has the quantifier order $\exists c>0\,\exists N\,\forall n\geq N\,\forall X$ satisfying the restriction.

**The literal displayed assertion is false.** A regular polygon has very few distinct distances even though each individual distance from a vertex occurs at most twice. The maintained page credits this observation to Zach Hunter, but, as of the 13 October 2026 inspection of its publicly indexed text, still displays the catalogue status OPEN. The original intended hypotheses require separate source-fidelity treatment.

## A small example

Take the seven vertices of a regular heptagon on the unit circle:

$$
X=\{(\cos(2\pi j/7),\sin(2\pi j/7)):j=0,\ldots,6\}.
$$

The chord length between vertices separated by $k$ steps is

$$
d_k=2\sin\left(\frac{\pi\min(k,7-k)}7\right).
$$

Consequently the entire seven-point set determines precisely **three** nonzero distances, namely $d_1,d_2,d_3$. At any chosen vertex, each of those three distances occurs at exactly two other vertices, so no circle centred at that vertex passes through three other members of $X$.

For comparison, the proposed bound would require $3\geq(1+c)7/2>7/2$, impossible. A seven-point example alone would not refute an *eventual* assertion; the crucial point is that the same construction works for every $n\geq3$.

## What is known

Let

$$
X_n=\{(\cos(2\pi j/n),\sin(2\pi j/n)):0\leq j<n\}.
$$

For any two distinct vertices, their Euclidean distance depends only on their cyclic separation $k$, with $1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. The distance is

$$
2\sin\left(\frac{\pi k}{n}\right).
$$

These $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ values are distinct because $\sin t$ is strictly increasing on $[0,\pi/2]$. Each vertex has at most two other vertices at any one distance (one in each direction around the polygon; for even $n$, the antipodal vertex occurs only once). Thus **all** $X_n$ obey the stated restriction while determining exactly $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ nonzero distances. For every fixed $c>0$,

$$
D(X_n)=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor
\leq\frac n2<(1+c)\frac n2.
$$

The asserted universal bound therefore fails for arbitrarily large $n$, indeed for every $n\geq3$. This is an elementary, complete argument for the *literal* formulation, attributed to Hunter rather than to a new GCL discovery.

The maintained [Erdős problem page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/655) both displays OPEN and describes the counterexample. It suggests that the intended question may have assumed **general position** (for example, no three collinear and no four concyclic). That stronger version excludes regular $n$ gons for $n\geq4$, and this counterexample does not decide it. The primary original reference is Erdős's 1997 article [Er97e]; its exact passage and historical hypotheses have not been independently inspected in this cohort.
