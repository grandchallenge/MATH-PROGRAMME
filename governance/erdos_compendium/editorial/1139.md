## The question in plain language

How large can the gaps become between integers with **at most two prime factors**?

[Erdős Problem #1139](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1139) writes the eligible integers in increasing order as $u_1<u_2<\cdots$ and asks whether

$$
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{u_{k+1}-u_k}{\log k}=\infty.
$$

The denominator uses the **sequence index $k$**, not the integer $u_k$.

The source phrase “at most two prime factors” requires clarification: does it count factors with multiplicity or only distinct prime divisors?

## A small example

In number theory, $\Omega(n)$ counts factors **with multiplicity**, while $\omega(n)$ counts distinct prime divisors.

For example, $8=2^3$ has

$$
\Omega(8)=3,\qquad\omega(8)=1.
$$

Thus $8$ is excluded by $\Omega(n)\le2$ but included by $\omega(n)\le2$.

Exact factorization through 10,000 gives **3,855** integers with at most two prime factors counted with multiplicity and **5,378** with at most two distinct prime divisors. For the first interpretation the largest observed neighboring gap is $10$. These differences matter for the statement itself, and no finite search establishes the infinite limsup.

## What is known

The maintained catalogue, citing Vardi (1999), lists the conjecture as **open**. Comments note the lack of a source-verified nontrivial lower bound of the kind needed.

The semiprime sequence, usually associated with $\Omega(n)=2$, has its own sieve-theoretic gap behavior. Including all prime powers through the weaker $\omega(n)\le2$ condition would produce a different problem.
