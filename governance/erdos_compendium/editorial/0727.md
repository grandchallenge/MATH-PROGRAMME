## The question in plain language

For every FIXED integer k≥2, are there infinitely many n such that ((n+k)!)² divides (2n)!? The maintained #727 says OPEN EVEN for k=2; the case k=1 is classically proved. The factorials have to satisfy n≥k for the quotient to be nontrivial; finite checks never prove infinitely many n.

## A small example

Use Legendre's formula v_p(m!)=Σ_{j≥1}floor(m/p^j). The divisibility condition is equivalent for every prime p≤2n to Δ_p(n,k)=v_p((2n)!)-2v_p((n+k)!)≥0. For k=2,n=3 one has (5!)²=14400, (6)!=720, so divisibility FAILS. For k=2,n=2 it also fails. One cannot infer permanent failure from these fixtures.

## What is known

Balakran [Ba29] proved infinitely many n for k=1, equivalent to (n+1)² dividing central binomial coefficient C(2n,n). Erdős–Graham–Ruzsa–Straus [EGRS75] proved a weaker crossed condition (n+k)!(n+1)!|(2n)! infinitely often for each fixed k, with a logarithmic k range in their original statement. The GCL-pinned FormalConjectures #727 includes main, k=2, k=1, and crossed-condition statements; all theorem bodies remain sorry despite a known classical k=1 result.
