## The question in plain language

A graph can contain cycles of many lengths. Erdős and Hajnal asked how large the sum of the reciprocals of those **different lengths** must be when a graph has many edges. They also asked which graphs make that sum as small as possible.

If a graph has $n$ vertices and $kn$ edges, let $\mathcal C(G)$ be its set of distinct simple-cycle lengths. The quantity is

$$
R(G)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal C(G)}\frac1\ell.
$$

## A small example

The balanced complete bipartite graph $K_{m,m}$ has $2m$ vertices, $m^2$ edges, and every even cycle length $4,6,\ldots,2m$, with no odd cycles. Therefore

$$
R(K_{m,m})=\frac14+\frac16+\cdots+\frac1{2m}
=\frac12(H_m-1)
=\frac12\log m+O(1).
$$

Here $H_m=1+1/2+\cdots+1/m$. Since $k=m/2$, this example has the leading growth $(1/2)\log k$. Counting cycles rather than **distinct cycle lengths** would give a different, incorrect sum.

## What is known

The lower-bound question is solved. Gyárfás, Komlós and Szemerédi proved logarithmic growth. Liu and Montgomery sharpened the bound: if $d$ is the average degree, then

$$
R(G)\ge\left(\frac12-o_d(1)\right)\log d.
$$

This is their [Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.15802v2), derived from a theorem guaranteeing long intervals of even cycle lengths in sufficiently dense graphs. As $d=2k$, it matches the leading constant exhibited by balanced complete bipartite graphs.

The exact extremal characterization is a different question. The maintained catalogue describes a proposed **minimum** at complete bipartite graphs, but its note about forthcoming work says **maximum**. That apparent conflict must be checked against the underlying primary source and precise constraints; it cannot be silently resolved by editorial inference.
