## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #380 asks how often a product of consecutive integers has an unusually repeated largest prime factor. For positive integers $u\le v$, write

$$
Q(u,v)=\prod_{u\le m\le v}m.
$$

Call $[u,v]$ bad when the largest prime factor of $Q(u,v)$ occurs with exponent at least two. Equivalently, if $L(u,v)$ is that largest prime factor, then $L(u,v)^2\mid Q(u,v)$. Let $B_{\le x}$ be the set of integers $n\le x$ that lie in at least one bad interval. For $n\ge2$, let $P(n)$ be the largest prime factor of $n$; the comparison set is

$$
S_{\le x}=\{n\le x:P(n)^2\mid n\}.
$$

The problem asks whether, as $x$ tends to infinity, the two counts are asymptotic:

$$
\#B_{\le x}\sim\#S_{\le x}.
$$

The interval is inclusive. The original wording is naturally normalized by putting $N=u-1$ and $H=v-u+1$, so Tao's notation $\{N+1,\ldots,N+H\}$ describes exactly the same objects. The singleton interval is allowed; the product $1$ is not treated as bad because it has no largest prime factor.

## A small example

Take the interval $[24,25]$. Exact multiplication and factorization give

$$
24\cdot25=600=2^3\cdot3\cdot5^2.
$$

The largest prime factor is $5$, and it occurs twice, so this interval is bad. Consequently $24$ and $25$ belong to $B_{\le x}$ for every $x\ge25$. The interval $[8,9]$ is even stronger:

$$
8\cdot9=72=2^3\cdot3^2,
$$

so every prime occurring in the product occurs at least twice; this is called very bad in the later literature.

The following finite check was run with exact integer arithmetic: factor every product $Q(u,v)$ for $1\le u\le v\le25$, discard the empty-factorization case $Q(1,1)=1$, and union the elements of the bad intervals. It returns

$$
\{4,8,9,16,18,24,25\}.
$$

This is a check on intervals wholly contained in $[1,25]$, not the complete value of $B_{\le25}$, because an interval containing a small integer could extend beyond $25$. It illustrates the distinction between a finite witness and an asymptotic assertion: finding bad intervals at small scales neither proves the limit nor checks Tao's analytic estimates.

## What is known

The maintained Erdős Problems page records that Erdős and Graham posed the question in *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), page 73. It reports that Terence Tao resolved it in *Products of consecutive integers with unusual anatomy*, arXiv:2603.27990v3 (2026).

Tao uses $\mathcal B$ for the set of integers lying in at least one bad interval and $\mathcal B^1$ for the simpler set of integers divisible by the square of their own largest prime factor. Theorem 1.7, on page 6 of the version dated 26 September 2026, proves that the contribution from $\mathcal B\setminus\mathcal B^1$ is smaller than $\mathcal B^1$ by a factor $(\log x)^{1-o(1)}$. In particular,

$$
\#(\mathcal B\cap[1,x])\sim\#(\mathcal B^1\cap[1,x]).
$$

That is the registered question after the notation is aligned. The result is an external published-preprint claim and source report; this article does not independently reproduce its 42-page proof. Tao's paper also treats the related very-bad intervals, but those are additional statements rather than a change to Problem #380.

## What remains

For the mathematics, the registered yes-or-no question has an affirmative answer in Tao's source. The bounded remaining work is documentary and formal-interface work: preserve the inclusive interval convention, the largest-prime-factor condition, the $n\ge2$ convention on the comparison set, and the limit quantifier when translating the result. A future formalization would also need definitions for finite products, prime-factor exponents, and asymptotic counting functions before it could express Theorem 1.7.

A useful small successor is to formalize or independently verify the elementary family $[p^2-1,p^2]$ for odd primes $p$, then record exactly which part is a finite lemma and which part still depends on Tao's analytic proof. That task can improve the interface without pretending to replay the asymptotic theorem.
