## The question in plain language

Problem 312 asks how closely a finite collection of unit fractions can approach $1$ from below. The collection is a multiset: a denominator may occur more than once. If $A$ is such a multiset, write

$$
R(A)=\sum_{a\in A}\frac{1}{a}.
$$

For a submultiset $S$ whose reciprocal sum is at most $1$, define the gap to be

$$
\varepsilon(A)=1-\max\left\{R(S):S\subseteq A,\ R(S)\leq 1\right\}.
$$

The problem asks whether there is an absolute constant $c>0$ such that, for every $K>1$, every sufficiently large finite multiset of positive integers with $R(A)>K$ has a submultiset satisfying

$$
1-e^{-cK}<R(S)\leq 1.
$$

The phrase “sufficiently large” is part of the quantifier issue. The pinned Lean statement makes the threshold $N_0$ depend on $K$, then requires the assertion for every indexed multiset of length at least $N_0$. It also models a multiset by a function `a : Fin n → ℕ`, so it permits zero entries unless positivity is added separately. In Lean, the inverse of zero is zero; that is a semantic difference from the maintained problem’s positive-integer wording.

The original issue is [MATH-PROGRAMME #1582](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1582). Its frozen catalogue packet identifies the question as open, in number theory and unit fractions, and records a statement-formalized but not formally solved entry.

## A small example

Take the multiset $A=\{2,3,3\}$. Its total reciprocal mass is

$$
R(A)=\frac12+\frac13+\frac13=\frac76>1.
$$

Use denominator $6$ as a common denominator. The three available contributions are the integer weights $3,2,2$. Enumerating all submultisets gives numerator sums

$$
0,2,3,4,5,7.
$$

The largest numerator not exceeding $6$ is $5$, so the largest reciprocal subsum below $1$ is $5/6$ and the exact gap is $\varepsilon(A)=1/6$. There is no exact representation of $1$ in this example because $6$ is absent from the displayed set of attainable numerators.

This is an exact finite calculation, not a numerical approximation. It illustrates the optimization problem and the role of repeated denominators. It does not test the asymptotic assertion: the example has no “sufficiently large” threshold, and without a value of the conjectural constant $c$ it cannot decide the exponential inequality. A short integer-subset enumeration reproduces the numerator set exactly, so no floating-point or unverified computational output is being used here.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/312), last edited 20 January 2026, labels the problem open and says that Erdős and Graham knew the weaker estimate with $e^{-cK}$ replaced by $c/K^2$. Its displayed source key is `[ErGr80]`, referring to P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), the monograph listed as 128 pages in the catalogue. The exact page in that monograph was not independently read during this bounded audit, so the original page-level attribution remains a source boundary rather than a certified citation.

There is now a relevant primary research document: Samuel Korsky, [“Approximating 1 by Sums of Unit Fractions,” arXiv:2607.04157v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04157v2), submitted 5 July 2026 and revised 8 October 2026. Its introduction defines the same gap and states Theorem 1.1: for absolute constants $c>0$ and $K_0$, every finite multiset of positive integers with $R(A)>K\geq K_0$ satisfies

$$
\varepsilon(A)\leq \exp\bigl(-c\sqrt{K\log K}\bigr).
$$

The paper describes this as an improvement on the Erdős–Graham polynomial estimate. This is materially stronger than $K^{-2}$ for large $K$, but it is weaker than the requested $e^{-cK}$ bound. Thus it is progress toward Problem 312, not a resolution of its stated target. The associated forum post from 24 June 2026 is an unverified report of the same stretched-exponential direction and explicitly says that parts of its Fourier details warrant inspection; it is not used as proof evidence.
