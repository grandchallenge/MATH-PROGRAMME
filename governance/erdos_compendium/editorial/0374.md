## The question in plain language

Problem 374 concerns when a product of different factorials becomes a square. For a positive integer $m$, define $F(m)$ to be the smallest number of distinct factorials needed in a product whose largest factorial is $m!$ and whose product is a perfect square. More explicitly, we look for integers

$$
1 \leq a_1 < a_2 < \cdots < a_k=m
$$

such that $a_1!a_2!\cdots a_k!$ is a square. If no such product exists, $F(m)$ is left undefined for the counting question. Let $D_k$ be the integers with $F(m)=k$. The problem asks how quickly the number of elements of $D_k$ up to $n$ grows, for $3\leq k\leq 6$. One highlighted case is whether six-factor examples have positive density: roughly, whether a fixed positive proportion of integers require exactly six factorials.

This is a question about asymptotic size, not about finding one especially large example. It also matters that “exactly $k$” excludes integers already represented with fewer factors.

## A small example

For $m=6$, direct integer arithmetic gives

$$
3!\,5!\,6!=518400=720^2.
$$

The five possible two-factor products with largest argument 6 are $1!6!=720$, $2!6!=1440$, $3!6!=4320$, $4!6!=17280$, and $5!6!=86400$; none is a square. Thus this finite check verifies $F(6)=3$. It illustrates both parts of the definition: producing a square is not enough; minimality must also be checked.

A second exact identity explains why four-factor examples are plentiful. If $m=v^2u$ with $v\geq2$ and $u\geq2$, then

$$
m!(m-1)!u!(u-1)!=\bigl(vu(m-1)!(u-1)!\bigr)^2.
$$

For instance, with $m=12$, $v=2$, and $u=3$, both sides equal $229442532802560000$. The four arguments are distinct and ordered. This proves a useful finite-family inclusion, but it does not determine whether the four-factor set has a limiting density, and it says nothing by itself about six factors.

## What is known

The frozen catalogue packet records Problem 374 as open and unformalized at the pinned `teorth/erdosproblems` commit `536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89`; the entry’s Git blob is `4e60263a80af6207aca494d2e1388e44fa372889`, with last update 2025-08-31. Its four listed OEIS links are metadata, not proofs. The maintained page at [erdosproblems.com/374](https://www.erdosproblems.com/374) likewise presents the open formulation and summarizes earlier facts: primes have no representation, $D_2$ is the square set, the six-factor set begins at 527, and no $D_k$ exists for $k>6$.

A materially newer primary source is Fedir Yudin’s [*Square products of factorials and a conjecture of Erdős and Graham*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01899), arXiv:2610.01899v1, submitted 1 October 2026. Theorem 1.1 on p. 1 states $D_3(X)=\kappa_3\sqrt X+O_\varepsilon(X^{2/5+\varepsilon})$, with explicit $\kappa_3=2.709751\ldots$. Theorem 1.2 on p. 1 gives a positive-proportion lower bound for exactly five and exactly six factors. Combining these results with classical identities, Corollary 1.3 on p. 2 states $D_3(X)\sim\kappa_3\sqrt X$ and $D_4(X)\asymp D_5(X)\asymp D_6(X)\asymp X$, while $D_2(X)$ has square-root order and $D_k(X)=0$ for $k\geq7$.

The same paper is careful about scope: it says the orders of growth asked for in Problem 374 are settled, but asks separately whether $D_k(X)/X$ has a limit for $k=4,5,6$. Thus “positive density in the order-of-growth sense” and “a limiting density exists” are different claims. The older website status and the newer primary preprint have not been reconciled in the frozen ledger.
