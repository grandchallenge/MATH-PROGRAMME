## The question in plain language

Choose a strictly increasing sequence of positive moduli $m_0<m_1<\cdots$ and a residue $a_i$ for each modulus. Start with the nonnegative integers and, for each $i$, remove those $x$ that have **reached the threshold** $x\ge m_i$ and satisfy $x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}$. [Erdős Problem #25](https://www.erdosproblems.com/25) asks whether the surviving set necessarily has a **logarithmic density**.

Logarithmic density weights each positive survivor $x$ by $1/x$ and normalizes by the harmonic growth $\log X$. It is a different existence question from ordinary natural density.

## A small example

Use just the first two moduli $2$ and $3$, with excluded residues $0$ and $1$. Once both thresholds have been passed, the survivors are precisely integers congruent to 3 or 5 modulo 6. Their ordinary and logarithmic densities are both $2/6=1/3$.

The initial point $x=1$ nevertheless survives both restrictions, since $1<2$ and $1<3$, even though its residue modulo 3 is 1. The threshold is *part of the rule*. At $x=2$, the first exclusion already applies.

## What is known

Any **finite truncation** of the congruence list is eventually periodic with period equal to the least common multiple of its moduli. Consequently each finite truncation has both ordinary and logarithmic density; the finite initial threshold exceptions have no effect on either limiting density.

It does not follow that imposing **infinitely many** conditions yields a logarithmic density. As $X$ grows, new constraints become active; exchanging the limit over the number of constraints with the limit in $X$ requires uniform control. Overlapping moduli also defeat an unqualified independence-product argument.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1294](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1294#issuecomment-6104151067) proves an exact local-finiteness observation: because the increasing positive integers satisfy $m_i\ge i+1$, checking membership at any finite $x\le X$ only requires indices smaller than $X$. It also checks the finite-periodic truncation theorem and gives examples where naive products of survival fractions fail. The source record remains OPEN; the formal provider has an unfilled proof.

## What remains

Proving the global result needs a uniform bound on the logarithmic contribution of newly activated exclusions, or a different argument handling infinitely many dependent moduli. Neither computability of finite membership nor existence of densities for all finite truncations is enough.
