## The question in plain language

A set of positive integers may contain an unusual multiplicative pattern: three or more distinct numbers can have **the same least common multiple in every pair**. For example, the numbers $2,3,6$ satisfy

$$
\mathrm{lcm}(2,3)=\mathrm{lcm}(2,6)=\mathrm{lcm}(3,6)=6.
$$

For a fixed $k\ge3$, call a subset $A$ of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ *free of LCM patterns of order $k$* when it contains no $k$ distinct numbers with this property. [Erdős Problem #856](https://www.erdosproblems.com/856) asks how large the reciprocal sum

$$
f_k(N)=\max_{A\ \mathrm{LCM}\text{-}k\text{-free}}\ \sum_{a\in A}\frac1a
$$

can be as $N$ grows. The reciprocal sum gives greater weight to small integers. This differs from simply counting how many integers can be selected.

## A small example

For $k=3$ and $N=5$, every subset is permitted: there are no three distinct numbers in $\{1,\ldots,5\}$ with identical pairwise least common multiples. The maximum is therefore the full harmonic sum

$$
f_3(5)=1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15=\frac{137}{60}.
$$

At $N=6$, the triple $2,3,6$ becomes forbidden. Omitting $6$ loses less reciprocal weight than omitting $2$ or $3$, so $f_3(6)=137/60$ still. Exhaustive rational arithmetic gives $f_3(7)=1019/420$ and $f_3(12)=76319/27720$. The [finite-check script](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/blob/editorial/ec-bulk-006-200/governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_856_857.py) in the cohort's research evidence enumerates all candidate subsets and forbidden triples; these values are finite checks, not asymptotic estimates.

## What is known

Erdős gave an elegant double-counting argument in his [1970 paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1970-21.pdf), printed pages 127–128. For any fixed $t$, suppose there were $k$ representations

$$
t=a_1p_1=\cdots=a_kp_k,
$$

where each $a_i\in A$ and each $p_i$ is prime. The primes must be distinct because the $a_i$ are distinct. For different primes $p_i,p_j$ dividing $t$, the least common multiple of $t/p_i$ and $t/p_j$ is exactly $t$. Such representations would therefore create a forbidden tuple of order $k$. There can be at most $k-1$ representations for each $t$.

Sum the reciprocal weights of all pairs $(a,p)$ with $a\in A$ and $p\le N$ prime. Counting first by $a$ and then by $t=ap$ gives

$$
\left(\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a\right)
\left(\sum_{p\le N}\frac1p\right)
\le(k-1)\sum_{t\le N^2}\frac1t.
$$

The sum of prime reciprocals grows like $\log\log N$, whereas the rightmost harmonic sum grows like $\log N$. Consequently

$$
f_k(N)=O_k\!\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right).
$$

A 2025 paper by Quanyu Tang and Shengtong Zhang, [*Harmonic LCM patterns and sunflower-free capacity*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20055), obtains substantially sharper bounds involving powers of $\log N$ upper and lower bounds. Its exponents are connected to how large a family of subsets can be without a sunflower of order $k$, the subject of [Problem #857](https://www.erdosproblems.com/857). The bounds do not, by themselves, supply an exact asymptotic formula.

## What remains

The first bounded mathematical task is to formalize the finite multiplicity lemma: freedom from LCM patterns of order $k$ implies that each $t$ has at most $k-1$ representations as $ap$, with $a\in A$ and $p$ prime. A finite-sum rearrangement should then reproduce Erdős's inequality exactly. Small cases provide falsifiable tests of both the lemma and the conventions for distinct elements.

The harder research question is to determine whether the improved upper and lower logarithmic exponents coincide, and under what sunflower-capacity assumptions. A complete review of the 2025 paper and any subsequent claimed solution is required before changing the catalogue's open status.
