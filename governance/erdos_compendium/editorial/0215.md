## The question in plain language

Is there a subset $S$ of the real plane such that **every rigid motion** $T$ places **exactly one integer lattice point** inside $T(S)$?

$$
|T(S)\cap\mathbb Z^2|=1.
$$

Here rigid motions include translations and rotations, and reflections may be included without changing the formulation. Applying the inverse motion makes this equivalent to asking for a set $S$ that meets **every isometric copy of $\mathbb Z^2$** at exactly one point. This is the classical Steinhaus problem.

## A small example

A lattice fundamental domain such as the half-open unit square $[0,1)^2$ contains exactly one lattice point after every **translation**, but rotations break that property. A rotated copy of the domain can contain zero or more than one lattice point. Thus a solution must be much more subtle than merely choosing one representative from each coset of $\mathbb Z^2$.

The set's existence is not automatically a claim of **measurability**. Choice-dependent sets can have unexpected descriptive-set-theoretic properties; a construction in unrestricted set theory does not imply that one can algorithmically decide membership.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Steve Jackson and R. Daniel Mauldin, [*On a lattice problem of H. Steinhaus*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-02-00400-9), *Journal of the American Mathematical Society* 15 (2002), 817–856, constructed a subset of $\mathbb R^2$ meeting **every isometric copy of $\mathbb Z^2$ in exactly one point**. Their shorter primary account, [*Sets meeting isometric copies of the lattice $\mathbb Z^2$ in exactly one point*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.222551699), *PNAS* 99 (2002), 15883–15887, states the same theorem.

The construction depends on the **axiom of choice**. The proof solves the existential set-theoretic question, not the separate question whether a Borel, Lebesgue-measurable or effectively computable example exists.

The maintained [Erdős #215](https://www.erdosproblems.com/215) records Erdős's historical belief that no such set exists; the existence theorem resolves the question in the opposite direction.

## What remains

A bounded semantic task proves the equivalence between the two isometry quantifiers by taking inverse motions, then tests translated lattice fundamental domains and records why they fail under rotations. Acceptance: both existential quantifications retain **exactly one** intersection, rather than “at least one”; no claim of a constructive algorithm or measurable set is added without proof.
