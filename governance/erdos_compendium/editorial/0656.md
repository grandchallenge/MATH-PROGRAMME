## The question in plain language

Suppose $A$ is a set of positive whole numbers that occupies a non-negligible fraction of the integers infinitely often. Can we find infinitely many members of $A$ whose **pairwise sums**, after adding one fixed shift, all return to $A$?

The precise [Erdős Problem #656](https://www.erdosproblems.com/656) asks for an infinite $B\subseteq A$ and an integer $t$ such that

$$
\{b_1+b_2+t:b_1,b_2\in B,\ b_1\ne b_2\}\subseteq A.
$$

Two subtleties matter: the elements of $B$ must themselves lie in $A$, and the sums use **two distinct elements**. We may translate all sums by one integer $t$, but we do not choose a different shift for each pair.

This statement is **proved** by a published 2024 theorem of Bryna Kra, Joel Moreira, Florian K. Richter and Donald Robertson. It is not an open mathematical conjecture.

## A small example

Let $A$ consist of all positive integers leaving remainder $1$ on division by $3$:

$$
A=\{1,4,7,10,13,\ldots\}.
$$

This has density $1/3$. Choose $B=A$ and $t=2$. Every pair $b_1,b_2\in B$ has

$$
b_1+b_2\equiv 2\pmod 3,
\quad\text{so}\quad
b_1+b_2+2\equiv1\pmod3.
$$

For instance, the distinct members $1$ and $4$ have $1+4+2=7\in A$; $4$ and $7$ give $13\in A$. Indeed the property holds for *all* pairs. Conversely, choosing an infinite $B\subseteq A$ cannot make unshifted pairwise sums belong to $A$ in this example: they always have remainder $2$, not $1$. So the permission to shift is mathematically significant.

This example is a direct elementary check, not a proof for every positive-density set.

## What is known

The upper asymptotic density of $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ is

$$
\overline d(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}
\frac{|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|}{N}.
$$

The registered problem assumes $\overline d(A)>0$. A related, broader notion is **upper Banach density**: one is allowed to find long intervals anywhere on the number line, rather than only initial intervals, that have a positive fraction of members in $A$.

Kra, Moreira, Richter and Robertson proved a result for the broader class. Their published [*A proof of Erdős's $B+B+t$ conjecture*](https://doi.org/10.1090/cams/34), *Communications of the American Mathematical Society* **4** (2024), 480–494, states in **Theorem 1.2(i), page 481**:

For every $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ of positive upper Banach density, there are an infinite $B\subseteq A$ and $t\in\mathbb N$ for which the restricted pairwise-sum set of $B$ is contained in $A-t$.

Writing $S\subseteq A-t$ means that $s+t\in A$ for every $s\in S$. Thus the theorem's shift has the **same sign** as the original registered requirement. The authors also emphasize that the diagonal exclusion and the translation cannot simply be dropped.

Why does this settle the upper-*asymptotic*-density version? If $\overline d(A)>0$, choose a sequence $N_k\to\infty$ along which the initial-interval densities converge to some positive number. The intervals $[1,N_k]$ form a Følner sequence because translating them by a fixed $h$ changes only $O(h)$ positions out of $N_k$. Thus $A$ has positive upper Banach density, so the published theorem applies with precisely $B\subseteq A$ as required.

The theorem is not merely an assertion about two unrelated infinite sets $B,C$ with $B+C\subseteq A$. Nor is it the later **density finite sums theorem**, which permits $B\subseteq\mathbb N$ and asks simultaneously for several subset-sum lengths. The $B\subseteq A$ condition and fixed two-summand constraint identify this particular problem.

At a high level the 2024 paper transports an arithmetic density condition into an ergodic-dynamical system and then extracts an infinite combinatorial pattern. The precise bridge passes through its **Theorem 1.4**. This is a published proof, not an independently reconstructed or Lean-replayed GCL proof.
