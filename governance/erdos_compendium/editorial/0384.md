## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 384 asks how small a prime divisor must occur in a nontrivial binomial coefficient. In the usual notation, take integers with $1<k<n-1$ and inspect $\binom{n}{k}$. The maintained problem page currently displays the question as asking for a prime $p<n/2$, apart from $\binom{7}{3}=5\cdot7$.

The primary paper gives a nearby, and mathematically coherent, normalization. Ecklund begins with $n\geq2k$ and proves that $\binom{n}{k}$ has a prime divisor satisfying

$$
p\leq\max\{n/k,n/2\},
$$

with the single exception $\binom{7}{3}$. For $k\geq2$, the maximum is $n/2$, so this is a non-strict bound. Passing from $k\leq n/2$ to every $1<k<n-1$ by $\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}$ introduces the symmetric exception $\binom{7}{4}=35$ as well. Thus the source-level theorem is: outside $(n,k)=(7,3)$ and $(7,4)$, some prime divisor $p$ of $\binom{n}{k}$ satisfies $2p\leq n$.

That distinction is not cosmetic. The displayed strict inequality fails already at $(n,k)=(4,2)$, where $\binom42=6$ has prime divisors $2$ and $3$, neither satisfying $2p<4$. The exact issue-specific source audit is [GitHub issue #1654](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1654), assignment `ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0384-S01`.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check of the non-strict theorem's shape. Take $n=10$ and $k=4$. Direct integer arithmetic gives

$$
\binom{10}{4}=210=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7.
$$

The bound is $n/2=5$, and the witness $p=2$ satisfies $2p=4\leq10$. A reproducible check, using no floating-point arithmetic, is:

```python
import math
n, k = 10, 4
b = math.comb(n, k)
primes = [p for p in range(2, b + 1)
          if all(p % d for d in range(2, p) if d*d <= p) and b % p == 0]
print(b, primes, [p for p in primes if 2*p <= n])
```

The executed output was `210 [2, 3, 5, 7] [2, 3, 5]`. This verifies one concrete instance and makes the endpoint convention visible. It does not prove the universal theorem, reproduce Ecklund's estimates, or turn a finite search into a resolution of any stronger conjecture. The same exact calculation at $(n,k)=(4,2)$ returns $6$, prime divisors $[2,3]$, and no strict witnesses with $2p<4$; this is the small obstruction to the strict formulation.

## What is known

The primary resolution is E. F. Ecklund Jr., “On prime divisors of the binomial coefficient,” *Pacific Journal of Mathematics* 29 (1969), 267–270, [PDF](https://msp.org/pjm/1969/29-2/pjm-v29-n2-p04-p.pdf). The theorem is printed on p. 267. The paper proves the result by estimates for prime-counting functions, a Sylvester–Schur-type argument, and a finite check. On pp. 269–270 it says that the remaining ranges were checked for $4\leq k\leq60$ with $2k\leq n\leq k^2$ and for $61\leq k\leq105$ with $2k\leq n\leq4k$, using an IBM 1620 and manual cross-checking. This description is evidence about the published proof, not a modern replay.

The maintained [Erdős Problems page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/384), accessed 2026-10-13, labels the problem proved, cites [ErGr80, p. 73], and attributes the proof to Ecklund. It also records stronger conjectural directions, including the proposed $p<n/k$ conclusion when $n>k^2$; those stronger claims are not established by the theorem audited here. The page's displayed strict statement and sole exception do not match Ecklund's non-strict theorem plus symmetry, so the editorial record preserves the discrepancy rather than silently repairing the live wording.

The primary paper is available and unambiguous about its theorem and page. No claim is made here about an independent proof, a prize, or a new result. The finite calculation above is only an interface test.

## What remains

The mathematical resolution of the non-strict Problem 384 statement is already supplied by Ecklund. The bounded successor task should therefore be semantic and formal rather than a fresh search for a proof. First, fix the exact target as natural numbers $n,k,p$, hypotheses $1<k<n-1$, exclusions $(n,k)\ne(7,3)$ and $(n,k)\ne(7,4)$, primality of $p$, divisibility $p\mid\binom{n}{k}$, and $2p\leq n$. Then prove the elementary symmetry bridge between the lower-half source theorem and the unrestricted statement.

A separate falsification lemma should state that the strict target with $2p<n$ is false at $(4,2)$. A later formal proof may import the mathematical content of Ecklund as an admitted external theorem, but it must label that dependency and must not present `sorry` as a proof. Acceptance would require an exact statement comparison, a source citation to Ecklund p. 267, and a successful pinned Lean replay when such a replay is authorized. Failure of the comparison, or discovery that the target still says strict inequality, is a stopping condition and should return the task to source normalization.
