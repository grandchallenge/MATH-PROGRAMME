## The question in plain language

Color each integer from $1$ through $N$ with one of $k$ colors. A target $s\le N$ is a distinct-summand monochromatic sum if

$$
s=a+b,\quad 1\le a<b\le N,\quad \operatorname{color}(a)=\operatorname{color}(b).
$$

We count distinct **target values**, not representations. Roth asked whether some absolute $c>0$ works for every number of colors $k$, once $N$ is sufficiently large depending on $k$, so that at least $cN$ targets are representable.

The inequality $a\ne b$ is essential. If repeated summands were permitted, all $\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ even targets would qualify trivially as $2a$. Erdős's 1961 Problem I.16 does not explicitly display the distinctness condition; the 1989 resolving paper expressly identifies it as necessary. This historical semantic difference must not be hidden.

## A small example

Define a two-coloring by the odd part of an integer. If $n=2^r u$ with $u$ odd, give $n$ color 0 when $u\equiv1\pmod4$, and color 1 when $u\equiv3\pmod4$.

No two **distinct**, equally colored positive integers sum to a power of two. For suppose $a+b=2^j$. The valuations $v_2(a)$ and $v_2(b)$ must agree, say equal $r$; otherwise the sum has the smaller valuation, contrary to its being $2^j$. The odd parts $u=a/2^r$, $v=b/2^r$ add to $2^{j-r}$. Distinctness forces $j-r\ge2$, so $u+v\equiv0\pmod4$. One odd part is $1$ modulo four and the other $3$ modulo four, giving different colors.

For $N=16$, exact enumeration finds missing target values $1,2,4,8,16$. Powers of two thus give a logarithmic family of exceptions. This does not contradict positive density, since only $O(\log N)$ powers of two lie below $N$.

## What is known

The primary solution is P. Erdős, A. Sárközy and V. T. Sós, *On a conjecture of Roth and some related problems. I* (1989), printed pp. 47–59, Theorem 1 (p. 48) and proof (pp. 48–51). Let $C_N^{(2)}$ denote the **even** targets at most $N$ having a representation by distinct, equally colored summands. For every integer $k\ge2$ there exists $N_0(k)$ such that every $k$-coloring, for $N>N_0(k)$, satisfies

$$
|C_N^{(2)}|>\frac N2-3N^{1-2^{-(k+1)}}.
$$

The exponent is less than one for fixed $k$. For sufficiently large $N$ depending on $k$, the right side exceeds $N/4$. Thus Roth's conjecture holds with universal $c=1/4$. Theorem 1(ii) improves the two-color bound to

$$
|C_N^{(2)}|>\frac N2-\frac{\log N}{\log\bigl((1+\sqrt5)/2\bigr)},
$$

and Theorem 1(iii) constructs a two-coloring avoiding every power of two. The logarithmic deficit for two colors is therefore sharp in order of magnitude.

The general proof uses a density form of Hilbert's cube lemma. If too many even targets were missing, they would contain every vertex of a combinatorial cube

$$
u+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\varepsilon_i v_i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\},
$$

where the $v_i$ are distinct positive integers and $u$ is even. Among the $k+1$ distinct positive integers $u/2+v_i$, two have the same color. Their sum is a cube vertex, contradicting its missing status. The paper proves the required quantitative cube lemma before this pigeonhole step.

There is an elementary additional proof of the two-color logarithmic bound. List the missing even targets as $b_1<b_2<\cdots$. If $b_{j+2}<b_j+b_{j+1}$, then

$$
x=\frac{b_j+b_{j+1}-b_{j+2}}2,\quad
y=\frac{b_j+b_{j+2}-b_{j+1}}2,\quad
z=\frac{b_{j+1}+b_{j+2}-b_j}2
$$

are three distinct positive integers satisfying $x+y=b_j$, $x+z=b_{j+1}$, and $y+z=b_{j+2}$. Two of $x,y,z$ share a color, contradicting the missing status. Hence

$$
b_{j+2}\ge b_j+b_{j+1}.
$$

Since $b_1\ge2$ and $b_2\ge4$, induction gives $b_j\ge2F_{j+1}$, where $F_1=F_2=1$. Only $O(\log N)$ missing even targets can lie below $N$.
