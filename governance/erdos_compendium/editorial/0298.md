## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 298 asks whether a sufficiently thick set of positive integers must contain a finite Egyptian-fraction representation of $1$. In precise terms, if $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ has positive density, must there be a finite set $S\subseteq A$ such that

$$
\sum_{n\in S}\frac{1}{n}=1?
$$

Here “positive density” means that the limit

$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,N]|}{N}=d
$$

exists with $d>0$. The quantifier is important: the same finite set $S$ is allowed to depend on the particular set $A$, but it must be a set of distinct members of $A$, not a list with repetitions and not an infinite series. The question is therefore not whether the reciprocal series over all of $A$ sums to $1$; it asks whether one finite subcollection has exactly that sum.

The current mathematical status is proved. Bloom’s Theorem 1.2 proves the stronger assertion that positive *upper* density is enough: it assumes only

$$
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,N]|}{N}>0.
$$

Every set with positive density has positive upper density, so Bloom’s theorem implies the registered question. The converse implication does not hold in general: a set can have positive upper density while its ordinary density fails to exist. That distinction explains why the newer theorem is stronger rather than merely a restatement.

## A small example

The finite arithmetic is elementary, but it illustrates exactly what the witness must look like. Take $A=\{2,3,6\}$. Then $S=A$ is finite, all denominators are distinct, and

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16=\frac{3+2+1}{6}=1.
$$

This finite $A$ is only an arithmetic toy, not itself a positive-density set; the density theorem says that every genuinely positive-density set contains some witness of this kind.

There is also a useful scaled identity. For every integer $m\geq 1$,

$$
\frac{1}{2m}+\frac{1}{3m}+\frac{1}{6m}=\frac{1}{m}.
$$

At $m=1$ this gives the required sum. For a reproducible exact check, the following uses rational arithmetic rather than floating-point approximations; it verifies the scaled identity for the finite range $1\leq m\leq1000$:

```python
from fractions import Fraction

assert Fraction(1, 2) + Fraction(1, 3) + Fraction(1, 6) == Fraction(1, 1)
assert all(
    Fraction(1, 2*m) + Fraction(1, 3*m) + Fraction(1, 6*m) == Fraction(1, m)
    for m in range(1, 1001)
)
```

This check is independently reproducible and is reported with the exact audit evidence in [RESULT/1 for issue #1568](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1568#issuecomment-6137974164). It proves only these finite identities. It says nothing by itself about density, which is an asymptotic hypothesis quantified over an infinite set of integers.

## What is known

The original source trail is Erdős and Graham’s *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), where the positive-density strengthening appears on printed p. 36, and Erdős’s *Some of my forgotten problems in number theory* (Hardy–Ramanujan Journal 15, 1992, pp. 34–50). The 1992 scan is an historical source for the problem, but the exact page containing Problem 298 was not established in the source audit; that bibliographic limitation is retained here rather than filled by inference.

Bloom’s paper, *On a density conjecture about unit fractions*, JEMS 27 (2025), 4563–4589, DOI [10.4171/JEMS/1456](https://doi.org/10.4171/JEMS/1456), gives the decisive published theorem. On printed p. 4564, Theorem 1.2 states: if $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ has positive upper density, then there is a finite $S\subseteq A$ with reciprocal sum $1$. The paper also gives a quantitative finite-set theorem (Theorem 1.3) and explains how its technical proposition supplies the density result. Those details are evidence for the theorem’s proof, not a license to replace its hypotheses with a different statement.

The paper’s Appendix B, printed pp. 4585–4586, describes a Lean formalisation and displays the corresponding upper-density theorem. That report is useful provenance. It does not turn a source audit into a new mathematical certification, and it does not erase the distinction between the published human-readable theorem and a separately replayed formal artifact.

## What remains

There is no remaining open mathematical conjecture at the registered strength in the evidence reviewed here: Bloom’s published theorem settles it, and in fact settles the weaker upper-density hypothesis. What remains is evidence hygiene and semantic reconciliation. A successor audit could identify the exact page in the 1992 scan, replay the pinned Lean project with its specified Lean 3.42.1 and mathlib revision, and compare the formal theorem line by line with the natural-language statement.

That comparison must check several small but consequential details: whether the ambient naturals include $0$; whether the finite witness is represented as a `Finset`; whether inclusion means every witness denominator lies in $A$; whether the reciprocal is taken in $\mathbb Q$ rather than by natural-number division; and whether `upper_density A > 0` has exactly the intended limsup meaning. The external theorem does not exclude $0$, while the pinned Formal Conjectures statement does. In Lean’s rationals, $1/0=0$; removing $0$ from a witness therefore preserves its sum, but this is a semantic observation to document, not an excuse to skip comparison.

The stopping point for that successor task is one exact, hash-checked replay and one documented semantic comparison. A successful compilation would establish what that pinned source compiles to. It would not independently certify Bloom’s mathematics, change the published theorem’s status, or authorize a claim beyond the source evidence.
