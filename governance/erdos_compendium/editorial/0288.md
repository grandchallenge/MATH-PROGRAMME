## The question in plain language

Take a consecutive block of positive integers, such as $[3,6]=\{3,4,5,6\}$, and add the reciprocals of its members. Write

$$
H(a,b)=\sum_{n=a}^{b}\frac{1}{n},
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers with $a\le b$. Erdős Problem 288 asks whether there are only finitely many pairs of such intervals $I_1=[a_1,b_1]$ and $I_2=[a_2,b_2]$ for which

$$
H(a_1,b_1)+H(a_2,b_2)\in\mathbb{N}.
$$

Here “only finitely many” means that the entire set of qualifying pairs is finite: there is some bound beyond which no new qualifying pair exists. The question is not asking us merely to find examples, or to decide all pairs below a chosen numerical cutoff. The target integer is positive because every reciprocal is positive. The intervals need not be disjoint, non-overlapping, adjacent, or different; no such restriction appears in the current two-interval formulation. Thus the quantifiers matter: $I_1$ and $I_2$ range independently over all nonempty consecutive blocks.

The source also records nearby questions, but they are not interchangeable. One asks whether $H(a,b)+1/m$ is integral for only finitely many triples $(a,b,m)$; another asks about $k$ intervals for every fixed $k$. The universal statement for every $k$ is stronger than the original two-interval problem, while a claim about the existence of some $k>2$ is weaker. Keeping these readings separate prevents a result about a variant from being mistaken for a solution of Problem 288.

## A small example

There are immediate examples if intervals may overlap: two copies of $[1,1]$ give $1+1=2$. A more informative example uses one genuine block and one singleton:

$$
H(3,6)+H(20,20)
=\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac16+\frac1{20}
=\frac{20+15+12+10+3}{60}=1.
$$

This is an exact rational calculation, not a decimal approximation. I independently checked it with Python’s `fractions.Fraction`, which returned exactly $1$. The calculation demonstrates why the problem is not the question “can such a pair exist?”; examples are easy to exhibit. The difficult assertion is that, after all examples are collected, only finitely many pairs remain.

The source audit also gives a useful one-interval diagnostic. Suppose $H(a,b)=A/B$ is in lowest terms and

$$
H(a,b)+\frac1m=k
$$

for an integer $k$. Then $A/B=(km-1)/m$. Since $km-1$ is relatively prime to $m$, the reduced denominator must be $B=m$, and consequently $B$ divides $A+1$. This is a necessary-and-sufficient arithmetic test for a fixed block once its reduced numerator and denominator have been computed. It does not say that there are finitely many such blocks.

## What is known

The primary source is the passage on printed page 34 of Paul Erdős and Ronald L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, Monographies de l’Enseignement Mathématique 28, Université de Genève, 1980, MR 0592420. There the authors define the same consecutive reciprocal sum, say that the sum of two such quantities is probably an integer only finitely often, and immediately explain that this may be difficult because even adding one reciprocal, $H(a,b)+1/n$, is not known to be integral only finitely often. This is a problem statement and research outlook, not a theorem proving finiteness.

## What remains

The missing mathematical result is a theorem establishing either finiteness of all qualifying two-interval pairs or infinitude of such pairs. The smallest clearly stated unresolved interface in the source audit is already the singleton variant: determine whether only finitely many $(a,b,m)$ satisfy $H(a,b)+1/m\in\mathbb{Z}$. A solution of that variant would be relevant evidence, but it would still need to be related carefully to the two-interval statement; it should not be silently relabeled as a solution of the parent problem.

A bounded successor task can reproduce the singleton census through $b=1600$ using exact rational arithmetic, apply the denominator test above, and check the six-tuple list and digest. Its falsification hooks are concrete: a missing row, an extra row, or a disagreement in the exact fractions. Its stopping rule is equally important: after the finite range is exhausted, report a bounded computation and stop. Extending the cutoff may generate evidence, but no finite search can by itself prove the required global finiteness claim.
