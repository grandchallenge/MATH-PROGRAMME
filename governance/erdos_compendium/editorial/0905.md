## The question in plain language

A triangle in a graph consists of three vertices with all three possible edges present. Fix an edge $xy$. Every vertex adjacent to **both** $x$ and $y$ makes one triangle containing that edge. A collection of triangles all sharing the same edge is called a *book*, and its number of pages is its triangle count.

[Erdős Problem #905](https://www.erdosproblems.com/905) asks whether every simple graph with $n$ vertices and **strictly more** than $n^2/4$ edges has an edge contained in at least $n/6$ distinct triangles:

$$
m>\frac{n^2}{4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\exists\,xy\in E(G):
|N(x)\cap N(y)|\ge\frac n6.
$$

This is a proved theorem, attributed independently to Edwards and to Khadzhiivanov and Nikiforov. The assertion concerns **one edge with many common-neighbor triangles**, not merely the total number of triangles in the graph.

## A small example

Consider the balanced complete bipartite graph $K_{a,b}$, where $a=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $b=\lceil n/2\rceil$. It has exactly

$$
ab=\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor
$$

edges, but **no triangles**. Thus the theorem cannot replace its strict hypothesis $m>n^2/4$ with $m\ge\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$: the bipartite example would violate the conclusion.

For $n=6$, the threshold is $m>9$. Every six-vertex graph with ten or more edges contains an edge belonging to at least one triangle; the theorem asks for at least $6/6=1$ such triangle. The exact finite verifier independently enumerates all $2^{15}=32,768$ six-vertex graphs. Among them exactly **4,944** have more than nine edges, and every one passes the edge-book requirement.

For $n=5$, the strict threshold $m>25/4$ requires at least seven edges. The finite verifier finds 176 eligible five-vertex graphs, and each contains an edge in at least two triangles. This stronger finite observation does not generalize automatically.

## What is known

The result originated as a conjecture of Bollobás and Erdős, recorded in Erdős's 1975 problem survey, in [Er82e, p.71] and [Er93, p.344]. The maintained [Erdős catalogue #905](https://www.erdosproblems.com/905) calls the result **PROVED (LEAN)**.

The published solution is attributed to N. G. Khadzhiivanov and S. V. Nikiforov, [*Solution of a problem of P. Erdős about the maximum number of triangles with a common edge in a graph*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/905), *Comptes Rendus de l'Académie Bulgare des Sciences* (1979), pp.1315–1318. The other proof by C. S. Edwards is generally cited as **unpublished**. The precise full 1979 article was not independently retrieved and its proof was not reconstructed in this task.

As a separate later source, Jacob Fox and Po-Shen Loh, [*On the Erdős–Rothschild problem*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.0290), record the original theorem in their introduction and explain its threshold behavior. Below density $1/4$, related extremal book problems can have very different behavior even when every edge is required to belong to a triangle. Their work does not replace the strict-density conclusion of #905.

The maintained problem page records an April 2026 commenter reporting an online Aristotle/GPT-5.4 formal proof. The upstream [Formal Conjectures #905](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/main/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/905.lean) source links an external Lean development at version 4.29.1, but the upstream theorem body itself contains a placeholder. GCL has **not** independently compiled that external proof, matched all source hypotheses or inspected its axiom output.

## GCL's current state

The finite assertion has a clean integer formulation. Write

$$
b_G(xy)=|N(x)\cap N(y)|
$$

for the number of triangles containing an edge $xy$. The theorem's source statement is exactly

$$
4m>n^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\max_{xy\in E(G)}6b_G(xy)\ge n.
$$

The strict inequality on the left and multiplication by six on the right permit exact arithmetic, without floating-point comparisons or ambiguous rounding.

The executable program governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_905.py enumerates every labelled simple graph for $2\le n\le6$, a total of **33,866** graphs. It checks the edge density, computes every neighborhood as an integer bitmask and counts the common neighbors of each actual edge by exact bit intersection. Every graph exceeding the threshold passes. There are no eligible two-vertex graphs, because a two-vertex simple graph can have at most one edge.

The same code constructs balanced bipartite threshold examples for $2\le n\le8$, verifying their precise number of edges and the absence of triangles. This is a finite independent regression of the statement and its sharp strict-threshold requirement, not a replacement for the 1979 proof.

## What remains

A bounded reusable theorem is the **booksize-to-common-neighbors lemma**: for every edge in a simple graph, establish a bijection between triangles containing that edge and its common neighbors. Formalize the integer predicate $4m>n^2$ together with existence of an edge satisfying $6b_G(xy)\ge n$.

Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or a pinned no-placeholder Lean development with literal axiom reporting, exhaustive replay of all 33,866 graphs, the reported eligible-graph counts, and correct behavior at the complete bipartite boundary. Any missed triangle, incorrect edge orientation, non-strict threshold or rounding error invalidates the finite certificate.

A distinct source obligation is to obtain the full Khadzhiivanov–Nikiforov 1979 proof and independently reconstruct its extremal argument, then compare the exact theorem to the external 2026 Lean file and kernel-verify a pinned build. The finite checks do not establish that all graphs of arbitrary order satisfy the source theorem.
