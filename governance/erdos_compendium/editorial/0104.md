## The question in plain language

Given $n$ distinct points in the plane, consider circles of **radius exactly one** passing through at least three of them. How many distinct such circles can there be? Erdős #104 asks whether their maximum, $U_3(n)$, is **subquadratic**:

$$
U_3(n)=o(n^2).
$$

Two circles with different centres count separately, even when they contain overlapping groups of points. The radius and the requirement of at least three points are fixed; counting all possible radii is a different problem.

## A small example

Three vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $\sqrt3$ lie on a single circle of radius one: its circumradius is $1$. A triangle with side one has circumradius $1/\sqrt3$, not one, and therefore contributes no such unit circle through all three vertices.

A circle containing exactly $t\ge3$ of the points contains $\binom{t}{2}$ point pairs. Each pair at distance at most two lies on **at most two** circles of radius one. Double-count the incidences between point pairs and qualifying circles to obtain

$$
3U_3(n)\le \sum_C\binom{|C\cap A|}{2}
\le 2\binom n2,
\qquad
U_3(n)\le\frac{n(n-1)}3.
$$

This improves the rough $O(n^2)$ upper bound, but the result still has quadratic order.

## What is known

The conjecture is **OPEN**. Erdős showed that a linear number of unit circles can occur. György Elekes, [*n points in the plane can determine n^{3/2} unit circles*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/104), *Combinatorica* (1984), gave configurations with $\gg n^{3/2}$ qualifying circles. That reference is recorded in the maintained catalogue; the complete original construction was not independently reproduced for this audit.

Harborth and Mengersen's 1986 work clarified the constant in the elementary pair-incidence upper bound, $n(n-1)/3$, rather than the weaker $n(n-1)$ sometimes cited in Erdős's earlier accounts. Neither the lower exponent $3/2$ nor this upper bound resolves $o(n^2)$. The sharper $O(n^{3/2})$ proposal is another, stronger question.

## What remains

Implement a rational-coordinate checker that enumerates circles determined by triples, retains only circumcircles of squared radius one, and deduplicates by exact rational centres. Acceptance: for every input, each reported circle contains at least three points, and the count obeys $3U_3(n)\le2\binom n2$. A fixture with one equilateral $\sqrt3$ triangle must report one unit circle. Stop at finite verification; do not infer a subquadratic asymptotic bound.
