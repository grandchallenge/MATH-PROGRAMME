## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 353 asks about shapes forced inside a very large measurable subset of the plane. Let $A$ be a measurable subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ with infinite Lebesgue measure. Must four points of $A$ be the vertices of an isosceles trapezoid having area $1$? The same question was posed for an isosceles triangle, a right-angled triangle, a cyclic quadrilateral, and a convex polygon whose sides are all congruent. The original question is recorded in the [maintained Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/353) and is attributed there to Erdős's 1983 source [Er83d](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0072626).

The trapezoid part is an existence statement. It does not say that every four points in $A$ work, that the shape is unique, or that a finite search will find it. “Infinite measure” is also stronger than merely being unbounded: an unbounded set can have finite or even zero measure. The measurable-set hypothesis is used by the proof through density arguments.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check of the geometry. Take, in cyclic order,

$$
(0,0),\quad (2,0),\quad (3/2,2/3),\quad (1/2,2/3).
$$

The two horizontal bases have lengths $2$ and $1$, and the height is $2/3$. Thus the trapezoid area is

$$
\frac{2+1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}=1.
$$

The two nonparallel sides have squared lengths

$$
\left(\frac12\right)^2+\left(\frac23\right)^2=\frac{25}{36},
$$

so they are equal. A direct rational shoelace calculation gives area $1$, and the two leg-squared values are both $25/36$; these are the exact outputs of the finite arithmetic check for this example. This verifies one concrete isosceles trapezoid, not the Erdős assertion: its four points form a specially chosen finite set, not an arbitrary measurable infinite-measure set.

## What is known

The primary resolution is Junnosuke Koizumi, “Isosceles trapezoids of unit area with vertices in sets of infinite planar measure,” [arXiv:2501.01914v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01914), submitted 3 January 2025; the paper is also associated with DOI [10.1090/proc/17322](https://doi.org/10.1090/proc/17322). Its Theorem 2, stated in the Introduction on PDF page 1 and proved in Section 3 beginning on PDF page 4, says exactly that every measurable $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$ of infinite Lebesgue measure contains the four vertices of an isosceles trapezoid of area $1$.

The proof is constructive in mechanism, although not a finite construction from a description of $S$. It chooses a density point near a small disk and another density point far away. For a large scale factor $R$, it considers points that remain in $S$ after scaling by $R^{-1}$. A radius-dependent rotation $f$ is chosen so that $p$, $f(p)$, $R^{-1}f(p)$, and $R^{-1}p$ form an isosceles trapezoid. The angle is selected so that

$$
\left(1-\frac{1}{R^2}\right)\frac{r^2}{2}\sin\psi(r)=1,
$$

and the map preserves area. Density and a contradiction argument then force both $p$ and $f(p)$ into the required large-measure subset. The same paper proves stronger hypotheses for the isosceles- and right-triangle variants: unbounded measurable sets of positive measure suffice.

Kovač and Predojević’s [2024 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11725) supplies the positive cyclic-quadrilateral result and the negative congruent-sides result, which explains why the catalogue groups several related questions under one entry. Those are useful neighboring results, not substitutes for Koizumi’s trapezoid theorem.
