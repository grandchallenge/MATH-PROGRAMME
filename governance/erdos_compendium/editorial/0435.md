## The question in plain language

A binomial coefficient counts the ways to choose a prescribed number of objects from a larger collection. For example, $\binom63=20$. Fix an integer $n$ and take the **nontrivial** coefficients

$$
\binom n1,\binom n2,\ldots,\binom n{n-1}.
$$

Erdős Problem 435 asks for the **largest positive integer that cannot be written as a nonnegative integer combination of these coefficients**, with repetitions permitted. In the language of numerical semigroups, this is their *Frobenius number*.

There is an essential restriction: $n$ must **not** be a power of a prime. When $n=p^a$, all the nontrivial coefficients $\binom ni$ have common factor $p$. Consequently infinitely many integers are not representable, so no finite greatest missing integer exists without first rescaling the generating set. For $n$ having at least two different prime divisors, the coefficients instead have gcd one, and the Frobenius number is finite.

The problem connects elementary combinations, the distribution of prime factors in binomial coefficients and the structure of numerical semigroups. It is now solved by work of WonTae Hwang and Kyunghwan Song.

## A small example

Take $n=6=2\cdot3$, which is not a prime power. The nontrivial binomial coefficients in the sixth row of Pascal's triangle are

$$
\binom61=6,\quad
\binom62=15,\quad
\binom63=20,\quad
\binom64=15,\quad
\binom65=6.
$$

Thus the set of possible sums is generated by just the three denominations $6,15,20$. The largest integer that **cannot** be formed is $49$.

The assertion can be verified completely, rather than guessed from a partial search. The number $49$ is not a sum of nonnegative multiples of $6,15,20$: examining the possible numbers of coins with denomination $20$, and then reducing the remaining amount modulo $3$ and $6$, rules out a representation. Conversely, every integer from $50$ through $55$ is representable:

$$
\begin{aligned}
50&=20+15+15,\\
51&=6\cdot6+15,\\
52&=20+20+6+6,\\
53&=20+15+6+6+6,\\
54&=9\cdot6,\\
55&=20+20+15.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $6$ is an available denomination, adding further copies of $6$ covers every integer greater than $55$. Therefore **$49$ is exactly the Frobenius number**, not just a large missing value in a finite test.

The published formula gives the same result: the prime factorization of $6$ is $2^1 3^1$, so the predicted answer is

$$
(2-1)\binom62+(3-1)\binom63-6
=15+40-6=49.
$$

This single exact check illustrates the theorem without establishing the formula in general.

## What is known

The question appears in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed p. 86 of the [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). It asks for the Frobenius number of a special family of binomial coefficients rather than arbitrary coin denominations.

WonTae Hwang and Kyunghwan Song solved the binomial semigroup problem in [*The Frobenius problem for Binomial Coefficients*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17882), first submitted December 23, 2024. Their Theorem 0.1 and Corollary 3.3 describe the numerical semigroup generated by the relevant binomial coefficients and yield a closed form for the greatest nonrepresentable integer. The maintained [Erdős Problems #435](https://www.erdosproblems.com/435) page records the result as **PROVED (LEAN)** and also notes independent work by Michael Peake and Stijn Cambie.

If

$$
n=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{a_p}
$$

has at least two distinct prime factors, the reported formula is

$$
G_n=
\sum_{p\mid n}(p-1)
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{a_p}\binom n{p^j}\right)-n.
$$

The inner sum runs over **powers of each prime** appearing in the factorization of $n$; replacing this by all positive integers $j$ or by the exponents themselves would change the formula. The outer factor $p-1$ is also essential. The expression agrees with the exact example at $n=6$.

The same research develops additional numerical-semigroup invariants, including the Apéry set and the number of gaps. Those quantities provide structure behind the formula but are not the same as the single greatest missing integer. Prime powers require a different, normalized formulation and are excluded from the statement of the original Erdős question.
