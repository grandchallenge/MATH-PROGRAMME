## The question in plain language

[Erdős Problem #1181](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1181) asks the following. The maintained status is **OPEN**.

Write $q(n,k)$ for the **smallest prime dividing none** of the consecutive integers $n+1,\dots,n+k$. The source asks whether some absolute $c>0$ gives for all sufficiently large $n$
$$
q(n,\log n)<(1-c)(\log n)^2.
$$
But the product $\prod_{1\le i\le k}(n+i)$ requires **integer** $k$, while $\log n$ generally is not integral. It is necessary to source-normalize $k=\lfloor\log n\rfloor$ or $\lceil\log n\rceil$ and the logarithm base; asymptotic heuristic claims should not silently supply these. Dividing any of the $k$ factors is distinct from dividing their product.

## A small example

Exact prime trial division gives $q(10,2)=5$, $q(100,4)=5$, $q(1000,7)=23$, and $q(10000,10)=17$, where each $k$ is explicitly an integer. For example, $(11)(12)$ is divisible by 2, 3 and 11, but not 5, so $q(10,2)=5$. These sporadic values establish neither a uniform upper bound with constant $c>0$ nor an asymptotic optimality statement.

## What is known

https://www.erdosproblems.com/1181 currently **OPEN**, [Er79d,p.78]. Standard primorial reasoning yields $q(n,\log n)\le(1+o(1))(\log n)^2$; a uniform fixed relative improvement is unknown. Tao's probabilistic heuristic, quoted in the comments for #457, suggests potentially a much smaller upper size around $(\log n)(\log\log n)/\log\log\log n$ (asymptotically, subject to precise nesting). #457 addresses *lower bounds* and shows why one should separate lower and upper problems. No heuristic is an unconditional theorem.
