## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, let $F(n)$ be the largest prime divisor of the consecutive-integer product $n(n+1)$. Erdős Problem 368 asks how small this largest prime can be as $n$ grows. The motivating tension is simple: $n$ and $n+1$ cannot share a prime factor, but each might separately be built from relatively small primes. The question is therefore about the extreme smoothness of two consecutive integers at once.

The original formulation in Erdős's 1976 paper records two conjectural thresholds. For every positive $epsilon$, infinitely many $n$ should satisfy $F(n)<(\log n)^{2+\epsilon}$, while eventually every $n$ should satisfy $F(n)>(\log n)^{2-\epsilon}$. The maintained problem page presents the same question as open. These are asymptotic statements: a handful of small values cannot settle either one.

## A small example

Here is an exact check that can be reproduced using only integer arithmetic. Take $n=4374$. Direct factorization gives

$$
4374=2\cdot3^7,\qquad 4375=5^4\cdot7,
$$

so

$$
4374\cdot4375=19136250=2\cdot3^7\cdot5^4\cdot7.
$$

The largest prime factor is therefore $F(4374)=7$. The same calculation gives $F(8)=3$ from $8\cdot9=2^3\cdot3^2$, and $F(80)=5$ from $80\cdot81=2^4\cdot3^4\cdot5$. These three identities were also retained as kernel-checked examples in the later Formal Conjectures file. They verify the definition and provide regression tests for a factorization program; they do not establish a bound for all sufficiently large $n$, or the existence of infinitely many $n$ in any asymptotic range.

## What is known

Erdős's primary source, *Problems and results on number theoretic properties of consecutive integers and related questions*, states on printed page 27 that a classical result gives $P(n(n+1))>c\log\log n$, and records the stronger $(\log n)^{2-\epsilon}$ lower-bound expectation and the infinitely-often $(\log n)^{2+\epsilon}$ upper-bound expectation. The paper is careful that the conjecture rests on probabilistic intuition, not a proof.

The strongest directly relevant primary result located in this audit is Hector Pasten's paper *The largest prime factor of $n^2+1$ and improvements on subexponential $ABC$*. Its Corollary 1.5 states that for coprime positive integers $x<y$, the largest prime factor of $xy(x+y)$ is at least a constant multiple of $(\log_2 y)^2/\log_3 y$ for sufficiently large variables. Setting $x=1$ and $y=n$ satisfies the hypotheses for $n>1$ and gives the unconditional lower bound

$$
F(n)\gg\frac{(\log\log n)^2}{\log\log\log n}.
$$

This is a genuine external written proof, not a GCL result. It remains far below Erdős's conjectured order $(\log n)^2$. The live-page text also attributes earlier divergence, logarithmic-logarithmic bounds, and a subpower upper result to Pólya, Mahler, and Schinzel. Those attributions are recorded as source context here; this audit did not independently inspect all three older primary papers.

There is also a normalization issue worth keeping visible. The original paper expresses the conjecture with an arbitrary $epsilon$ and an eventual quantifier, whereas the later Lean declaration uses one constant $c$ and quantifies over every natural number with $1\leq n$. The declaration is therefore useful as an interface for formal work, but it is not evidence that the intended asymptotic theorem has been proved, and the two formulations should not be called identical without an explicit equivalence argument.

## What remains

The smallest useful bounded successor is a source-locked semantic bridge: verify line by line that Pasten's Corollary 1.5 permits $x=1$, $y=n$, then record the resulting statement with its exact asymptotic threshold and no stronger quantifiers. As a finite companion, an exact-arithmetic checker could factor every $n(n+1)$ for $2\leq n\leq10000$, emit a factorization identity and primality certificate for every factor, and reproduce the three examples above. Acceptance would require all identities to multiply back and the source-hypothesis bridge to be correct; any mismatch or hypothesis failure would falsify the task. The run should stop at $10000$, at the first failed identity, or when the source text needed for a claimed stronger result is unavailable. No finite run can decide the infinitely-often clause or the eventual lower bound.
