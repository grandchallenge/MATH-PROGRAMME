## The question in plain language

How large must the biggest prime factor of a binomial coefficient be? [Erdős Problem #683](https://www.erdosproblems.com/683) asks whether some fixed $c>0$ guarantees $P\!\left(\binom nk\right)\geq\min\{n-k+1,k^{1+c}\}$ for a suitable range of $k$, where $P(r)$ means the largest prime factor of $r>1$. The maintained problem remains OPEN. There are two source-definition issues: the maintained text includes $k=n$ (for which the coefficient is $1$), and the pinned formalization changes the non-strict inequality to a **strict** one.

## A small example

Take $n=8$ and $k=7$. Since $\binom87=8=2^3$, its largest prime factor is $2$. For every $c>0$, $\min\{8-7+1,7^{1+c}\}=2$. Thus the maintained **non-strict** claim reads $2\geq2$, correctly. But the [pinned Lean theorem](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/683.lean) requires **$2>2$**, which is FALSE. This is no isolated small accident: for every power of two $n=2^a\geq4$, choosing $k=n-1$ gives $\binom n{n-1}=n$ and largest prime factor $2$; the same false strict inequality recurs infinitely often.

## What is known

The classical Sylvester–Schur theorem, cited by the maintained catalogue, states that for $k\leq n/2$ the largest prime factor of $\binom nk$ exceeds $k$. Erdős later obtained a bound on the scale of $k\log k$. Neither is the requested power $k^{1+c}$ for an absolute $c>0$. A proposed still-stronger exponential-in $\sqrt k$ bound is heuristic, not proved. The symmetry $\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}$ makes the upper-half range relevant. At $k=n$, however, $\binom nn=1$ has no prime factors under the usual mathematical definition. One must either restrict to $k<n$ or explicitly define $P(1)$; adopting $P(1)=1$ makes the non-strict endpoint trivial, whereas the formal file's finite-set supremum convention gives $P(1)=0$.
