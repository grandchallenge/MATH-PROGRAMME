## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 348 asks how robust an infinite nondecreasing sequence of integers can be as a source of subset sums. Call a sequence complete when every sufficiently large integer can be written as a sum of finitely many terms, with each indexed occurrence used at most once. The question fixes integers $0 \leq m < n$ and asks whether there is a sequence that stays complete after deleting any $m$ occurrences, but becomes incomplete after deleting any $n$ occurrences.

The word “any” is decisive. The first requirement is universal over all choices of $m$ deleted positions, and the second is universal over all choices of $n$ positions. Repeated values are still separate occurrences: two copies of $1$ can be used independently, and deleting one copy need not delete the other. The 1980 source also uses eventual completeness, allowing finitely many exceptional integers. That is weaker than requiring every positive integer to be represented.

## A small example

The powers of two explain the easiest boundary. For the finite prefix $A_4 = \{1,2,4,8,16\}$, exact enumeration of all $2^5=32$ subsets gives every sum from $0$ through $31$. After deleting the occurrence $4$, the remaining subset sums are

$$
\{0,1,2,3\} + \{0,8\} + \{0,16\} = \{0,1,2,3,8,9,10,11,16,17,18,19,24,25,26,27\}.
$$

Thus the verified missing values in this finite window are $4$ through $7$, $12$ through $15$, $20$ through $23$, and $28$ through $31$. The calculation is exact integer arithmetic, not a heuristic search: an exhaustive bit-mask enumeration reports 32 sums for the full prefix and 16 for the deleted prefix. Passing to the infinite powers-of-two sequence makes the pattern permanent: numbers whose binary expansion needs the deleted bit occur infinitely often, so one deletion destroys eventual completeness. This finite check illustrates the mechanism; it does not by itself prove an infinite statement.

The other classical example is the Fibonacci sequence with two initial ones, $1,1,2,3,5,\ldots$. The original source records that it survives any one deletion but not any two deletions. The repeated initial $1$’s matter: they are two indexed occurrences, not one set element.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham’s *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), section 6, defines completeness as eventual coverage by finite subset sums on printed page 54. On printed page 57 it poses this question and records the powers-of-two and Fibonacci examples, while saying that the case $m=2$, $n=3$ was not known. That is the original primary statement and the historical baseline.

The maintained Erdős Problems entry remains marked OPEN when consulted for this audit. It repeats the two examples and says that Wouter van Doorn ruled out $2 \leq m < n$ for the stronger interpretation in which every positive integer, rather than all sufficiently large integers, must be represented. The entry itself warns that the intended Erdős–Graham interpretation is probably the weaker, eventual one. A 6 September 2025 discussion comment also identified this strong/weak distinction and said the formalization had been updated; a 16 July 2026 comment reports computational experiments around $(2,3)$, but explicitly presents them as evidence and not a witness or proof.

Jesse Geneson’s arXiv preprint, version 1 dated 20 September 2026, claims a full answer for the eventual interpretation: such sequences exist exactly when $m \leq 1$. Its Theorem 1 uses indexed occurrences and eventual completeness. The proof’s central-interval theorem controls long prefixes when the prefix slack $S_j-a_{j+1}$ tends to infinity, and the paper gives a deletion obstruction for $m \geq 2$. This is the strongest located result, but it is an external, unrefereed preprint; this audit read its statement and proof structure, not an independent mathematical verification.
