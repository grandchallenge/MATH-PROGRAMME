## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #12 concerns infinite sets of positive integers with a restriction on a smaller element dividing the sum of two larger elements. Its historical **Property P** forbids a configuration

$$
a\mid b+c,\qquad a<b\le c,\qquad a,b,c\in A.
$$

The pinned formal provider instead only forbids such triples when the two larger summands are **different**: it allows $b=c$. This is a real mathematical difference, not a harmless change of wording.

The problem has several parts about how numerous such sets can be. Its central remaining question is whether every eligible set has a **convergent sum of reciprocals**. Published results settling other parts do not settle this one.

## A small example

For the finite set $A=\{3,6\}$, the smaller number 3 divides $6+6=12$. The historical property rejects this repeated-summand configuration. The frozen formal predicate allows it because its conclusion when divisibility holds is $b=c$.

The [GCL audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1281#issuecomment-6103838848) even constructs an **infinite** example that satisfies the frozen predicate but not the historical condition:

$$
A_*=\{3,6\}\cup\{p^2:p>3\text{ prime},\ p\equiv3\pmod4\}.
$$

The argument uses residue restrictions and the fact that $-1$ is not a quadratic residue modulo primes congruent to 3 modulo 4. The reciprocal series for this particular set converges, so it is **not** a counterexample to the outstanding reciprocal-sum conjecture.

## What is known

The three-part formal record distinguishes two density-related statements with known mathematical answers from the unresolved question of convergence of the reciprocal series. Sparse natural density alone does not guarantee reciprocal summability.

A useful exact reduction is to group positive elements into dyadic intervals. Put $D_j=\#(A\cap[2^j,2^{j+1}))$. Then

$$
\frac12\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{D_j}{2^j}
\le \sum_{\substack{a\in A\\a\ge1}}\frac1a
\le\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{D_j}{2^j}.
$$

Consequently convergence of the reciprocal sum is equivalent to convergence of the dyadic weighted counting series. This elementary criterion does not itself prove convergence for every eligible set.

The formal source contains unfilled proof bodies, so it is not a local compiled certification.

## What remains

The central task is to establish or refute reciprocal-sum convergence for the **precisely stated** admissible class. Before importing any historical theorem, the repeated-summand distinction must be resolved: a result for the stronger historical Property P cannot automatically be applied to every set allowed by the weaker frozen predicate.
