## The question in plain language

Among $n$ distinct points in $\mathbb R^d$ with diameter exactly one, how many unordered pairs can be **exactly distance one apart**? Denote this maximum by $f_d(n)$. The diameter restriction forbids any distance greater than one, so pairs attaining distance one are *diameter pairs*.

The answer depends strongly on the dimension. This is not the classic unit-distance problem without a diameter restriction: a configuration with many unit pairs but greater diameter is inadmissible.

## A small example

In the plane, the three vertices of an equilateral triangle of side one form three diameter pairs. In $\mathbb R^3$, the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron of edge length one yield six pairs at the diameter, matching $2n-2$ when $n=4$.

For $n=2$ there is only one unordered pair, so $f_d(2)=1$, whatever the dimension. Statements such as $f_2(n)=n$ or $f_3(n)=2n-2$ require the **appropriate qualifications for small $n$**, and cannot be extrapolated to $n=2$.

## What is known

The extremal question is **SOLVED**. In the plane, Hopf and Pannwitz (1934) proved $f_2(n)=n$ for $n\ge3$. In three dimensions, Grünbaum, Heppes and Straszewicz independently established $f_3(n)=2n-2$ for $n\ge4$, with small configurations treated separately.

For dimensions $d\ge4$, Erdős (1960) showed that

$$
f_d(n)=\left(\frac{p-1}{2p}+o(1)\right)n^2,
\qquad p=\lfloor d/2\rfloor.
$$

This predicts quadratic growth unlike the planar and spatial linear formulas. Swanepoel (2009) supplied **exact** extremal values and structures for all sufficiently large $n$ in each fixed dimension $d\ge4$. “Sufficiently large” depends on $d$; the asymptotic expression does not determine the values for every small $n$.

The low-dimensional and asymptotic theorems are sourced from the maintained catalogue, but their full extremal-geometry proofs and exact Swanepoel classification were not independently replayed.

The original issue contains an authenticated [RESULT/1 source report](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1492#issuecomment-6180801295). This is unadjudicated source evidence only, not independent mathematical certification or editorial admission.

## What remains

A bounded task enumerates unit-diameter configurations for known regular simplices and planar unit-diameter polygons, verifying all pair distances and counts. Acceptance: the tetrahedron yields six pairs, an equilateral triangle yields three, and the $n=2$ exception is explicit. Any claim about maximality beyond these examples must cite the published theorem rather than numerical sampling.
