## The question in plain language

Choose some points inside a large circle. We want to keep every pairwise distance away from the integers. For example, two points exactly distance $3$ apart would be forbidden. Points at distance $3.3$ apart may be allowed.

For a real number $t$, write

$$
\|t\|=\min_{k\in\mathbb Z}|t-k|
$$

for its distance to the nearest integer. Given a disk of radius $X$ and a fixed exclusion threshold $0<\delta<1/2$, define $N(X,\delta)$ to be the largest possible number of points $P_1,\ldots,P_n$ inside the disk such that

$$
\bigl\|\,|P_i-P_j|\,\bigr\|\geq\delta
\qquad\text{whenever }i\ne j,
$$

where the inner absolute value is **Euclidean distance** and the outer pair of double bars measures distance to the nearest **integer**.

This is not the usual packing problem: we prohibit every narrow annulus around an integer distance, not only points that are too close. As the circle gets larger, can we arrange linearly many points while avoiding all those annuli?

Erdős conjectured that the answer was no. More precisely, for each fixed $\delta\in(0,1/2)$, he proposed

$$
N(X,\delta)=o(X)
\qquad (X\to\infty).
$$

This says that the maximum number of points, divided by the radius, tends to zero. He also suggested a much sharper upper bound of approximately square-root growth.

Both claims are **proved**.

## A small example

Place three points at equally spaced angles on a circle of radius $1$:

$$
P_1=(1,0),\qquad
P_2=\left(-\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),\qquad
P_3=\left(-\frac12,-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right).
$$

All three lie inside the disk of radius $X=1$. Each pair is at Euclidean distance exactly $\sqrt3$. The distance from $\sqrt3$ to the nearest integer, which is $2$, is

$$
\|\sqrt3\|=2-\sqrt3>\frac14.
$$

The last inequality is exact: it is equivalent to $\sqrt3<7/4$, and hence to $3<49/16$. Therefore these three points obey the condition with $\delta=1/4$, giving

$$
N(1,1/4)\geq3.
$$

The same three points also fit in every larger disk, so $N(X,1/4)\geq3$ for $X\geq1$. This is only a finite example. It does not prove that $N(X,\delta)$ grows like a particular power of $X$.

There is a simple upper bound from ordinary packing. Since $\|t\|\leq t$ for $t\geq0$, the restriction $\|\,|P_i-P_j|\,\|\geq\delta$ forces every two points at least $\delta$ apart. Place disjoint disks of radius $\delta/2$ around the chosen points. All these small disks fit inside the disk of radius $X+\delta/2$. Comparing areas gives

$$
N(X,\delta)\leq\left(\frac{2X}{\delta}+1\right)^2.
$$

That bound grows quadratically with $X$, far too weak to settle Erdős's conjecture. Avoiding neighborhoods of *all* integer distances is much more restrictive than ordinary separation.

## What is known

The original problem appears in Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed pages 92–93. The authors define the planar extremal quantity and ask for sublinear growth. By 1980, the first conjecture had already been established by **András Sárközy**.

Their monograph records the explicit upper bound

$$
N(X,\delta)\leq
\frac{4\cdot10^4}{\delta^3}
\frac{X}{\log\log X}
$$

for sufficiently large $X$. For any **fixed** positive $\delta$, the expression on the right is $o(X)$, since $\log\log X$ tends to infinity. This proved Erdős's original sublinear-growth conjecture.

Erdős also asked whether, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $\delta>0$, one could prove

$$
N(X,\delta)<X^{1/2+\varepsilon}
$$

once $X$ is sufficiently large. This was much stronger than the earlier upper bound, since any power $X^{1/2+\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon<1/2$ is much smaller than $X/\log\log X$.

A later theorem by **Sergei Konyagin** (2001) established the stronger estimate

$$
\boxed{N(X,\delta)=O_\delta(X^{1/2}).}
$$

Here the notation $O_\delta$ means that the multiplicative constant is allowed to depend on the fixed $\delta$. The result does **not** assert that this constant stays bounded if $\delta$ approaches zero.

The implication to the proposed estimate is elementary. If $N(X,\delta)\leq C_\delta\sqrt X$ for large $X$, then

$$
\frac{N(X,\delta)}X\leq\frac{C_\delta}{\sqrt X}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Also, for any fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the inequality $C_\delta<X^\varepsilon$ eventually holds, so

$$
N(X,\delta)<X^{1/2+\varepsilon}
$$

for all sufficiently large $X$. Thus Konyagin's theorem resolves both original upper-bound questions.

A modern, directly inspectable proof route appears in Ritesh Goenka and Kenneth Moore's preprint *Point sets avoiding near-integer distances* (May 2026). It treats the same question in arbitrary Euclidean dimension $d$. Their Theorem 1.2, on preprint page 2, specializes at $d=2$ to exactly the square-root upper bound. The proof, begun in Section 2, adapts Konyagin's Fourier-analytic approach and develops a trigonometric-polynomial lemma to control the forbidden distance set.

The precise d-dimensional result is more intricate than the planar statement: the exponent depends on the dimension modulo four. None of those higher-dimensional distinctions should be silently inserted into the simpler two-dimensional problem.

A neighboring [Erdős Problem #466](https://www.erdosproblems.com/466) asks whether large configurations exist at all, a **lower-bound** question. Graham and Sárközy established substantial lower bounds. The upper theorem for #465 alone does not supply a matching construction for every fixed $\delta$, so it should not be described as proving $N(X,\delta)\asymp_\delta\sqrt X$.
