## The question in plain language

Color every real number with one of two colors, using an arbitrary function
\[
c:\mathbb R\longrightarrow\{0,1\}.
\]
Erdős asked whether **every such coloring** has a set \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\) of cardinality \(\aleph_1\) for which
\[
c(a+b)=c(a'+b')
\]
whenever \(a,b,a',b'\in A\) and \(a\ne b,\ a'\ne b'\). In other words, must every two-coloring contain an **uncountable** set whose sums of **two distinct elements** are monochromatic?

**No. The statement is DISPROVED in ZFC.** There is a two-coloring \(c\) for which **every uncountable** set \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\) has two distinct-element sums of **different colors**. This is stronger than simply ruling out a subset of cardinality \(\aleph_1\). The original conjecture and the counterexample are catalogued at [Erdős Problem #965](https://www.erdosproblems.com/965).

The order of quantifiers is fundamental:
\[
\exists c:\mathbb R\to\{0,1\}\quad
\forall A\subseteq\mathbb R\text{ uncountable}\quad
\exists\{a,b\},\{u,v\}\in[A]^2:
c(a+b)\ne c(u+v).
\]
A single adversarial coloring refutes Erdős's universal claim. It does not imply that every coloring lacks a large monochromatic sumset.

## An elementary contrasting example

Consider the two-coloring \(c(x)=1\) for \(x>0\) and \(c(x)=0\) for \(x\le0\). Let \(A=(1,2)\). This set has cardinality continuum and every sum of two members lies in \((2,4)\), hence is colored \(1\). Therefore **some** colorings admit an enormous monochromatic pair-sum set.

This example does not contradict the negative theorem: the theorem asserts the existence of a *different*, carefully constructed coloring for which **no** uncountable monochromatic pair-sum set exists.

There are two distinctions that must remain visible. First, the summands must be **distinct**: diagonal sums \(a+a\) are excluded. The family of distinct pair sums is
\[
\operatorname{FS}_2(A)=\{a+b:a,b\in A,\ a\ne b\},
\]
not the full algebraic sumset \(A+A\) allowing repetitions. Second, the original problem is about an \(\aleph_1\)-sized subset. A theorem formulated for **every uncountable** set implies the original negative answer without the continuum hypothesis. Under the usual ZFC axioms an uncountable set has a subset of cardinality \(\aleph_1\).

## Historical progression

In [Er75b], Erdős reported that methods developed jointly with Hajnal and Rado give a counterexample **assuming the continuum hypothesis (CH)**. Hindman, Leader and Strauss later published a systematic treatment, *Pairwise sums in colourings of the reals*, *Abhandlungen aus dem Mathematischen Seminar der Universität Hamburg* **87** (2017), pp.275–287, [DOI 10.1007/s12188-016-0166-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12188-016-0166-x). They established countercolorings under CH and investigated stronger sums allowing repetition and regularity assumptions.

Péter Komjáth independently obtained an **unconditional** counterexample in *A Certain 2-Coloring of the Reals*, *Real Analysis Exchange* **41** (2016), pp.227–231, [DOI 10.14321/realanalexch.41.1.0227](https://doi.org/10.14321/realanalexch.41.1.0227). Dániel T. Soukup and William Weiss also gave an unconditional ZFC proof in [*Sums and anti-Ramsey colourings of \(\mathbb R\)*](https://danieltsoukup.github.io/academic/finset_colouring.pdf), an author-hosted five-page manuscript. The manuscript identifies Komjáth's independent proof. Its Corollary 3.2 (p.4) states the key stronger conclusion:
\[
\exists F:\mathbb R\to2\
\forall X\subseteq\mathbb R\text{ uncountable}\
\forall N\ge2,\qquad
F\!\left[\left\{\sum_{x\in E}x:E\in[X]^N\right\}\right]=\{0,1\}.
\]
Thus **the same two-coloring simultaneously defeats monochromatic \(N\)-term sums for every fixed integer \(N\ge2\)**. The integer \(N\) counts **distinct** summands and is not a bound on the size of \(X\). Setting \(N=2\) is precisely the negative instance of #965.

The distinction between the two unconditional sources matters: Komjáth's result is **published**; the Soukup–Weiss text is an **author manuscript** with a complete displayed proof but no identified journal publication. Both supply relevant provenance, but the unpublished manuscript should not be described as a separately refereed theorem.

## How the unconditional construction works

The Soukup–Weiss proof translates additive questions on the reals into questions about unions of finite subsets of the Cantor space \(2^\omega\). Choose a Hamel basis of \(\mathbb R\) over \(\mathbb Q\), indexed by that Cantor space. Every real then has a **finite support** in the basis. After appropriate thinning of an uncountable family, the supports of sums of \(N\) distinct selected reals behave like unions of supports (the \(\Delta\)-system and coefficient bookkeeping are essential).

The key combinatorial coloring in their Theorem 3.1 (p.3) is built from a Sierpiński-style coloring of unordered pairs of branches of the binary tree. For a finite set of branches it picks the pair with the **largest first-difference level**, with a fixed tie-breaking order, and assigns the color of that pair. An uncountable family can be thinned into structured uncountable subfamilies in which both possible orderings of the decisive pair occur. This forces **both colors** among unions of \(N\) distinct members. Lemma 2.1 then transfers the union-coloring theorem to **sums of real numbers**, producing Corollary 3.2.

This is a source-based explanation of the mechanism, not a substitute for checking the \(\Delta\)-system selection, cancellation avoidance and set-theoretic cardinal arithmetic in the original five-page proof. In particular, merely coloring real numbers by a Hamel-basis coefficient or by their sign does **not** prove the result.

## Important boundaries and nearby questions

The theorem requires \(N\ge2\). Its extension to \(N=1\) would be **false**: any two-coloring of the uncountable set \(\mathbb R\) must have an uncountable monochromatic color class. Thus a statement that the negative coloring simultaneously works for *all \(N\ge1\)* is incorrect.

The result also does **not** say that no **countably infinite** \(A\) can have monochromatic distinct pair sums. For **uncountable** \(A\), the same countercoloring also rules out monochromatic *full* sumsets \(A+A\): a monochromatic full sumset would necessarily make the smaller distinct-pair sumset monochromatic. The proof, however, only constructs the coloring through the distinct-summand theorem; it does not establish an independent prohibition for **countably infinite** sets.

The Soukup–Weiss paper's Corollary 2.3 describes a separate independence/consistency phenomenon when trying to require **three** colors to occur on every uncountable sumset. That cardinal-arithmetic qualification must not be imported into the already **ZFC-proved two-color counterexample**.
