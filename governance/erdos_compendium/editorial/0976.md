## The question in plain language

Fix a polynomial \(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\) **irreducible over \(\mathbb Z\)** with degree \(d\ge2\). For each positive integer \(n\), set
\[
F_f(n)=P^+\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n}f(m)\right),
\]
where \(P^+(M)\) is the greatest prime divisor of \(|M|\), and \(P^+(1)=1\). An irreducible polynomial of degree at least two has **no integer root**, so the product is never zero. The equivalent and often clearer description is
\[
F_f(n)=\max_{1\le m\le n}P^+(f(m)).
\]
Erdős asked how rapidly \(F_f(n)\) must grow, and particularly whether for **every fixed** irreducible \(f\) there is an exponent \(c_f>0\) such that
\[
F_f(n)\gg_f n^{1+c_f},
\]
or even \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^d\), with the implied constants and lower cutoff allowed to depend on \(f\). Both questions are **OPEN in full generality**. The stronger uniform quantifier “there is one \(c>0\) that works for all irreducible polynomials” is **not** supplied by the original formulation. [Erdős Problem #976](https://www.erdosproblems.com/976).

The problem is about a **running product**. Proving that one polynomial value somewhere has a large prime factor at arbitrarily large, widely separated inputs may be insufficient for a lower bound that holds for **every sufficiently large** cutoff \(n\).

## A small example

Let \(f(x)=x^2+1\), which is irreducible over \(\mathbb Z\). Its first six positive values are
\[
2,\quad5,\quad10,\quad17,\quad26,\quad37.
\]
Factoring these gives
\[
P^+(2)=2,\quad P^+(5)=5,\quad P^+(10)=5,\quad
P^+(17)=17,\quad P^+(26)=13,\quad P^+(37)=37.
\]
Hence \(F_f(6)=37\). At \(n=10\), the value \(f(10)=101\) is prime, so \(F_f(10)\ge101\). These are finite arithmetic facts, **not** an asymptotic proof of \(F_f(n)\gg n^2\).

Two elementary observations help frame the question.

1. \(F_f(n)\) is **nondecreasing in \(n\)**, because extending a running product cannot remove a prime divisor.
2. Since \(|f(m)|\ll_f n^d\) for \(m\le n\), one always has \(F_f(n)\ll_f n^d\). Thus the stronger conjecture \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^d\) would prove the **exact polynomial-scale order**
\[
F_f(n)\asymp_f n^d,
\]
not merely an improvement on an existing logarithmic factor.

Neither observation gives a nontrivial **lower** bound by itself.

## The strongest general published theorem found

Nagell and Ricci established the classical estimate
\[
F_f(n)\gg_f n\log n.
\]
Erdős's 1952 work strengthens this to a bound of the shape
\[
F_f(n)>n(\log n)^{c_f\log\log\log n}
\]
for sufficiently large \(n\), with a positive constant \(c_f\) depending on the fixed irreducible \(f\). The maintained catalogue suppresses that constant in one display; the printed article's safe theorem has a polynomial-dependent constant. [Historical catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/976); [primary-source audit](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/arithmetic_functions/E0976).

The strongest general theorem located in the source review is due to Gérald Tenenbaum, *Sur une question d'Erdős et Schinzel, II*, **Inventiones Mathematicae 99** (1990), pp.215–224, Theorem 2 (printed p.216):
\[
\boxed{
F_f(n)>n\exp\{(\log n)^\alpha\}
\quad\text{for every fixed }0<\alpha<2-\log4,
}
\]
once \(n\) is sufficiently large for the fixed polynomial and exponent. Here
\[
2-\log4\approx0.6137056.
\]
The range and the order of quantification matter: \(\alpha\) must be **fixed before taking \(n\to\infty\)**; the source does not give a bound uniform in \(\alpha\) as it approaches its endpoint. [Tenenbaum paper bibliographic record](https://geodesic.mathdoc.fr/item/IM_1990__99_1_143757/); [Theorem 2 source examination](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/library/arithmetic_functions/tenenbaum_1990_sur_une_question_erdos_schinzel_ii/theorem_2).

This theorem is a major gain over \(n\log n\), but it is **not** a fixed-power gain, since
\[
\exp((\log n)^\alpha)
=n^{(\log n)^{\alpha-1}}=n^{o(1)}
\qquad(\alpha<1).
\]
In particular, a bound \(F_f(n)\ge n^{1+c}\) for a fixed \(c>0\) cannot be inferred by relabeling the subpower factor.

Erdős's 1965 report described a related stronger bound with an unpublished complicated proof; the later literature established an appropriate general form rigorously. An unpublished historical assertion should not be recorded as a separately verified theorem.

## Important stronger results for special polynomials

The general conjecture remains open **despite** several substantial special-family advances. Each theorem must retain its own algebraic hypotheses and quantifiers.

- For the quadratic \(f(t)=t^2+1\), Alexandru Pascadi's published **Forum of Mathematics, Pi 14** (2026), article e8, proves \(P^+(m^2+1)>m^{1.3}\) for infinitely many \(m\). More importantly **for the running-product question**, the proof contains a stronger uniform dyadic assertion:
\[
P^+\left(\prod_{x\le m\le2x}(m^2+1)\right)\ge x^{1.30008}
\]
for every sufficiently large \(x\). This implies \(F_{x^2+1}(n)\gg n^{1.30008}\) for every sufficiently large \(n\): take \(x\) near \(n/2\). The infinitude result **alone** would not justify that conclusion. [Published paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/forum-of-mathematics-pi/article/large-sieve-inequalities-for-exceptional-maass-forms-and-the-greatest-prime-factor-of-n21/DC9B30CB4824955671C7B018C473863A); [dyadic-theorem provenance](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/library/arithmetic_functions/pascadi_2026_large_sieve_exceptional_maass_greatest_prime_factor/dyadic_product_bound).
- For certain **quartics** with cyclic or dihedral Galois group, Dartyge and Maynard obtained a positive-proportion large-prime-factor theorem, implying \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^{1+c_f}\). An earlier result of de la Bretèche covers Klein-four Galois quartics. Those theorems are **not** a solution for arbitrary irreducible quartics. [Research synthesis](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/arithmetic_functions/E0976).
- For **monic irreducible cubic polynomials**, Ermoshin's June 2026 arXiv revision (arXiv:2602.03642v3) claims a uniform dyadic positive-proportion power gain for each fixed cubic, implying \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^{1+c_f}\). As a **recent preprint** it needs separate mathematical review; it does **not** prove the degree-scale \(n^3\) lower bound or cover arbitrary nonmonic polynomials.
- An April 2026 note by Aron Bhalla derives \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^d\) **conditionally** on an unproved, strong quantitative prime-values hypothesis for irreducible polynomials. The conclusion is only conditional; no proof of that prime-values hypothesis is claimed. [Conditional theorem and hypotheses](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/arithmetic_functions/E0976/claims/2026_04_16_bhalla).

The presence of positive exponents for named quadratic, cubic or quartic subclasses does **not** settle the original “for every irreducible \(f\)” question.

## What the main difficulty is

Write \(P^+(m)\) for the largest prime factor. The desired bound asks for a **large prime in the factorization of at least one value \(f(m)\) with \(m\le n\)**, at every sufficiently large scale. A polynomial of degree \(d\) has values typically of size \(n^d\), yet the sizes of the values alone say nothing about whether their factors are all relatively small.

The proven general method uses information about **divisors of polynomial values in short multiplicative intervals**. Tenenbaum's divisors-in-interval theorems show that many values have factors in strategically chosen ranges, yielding a prime-factor gain beyond \(n\). To obtain a fixed power \(n^{1+c}\) for all irreducible \(f\), one needs much stronger uniform exclusion of polynomials whose values remain smooth over an entire dyadic block. For higher degrees, relevant sums and root-of-polynomial congruences reach moduli large enough that elementary averaging loses control.

A local computation could test an alleged analytic inequality but cannot certify a positive exponent independent of the cutoff \(n\).
