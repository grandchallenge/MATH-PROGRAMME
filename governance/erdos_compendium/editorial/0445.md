## The question in plain language

Fix a prime $p$ and consider the residues of integers in a short interval $(n,n+L)$. Erdős Problem 445 asks whether this interval always contains two numbers $a,b$ that are multiplicative inverses modulo $p$ once its length is just a little larger than $\sqrt p$. Precisely, for **every** real $c>1/2$, does every sufficiently large prime $p$ have the property that for **every** integer shift $n\geq0$ one can find

$$
n<a,b<n+p^c,\qquad ab\equiv1\pmod p?
$$

The modulus is a prime and the interval is open at both endpoints. The quantifier order matters: a threshold on $p$ may depend on $c$, but the conclusion must hold for all shifts $n$, not merely for some conveniently chosen interval. The two numbers need not be distinct in the stated problem, although a distinct-pair example can be used for illustration.

The threshold $1/2$ is natural because multiplying two sets of about $\sqrt p$ residues produces about $p$ ordered pairs, but counting heuristics are not proof that a particular residue is represented in every translated short interval.

## A small example

Take $p=7$, $n=7$ and $c=4/5$. Since $7^{4/5}>4$, the open interval $(7,7+7^{4/5})$ contains the integers $8,9,10,11$. Choosing $a=9$ and $b=11$ yields $ab=99\equiv1\pmod7$, so this short interval has a valid pair of **distinct** inverses. Both inequalities can be checked exactly after comparing $7^{4/5}$ with the integer $4$.

Another elementary example is $p=5,c=1,n=1$: the open interval $(1,6)$ contains $2,3$, and $2\cdot3=6\equiv1\pmod5$. These are exact finite witnesses. They do not test the hard universal quantifier for every $n$ at every sufficiently large prime.

The inverse condition can be checked either by multiplying the residues or by computing $a^{-1}\pmod p$. It is important that $a$ and $b$ themselves are integers in the translated interval, rather than unrestricted representatives of their residue classes.

## What is known

The question appears in Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 89. Heilbronn established an unpublished partial result for exponents sufficiently close to $1$. Heath-Brown's [Arithmetic applications of Kloosterman sums](https://www.erdosproblems.com/445), published in 2000, improved the range: the desired interval-inverse property holds for **every fixed $c>3/4$** and all sufficiently large primes, uniformly in the starting position $n$.

The full range $c>1/2$ remains **OPEN** on the maintained [Erdős Problem #445](https://www.erdosproblems.com/445) page. Kloosterman sums detect cancellation in bilinear modular inverses and can force solutions in intervals that are longer than the conjectured threshold. The existing analytic bounds do not automatically extend to the square-root barrier. In particular, a theorem for $c>3/4$ cannot be restated as a theorem for $c>1/2$ by changing notation or relying on numerical evidence.

Related MathOverflow discussion concerns small residue classes and their reciprocals. That community discussion is context, not an unconditional proof of the remaining exponent range.

## What remains

A bounded experiment can fix small primes and rational exponents, enumerate starting positions modulo $p$, and find the shortest translated interval containing a reciprocal pair. The property repeats modulo $p$, so examining $0\leq n<p$ is enough for a fixed prime and integer interval length, although real endpoint conventions still need care. The fixture should include the distinct witness $(9,11)$ for $p=7$, $n=7$, $c=4/5$.

The mathematical source task should map Heath-Brown's theorem into the pinned $c>3/4$ formal statement, checking all quantifier orders and whether the interval endpoints are strict. Success is exact computational agreement and primary-source fidelity; failure includes allowing integers only congruent to interval members, choosing $n$ after seeing a witness, or promoting $c>3/4$ to $c>1/2$. Stop after the finite and statement check, retaining the main question as open.

The central unresolved difficulty is uniformity in the translate: an argument that produces one favorable interval for each prime does not satisfy the requirement for every $n$. Likewise, a finite check of all residue shifts for one small prime, though exact, cannot establish that the prime threshold works for all sufficiently large primes.
