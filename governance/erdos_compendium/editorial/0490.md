## The question in plain language

Choose two subsets $A,B$ of the positive integers up to $N$. We require that every ordered choice of one element from each set gives a **different product**:

$$
ab=a'b'\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=a'\ \text{and}\ b=b'
\qquad (a,a'\in A,\ b,b'\in B).
$$

Thus the multiplication map from the Cartesian product $A\times B$ into the positive integers is *injective*. It is not enough for the elements of $A$ to be distinct and those of $B$ to be distinct separately. For example, $A=\{2,3\}$ and $B=\{4,6\}$ fail because $2\cdot6=3\cdot4$.

Erdős asked whether there is an **absolute constant** $C$ such that, for all sufficiently large $N$, every such pair satisfies

$$
\boxed{|A|\,|B|\le C\frac{N^2}{\log N}.}
$$

Here $\log$ is the natural logarithm. The question is about the maximum possible product of the *sizes* of two sets, under the requirement that **none** of their cross-products collide. Its answer is **yes**.

This is substantially stronger than the elementary count $|A||B|\le N^2$. The additional logarithm reflects restrictions imposed by the distribution of prime divisors across the two sets.

## A small example

A construction explains why the logarithm in the upper bound has the right order of magnitude. Take

$$
A=\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor\},\qquad
B=\{p:N/2<p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}\}.
$$

If $ap=a'p'$ with $a,a'\in A$ and $p,p'\in B$, the prime $p>N/2$ divides $a'p'$. It cannot divide $a'\le N/2$, hence $p=p'$, and then $a=a'$. Thus **every cross-product is distinct**.

By the prime number theorem, the number of primes in $(N/2,N]$ is asymptotic to $N/(2\log N)$. Therefore this example has

$$
|A||B|\sim\frac{N^2}{4\log N}.
$$

No uniform upper bound of strictly smaller *order* than $N^2/\log N$ can hold. But the factor $1/4$ is only the constant achieved by this simple construction; it is **not** claimed to be the best constant.

In contrast, $A=\{2,3\}$, $B=\{4,6\}$ has four ordered input pairs but only three distinct products: $8,12,18$. The repeated product $12$ demonstrates why simple cardinality counting is insufficient without the injectivity hypothesis.

## What is known

### Historical formulation

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p. 237, Problem I.29, Erdős describes sequences with elements bounded by $\sqrt n$ and asks for $xy\ll n/\log n$ when all their pairwise cross-products are distinct. Substituting $N\approx\sqrt n$ translates this to the modern order $N^2/\log N$. The exact primary page was visually inspected.

Szemerédi established the affirmative bound in *On a problem of P. Erdős*, *Journal of Number Theory* **8** (1976), 264–270. Its attribution is documented by the resolving authors, although that original Szemerédi paper was **not independently retrieved or proof-checked** in this audit.

### Accessible complete primary proof (1976)

A complete alternative published proof appears in P. Erdős and A. Szemerédi, *On multiplicative representations of integers*, *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society, Series A* **21** (1976), 418–427, [original paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1976-24.pdf). Theorem 1 on printed p. 421 states the exact distinct-products result with an absolute constant:

$$
kl<C\frac{x^2}{\log x},
$$

where $1\le a_1<\cdots<a_k\le x$, $1\le b_1<\cdots<b_l\le x$, and all $kl$ products $a_ib_j$ are distinct. The proof occupies printed pp. 421–423, and those pages were directly inspected.

The proof combines several number-theoretic counting ideas. It discards elements highly divisible by primes not sufficiently represented in the other set, retaining a positive fraction of each original sequence. The remaining primes are sorted according to whether they are associated with one or both sequences. If too many prime factors were associated with both, removing them from pairs of products would yield more pairs than the distinctness assumption allows. Brun-type sieve estimates and Mertens estimates constrain the remaining possibilities, giving the asserted bound. In the original source, this argument is equations (10)–(20).

The printed proof establishes the main inequality, not the exact leading constant. The audit distinguishes a **direct inspection of the published argument** from an independent reconstruction of every analytic sieve bound and its hypotheses.

### The separate sharp-constant problem

Erdős also asked whether the sequence of normalized extremal quantities

$$
M(N)=\max_{\substack{A,B\subseteq[1,N]\\
(a,b)\mapsto ab\ \mathrm{injective}}}|A||B|,
\qquad
\frac{M(N)\log N}{N^2}
$$

has a limit and, if so, what it equals. The 1976 Erdős–Szemerédi paper discusses a stronger conjecture and more elaborate lower constructions. The maintained [Erdős #490 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/490) cites the later question as related to [#896](https://www.erdosproblems.com/896).

An order-of-magnitude bound does **not** imply convergence of the normalized maximum. This subsequent question must therefore remain separate from the **PROVED** original statement.
