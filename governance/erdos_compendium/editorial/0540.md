**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #540 is **PROVED (LEAN reported externally)**. Erdős and Heilbronn conjectured that a set of at least a constant times $\sqrt N$ **distinct residues modulo $N$** must contain a **nonempty subset whose sum is zero**. Endre Szemerédi proved the conjecture in 1970, more generally for any finite abelian group. Subsequent work identified the sharp threshold for prime moduli.

## The question in plain language

Fix a positive integer $N$. A residue modulo $N$ is an integer considered up to multiples of $N$. For a set

$$
A\subseteq\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z
$$

we may choose a subset $S\subseteq A$, using each element at most once, and add its elements modulo $N$.

A **nonempty zero-sum subset** is a choice with

$$
S\ne\varnothing,\qquad \sum_{a\in S}a\equiv0\pmod N.
$$

The Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture asked whether there is one **absolute constant** $C>0$ such that for every $N\ge1$ and every set of **distinct residue classes** with

$$
\boxed{|A|\ge C\sqrt N,}
$$

one can always find such a subset. The answer is yes. The threshold concerns a *set*, not a sequence in which repeated residues count as different indexed terms; that distinction is important for the adjacent problem #541.

If $0\in A$, the singleton $\{0\}$ is already a valid witness. The challenge is to force a zero sum when the residues are nonzero and all different.

## A small example

Consider the group $\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z$.

The set $A=\{1,2,3\}$ has a nonempty zero-sum subset only if one of its seven nonempty subset sums vanishes modulo seven. Those sums are $1,2,3,3,4,5,6$, none zero. Therefore $A$ is **zero-sum-free**.

Adding $4$ creates an immediate witness: $\{3,4\}$ has sum $7\equiv0\pmod7$.

For any finite set of residues $A$ define its **nonempty subset-sum set**

$$
\Sigma^*(A)=\left\{\sum_{a\in S}a:
\varnothing\ne S\subseteq A\right\}\subseteq\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z.
$$

Then $A$ is zero-sum-free exactly when $0\notin\Sigma^*(A)$.

There is an exact recurrence. When a new, distinct residue $a$ is adjoined,

$$
\boxed{
\Sigma^*(A\cup\{a\})
=\Sigma^*(A)\cup\{a\}\cup\bigl(a+\Sigma^*(A)\bigr).
}
$$

The three possibilities correspond to a chosen subset that omits $a$, is the singleton $\{a\}$, or includes $a$ together with other elements. Computing this recurrence with integers modulo $N$ is a useful independent finite verification method.

## What is known

### The original statement and Szemerédi's theorem

In *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, Chapter 12 of *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), [printed p.126](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=10), Erdős presents the conjecture jointly attributed to Heilbronn. The **original page was visually inspected**. Erdős explicitly states that Szemerédi had proved it in 1970, and that the proof works for arbitrary **finite abelian groups**, not merely cyclic ones.

The primary paper is Endre Szemerédi, *On a conjecture of Erdös and Heilbronn*, *Acta Arithmetica* **17** (1970), 227–229, [DOI 10.4064/aa-17-3-227-229](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-17-3-227-229). The [original publisher record](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/acta-arithmetica/all/17/3/97245/on-a-conjecture-of-erdos-and-heilbronn) was checked. Direct retrieval of the complete paper produced HTTP 403; the full proof has **not** been independently reconstructed.

The general theorem asserts that some constant $C$ works for every finite abelian group $G$ of order $N$ and every subset with at least $C\sqrt N$ distinct elements. It proves a **square-root-order bound**, not the best constant.

John E. Olson proved the prime-modulus case in *An addition theorem modulo* $p$, *Journal of Combinatorial Theory* (1968), 45–52, with the sufficient condition $|A|>\sqrt{4p-3}$. His full original proof was not reread here.

### Refinements and the exact prime-modulus answer

Yahya Ould Hamidoune and Gilles Zémor, *On zero-free subset sums*, *Acta Arithmetica* **78** (1996), 143–152, established an asymptotically sharper sufficient bound

$$
|A|>(1+o(1))\sqrt{2N}
$$

for arbitrary finite abelian groups. The error term matters: this is **not** an exact finite‑$N$ threshold.

For prime moduli, Éric Balandraud's *An addition theorem and maximal zero-sum free sets in* $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, *Israel Journal of Mathematics* **188** (2012), 405–429, [primary author preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0907.3492), gives an exact answer. Let $k_p$ be the greatest integer $k\ge0$ satisfying

$$
\boxed{\frac{k(k+1)}2<p.}
$$

Then the maximum cardinality of a **zero-sum-free set of distinct residues modulo prime $p$** is exactly $k_p$.

The complete original seventeen-page preprint was retrieved, and **Theorem 9 on PDF p.16 was directly checked**. The lower construction is simple: the set

$$
A=\{1,2,\ldots,k_p\}\pmod p
$$

has all its positive integer subset sums between $1$ and $k_p(k_p+1)/2$, strictly less than $p$. It therefore contains no zero-sum nonempty subset.

The converse is much deeper. Balandraud proves an addition theorem controlling the growth of subset-sum sets; a consequence is that $k_p+1$ distinct nonzero residues cannot remain zero-sum-free. This resolves Selfridge's exact conjecture **for primes**.

The strict inequality defining $k_p$ is essential. For $p=7$, the first three positive integers have total sum $6<7$ and avoid zero; adding a fourth residue forces a zero-sum subset. None of these statements licenses treating composite moduli as though they obey the identical closed formula.

The conjecture is mathematically solved. Stronger proof provenance would require obtaining Szemerédi's full original paper and compiling the exact external Lean 4.29.1 source with immutable imports and an audited axiom report.
