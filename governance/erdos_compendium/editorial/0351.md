## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 351 asks whether a sequence built from a rational-coefficient polynomial is strongly complete. Let $p$ be a non-constant polynomial with rational coefficients and positive leading coefficient, and form

$$
A=\{p(n)+1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

The question is whether, after deleting any finite set $B$ of values from $A$, every sufficiently large integer can be written as a sum of distinct remaining values. “Sufficiently large” may depend on $p$ and on $B$. The quantifiers matter: this is an eventual assertion for every admissible polynomial and every finite deletion, not merely a statement that many integers have representations.

The live problem page now uses “integers,” not “rationals.” That correction matters. Representing every sufficiently large rational would be a much stronger and different property, called rational completeness in the discussion. The page was last edited 10 May 2026 and currently shows nine comments, no proof exposition, and no proof claim. This article records source evidence and boundaries; it does not decide accepted coverage.

## A small example

Take $p(n)=n$. The three distinct indices $2,3,6$ satisfy

$$
2+3+6=11,
\qquad
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16=1.
$$

Therefore the corresponding values of $A$ give the exact calculation

$$
\left(2+\frac12\right)+\left(3+\frac13\right)+\left(6+\frac16\right)=11+1=12.
$$

This is exact rational arithmetic, not a decimal approximation. A second check from Graham’s displayed examples is $2+4+6+12=24$ and $1/2+1/4+1/6+1/12=1$, giving a sum of values equal to $25$.

These calculations illustrate the useful reduction: if distinct positive integers $a_i$ have sum $N$ and reciprocal sum $1$, then the values $a_i+1/a_i$ sum to $N+1$. They do not establish an eventual theorem. A finite list neither covers all large integers nor shows that the indices can avoid an arbitrary finite deletion. The latter is exactly why the “for every lower bound on the $a_i$” clause in Graham’s theorem is important.

## What is known

The primary source I read is R. L. Graham, “A theorem on partitions,” *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society* 3 (1963), 435–441. On p. 435, Theorem 1 states that every integer exceeding 77 is a sum of distinct positive integers whose reciprocals sum to 1. On pp. 437–439, Theorem 2 states that for every integer $m$ there is a threshold $r(m)$ such that every integer $N>r(m)$ has a representation by distinct positive integers $a_i$ with $a_i\ge m$ and $\sum a_i=N$, $\sum 1/a_i=1$. Substituting $p(n)=n$ proves the strong-completeness statement for that special polynomial, including finite deletions.

The live page cites Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 58, as the original catalogue source. It also cites Graham’s “Complete sequences of polynomial values,” *Duke Mathematical Journal* (1964), 275–285, for a related characterization of complete polynomial-value sequences. Those references contextualize the question but do not, from the material inspected here, supply a proof of the full strong statement for every rational polynomial.

For $p(n)=n^2$, the live page reports a route through Graham’s theorem and Max A. Alekseyev’s 2019 paper “On partitions into squares of distinct integers whose reciprocals sum to 1,” pp. 213–221, cited on Problem 283. The report is plausible source evidence, but this audit did not independently read Alekseyev’s paper or verify the reduction. A discussion post also points to a short write-up; that is an external report, not GCL certification.
