## The question in plain language

How many colours might a graph need if it has **no triangles**?

The chromatic number $\chi(G)$ is the fewest colours needed to colour vertices so that no edge joins vertices of the same colour. A triangle-free graph cannot contain three vertices all mutually adjacent. [Erdős Problem #1104](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1104) asks for

$$
f(n)=\max\{\chi(G):|V(G)|=n,\ G\text{ triangle-free}\}.
$$

The main open issue is the sharp constant in its large‑$n$ growth, not whether high-chromatic triangle-free graphs exist.

## A small example

The five-vertex cycle $C_5$ contains no triangle but needs three colours. Applying the classical **Mycielski construction** adds five companion vertices and an apex, yielding a graph with

$$
|V|=11,\qquad |E|=20,\qquad \chi(G)=4.
$$

An exact program enumerates all its triangles and finds **zero**, then uses chromatic backtracking to verify no three-colouring exists and a four-colouring does. Thus $f(11)\ge4$. This is a finite illustration, not a proof of the asymptotic optimal constant.

## What is known

The maintained catalogue reports the sharpest bounds currently incorporated there:

$$
(1-o(1))\sqrt{\frac n{\log n}}
\ \le f(n)\le\
(2+o(1))\sqrt{\frac n{\log n}}.
$$

The upper bound is attributed to **Davies and Illingworth (2022)**, and the lower bound to **Hefty, Horn, King and Pfender (2025)**. The [published Davies–Illingworth journal abstract](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10189639/1/chi_Ramsey_SIDMA.pdf) confirms an improvement by a factor $\sqrt2$ over the earlier $2\sqrt2$ upper constant.

The *order* $\sqrt{n/\log n}$ is therefore established. Determining the limiting sharp leading coefficient remains **open**.

There is also an analogous edge-count problem, with extremal order roughly $(m/(\log m)^2)^{1/3}$. It has a different parameter and is not interchangeable with $f(n)$.
