## The question in plain language

Choose a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers

$$
a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots.
$$

Call it an irrationality sequence if, for **every** choice of positive integers $t_1,t_2,\ldots$, the series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t_n a_n}
$$

is irrational. The multipliers are positive: $t_n\geq1$. They are not allowed to be zero or negative, and the requirement is universal rather than existential. Erdős asks how slowly an irrationality sequence can grow.

The question is subtler than asking whether one rapidly convergent series is irrational. The sequence $a_n=2^{2^n}$ works for every positive multiplier sequence, while merely requiring $a_n\to\infty$ does not suffice. For instance, the factorial sequence has a positive multiplier choice that makes the sum rational. The target is therefore a growth boundary for a whole family of adversarial series.

## A small example

For $a_n=n!$, choose $t_n=n+2$. Every multiplier is positive, but

$$
\frac{1}{(n+2)n!}=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}-\frac{1}{(n+2)!}.
$$

Consequently the first $N$ terms sum to

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(n+2)n!}=\frac12-\frac{1}{(N+2)!},
$$

and the infinite sum is $1/2$. This is a concrete disproof of the claim that factorial growth automatically gives the required universal irrationality.

For the positive example $a_n=2^{2^n}$, the first three denominators are $4,16,256$, so with $t_n=1$ the first three terms are

$$
\frac14+\frac1{16}+\frac1{256}=\frac{81}{256}.
$$

The remaining terms are smaller than the geometric tail beginning with $2^{-16}$, hence are less than $1/65535$. The following independent calculation checks both exact identities and the tail bound:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import factorial

for N in (1, 2, 5, 10):
    s = sum((Fraction(1, (n + 2) * factorial(n))
             for n in range(1, N + 1)), Fraction())
    assert s == Fraction(1, 2) - Fraction(1, factorial(N + 2))

a = [2 ** (2 ** n) for n in range(1, 4)]
assert a == [4, 16, 256]
assert sum((Fraction(1, x) for x in a), Fraction()) == Fraction(81, 256)
assert Fraction(1, 2**16) / (1 - Fraction(1, 2**16)) == Fraction(1, 65535)
```

This verifies finite arithmetic and a convergence estimate only. It does not prove irrationality.

## What is known

Erdős’s primary proof interface is a divisibility theorem, not a characterization of the slowest possible sequence. In “Some problems and results on the irrationality of the sum of infinite series,” printed p. 3 defines property $P$: whenever $m_k>0$ and $m_k$ is divisible by $n_k$, the sum of $1/m_k$ is irrational. Theorem 3 on printed p. 6 proves that $n_k=2^{2^k}$ has this property. Substituting $m_k=t_k2^{2^k}$ gives the Problem #262 example for every positive integer sequence $(t_k)$.

The proof’s useful interface is exact: it accepts arbitrary positive multiples of the prescribed $n_k$. Its limitations are equally important. Erdős uses denominator and least-common-multiple estimates after rearranging a sequence of denominators; the theorem proves one explicit construction, not that its double-exponential scale is optimal. The same paper records uncertainty about slower sequences having property $P$.

Hančl’s 1991 paper supplies the principal obstruction. In Theorem 2 on p. 100, a sequence containing a subsequence $(c_n)$ with a bound of the form

$$
c_n<K2^{2^{n-F(n)}},\qquad F(n)<n,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-F(n)}<\infty,
$$

admits positive integer multipliers producing a prescribed sufficiently small positive target. Taking a rational target makes the sequence fail the irrationality-sequence property. Corollary 2 on p. 102 gives the simpler consequence

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log_2\log_2 a_n}{n}<1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(a_n)\text{ is not an irrationality sequence}.
$$

Thus the classical record places a sharp-looking double-exponential scale between a proved example and a broad impossibility region, but it does not establish the exact answer to “how slowly.”

## What remains

The central mathematical remainder is a characterization, or a sharper lower and upper growth boundary, for irrationality sequences. Erdős’s example and Hančl’s obstruction do not decide whether a substantially slower sequence exists, nor whether the displayed limsup threshold is sufficient in an appropriate sense. The live page’s phrase “essentially solved” should therefore be read as a report of strong partial results, not completion of the original optimization question.

The documentary and formal remainder is to reconcile the contradictory catalogue metadata. A future bounded successor could identify the source of the “solved (Lean)” label, formalize the exact predicate with $t_n\geq1$, and replay any claimed development in its actual toolchain with its axiom and `sorry` report. The elementary factorial identity and finite geometric estimate are suitable interface lemmas; neither discharges Erdős’s or Hančl’s analytic arguments. Until that evidence exists, claims of Lean formalization remain external or unresolved.
