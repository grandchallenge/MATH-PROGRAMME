## The question in plain language

Take the reciprocals $1,1/2,1/3,\ldots,1/N$. We seek the largest set of denominators $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ for which choosing or not choosing each reciprocal always gives a different total. In symbols, if $S$ and $T$ are subsets of $A$, then

$$
\sum_{n\in S}\frac{1}{n}=\sum_{n\in T}\frac{1}{n}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S=T.
$$

Write the maximum size as $R(N)$. The empty choice counts, and the sums are ordinary rational sums: there is no approximation or rounding. The question recorded by Erdős and Graham asks for the size of $R(N)$, or, in modern language, for the growth of the largest dissociated subset of these particular unit fractions with coefficients restricted to $0$ and $1$.

This is closely related to, but not identical with, counting every distinct subsum of all the reciprocals up to $N$. If $E_N$ denotes the set of all such subsums, then every admissible $A$ contributes $2^{|A|}$ different elements of $E_N$, so

$$
2^{R(N)}\leq |E_N|.
$$

That inequality is useful for upper bounds on $R(N)$, but a large number of total subsums does not automatically exhibit one large admissible set.

## A small example

For $N=4$, the whole set $A=\{1,2,3,4\}$ already works. Multiplying every sum by the common denominator $12$ turns the four available weights into $12,6,4,3$. Exhaustive exact subset addition gives the sixteen values

$$
0,3,4,6,7,9,10,12,13,15,16,18,19,21,22,25.
$$

They are all different, so the sixteen rational subsums are all different. Since no subset of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ can have more than four elements, this verifies $R(4)=4$ exactly. The calculation is finite and uses integers after clearing denominators; it does not say that $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ works for larger $N$, nor does it determine the asymptotic size of $R(N)$.

The obstruction is an exact equality between two different subcollections. For example, once a denominator set contains a suitable relation such as $1/2=1/3+1/6$, it cannot be admissible. The same principle applies to longer relations, which is why testing pairs alone is insufficient.

## What is known

The primary 1975 paper of Bleicher and Erdős studies the number $S(N)=|E_N|$ and constructs special denominators whose subsums are distinct. Its lemma on the final pages proves uniqueness for sums formed from the constructed set $Q(N)$; consequently that construction supplies an admissible set and a lower bound for $R(N)$, not merely a count of unrelated subsums. Their corollary gives iterated-logarithm lower bounds, under explicit conditions on the iterated logarithms.

Their companion 1976 paper gives corresponding upper estimates for the number of distinct subsums. Together with $2^{R(N)}\leq S(N)$, these results place $R(N)$ in the same broad iterated-logarithmic scale as $\log S(N)$, but the exact parameter choices and the transfer from $S(N)$ to $R(N)$ must be read with their hypotheses intact.

A newer primary source is Bettin, Grenié, Molteni, and Sanna, *A lower bound for the number of Egyptian fractions* (arXiv:2509.10030, version dated 12 September 2025). Their Theorem 1 is a lower bound for $\log |E_N|$, improving the admissible condition on the iterated logarithm and the leading constant. It is important to state its target correctly: the theorem is about $E_N$, not by itself a theorem giving the exact value of $R(N)$.

The live Erdős Problems page, last edited 16 July 2026, reports that the correct order of magnitude of $R(N)$ is known and calls the problem solved in that order-of-growth sense. Its discussion records that the lower bound is implicit in the newer Egyptian-fraction work, while the upper bound is attributed to a GPT-generated argument linked through Problem #320. That is a current external report, not a human-authored primary proof that was independently checked in this audit. The more precise asymptotic questions remain described there as open.

## What remains

A useful next step is a source-locked semantic audit of the claimed order-of-growth theorem. It should write down one exact function $g(N)$, the quantifiers on all iterated-logarithm parameters, and separate the lower construction for $R(N)$ from estimates for $S(N)$. It should then trace the upper argument linked from Problem #320 to a stable, citable mathematical document and check every conversion between logarithm bases and every range condition.

Finite computation remains useful for falsification and for formalization. Exact rational enumeration can extend the verified initial values and test candidate forbidden relations, but it cannot establish an asymptotic theorem. Likewise, translating the Lean definition into a proved theorem would certify only the formal statement actually encoded; it would not certify that the `answer(sorry)` placeholder has been replaced by the mathematically intended asymptotic claim.
