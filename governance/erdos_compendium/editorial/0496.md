**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #496 is historically classified **PROVED** by Margulis's resolution of the Oppenheim conjecture, but the maintained statement has a **material sign-domain error**. For the displayed quadratic form to be indefinite one must require $\alpha>0$. As written, with all irrational *real* $\alpha$, the statement is false.

## The question in plain language

Consider the quadratic expression

$$
Q_\alpha(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-\alpha z^2,
$$

and ask whether it can be made arbitrarily close to zero with **positive integer** inputs $x,y,z$:

$$
\forall \varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists x,y,z\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\qquad
|Q_\alpha(x,y,z)|<\varepsilon.
$$

The [maintained Erdős #496](https://www.erdosproblems.com/496) asks this for every *irrational real* $\alpha$ and assigns status PROVED. But that cannot be right without an additional **sign restriction**: for $\alpha<0$,

$$
Q_\alpha(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+|\alpha|z^2
\ge 2+|\alpha|.
$$

There is no positive-integer solution for $\varepsilon=1$. In particular, the irrational choice $\alpha=-\sqrt2$ is an immediate counterexample to the **literal** all-real-domain formulation.

The mathematical content behind the Oppenheim conjecture concerns **indefinite** quadratic forms, those taking both positive and negative real values. This particular form is indefinite when $\alpha>0$: one sees $Q_\alpha(1,0,0)=1$ but $Q_\alpha(0,0,1)=-\alpha<0$. The coefficient ratio involving irrational $\alpha$ also prevents this form from being a real scalar multiple of a rational-coefficient quadratic form. These are exactly the standard structural features relevant to the Oppenheim theorem.

This source correction does not imply that Margulis's theorem is false. It distinguishes a proved theorem with **correct hypotheses** from a catalogue paraphrase whose larger domain contains elementary counterexamples.

## A small example

Choose $\alpha=-\sqrt2$ and any positive integers $x,y,z$. Then

$$
|x^2+y^2+\sqrt2 z^2|
=x^2+y^2+\sqrt2 z^2
\ge 2+\sqrt2>3.
$$

Consequently $\varepsilon=1$ is impossible, and the all-real assertion is disproved by direct algebra.

For a positive irrational coefficient, say $\alpha=\sqrt2$, the two simple real directions give opposite signs:

$$
Q_{\sqrt2}(1,0,0)=1,\qquad
Q_{\sqrt2}(0,0,1)=-\sqrt2.
$$

This shows **indefiniteness**, not by itself the existence of positive *integer* triples with arbitrarily small value. Rational approximations or an ordinary finite search can find small values for some cutoffs, but the universal assertion needs a proof.

The 1961 original paper poses a pair of closely related ternary inequalities:

$$
\left|\alpha(x^2+y^2)-z^2\right|<\varepsilon,
\qquad
\left|x^2+y^2-\alpha z^2\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

It says *integers*, not explicitly *positive integers*, and does not explicitly exclude the all-zero triple in this sentence. That triple would trivially solve both inequalities for every $\alpha$ if allowed. Any mathematically meaningful reading of the question must therefore include **nontrivial integer inputs**. The modern positive-integer version attempts to ensure nontriviality, but introduces the missing-sign problem described above.

## What is known

### Erdős's original formulation

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed pp.238–239, Erdős places these inequalities immediately after the Littlewood conjecture. He cites Davenport and Heilbronn's 1946 treatment of indefinite quadratic forms in five variables and notes that even $\alpha=\sqrt2$ was not resolved in this three-variable setting at the time.

The original 34-page scan was retrieved directly on the authenticated CHAD host, and text from the printed pp.238–239 was extracted and checked. Unlike the maintained single-inequality catalogue entry, Erdős prints **two** inequalities, with $\alpha$ multiplying different terms. The all-zero issue and the sign of $\alpha$ are not explicitly normalized in the quoted original wording. These are real hypothesis questions, not reasons to ignore the surrounding indefinite-form context.

### Margulis's Oppenheim theorem

G. A. Margulis solved the **Oppenheim conjecture**, which asserts that if a nondegenerate indefinite real quadratic form $Q$ in at least three variables is not proportional to a rational form, its values at nonzero integer vectors are dense in $\mathbb R$. The maintained catalogue attributes #496 to Margulis, *Discrete subgroups and ergodic theory*, in *Number Theory, Trace Formulas and Discrete Groups* (1989), pp.377–398.

A direct modern primary confirmation appears in M. Einsiedler, A. Katok and E. Lindenstrauss, *Invariant measures and the set of exceptions to Littlewood's conjecture*, *Annals of Mathematics* **164** (2006), pp.513–560. Its opening page explicitly names Margulis's solution and identifies the essential **indefinite** and dimension-at-least-three setting. The [published journal PDF](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v164-n2-p04.pdf) was inspected.

For **positive irrational** $\alpha$, the three-variable form $Q_\alpha$ meets the standard structural requirements: it is nondegenerate with diagonal entries $1,1,-\alpha$; it is indefinite; and its coefficients are not all proportional to rationals. By Oppenheim's theorem, arbitrarily small nonzero *values* occur on nonzero integer vectors.

**Exact semantic boundary:** the usual Oppenheim statement quantifies over nonzero integer vectors, while the catalogue explicitly demands **strictly positive** $x,y,z$. Reflecting signs changes no value because the form is diagonal, so an integer witness with all three coordinates nonzero can be made positive. But arbitrary nonzero vectors can have a zero coordinate. Therefore the strictly-positive-coordinate adaptation needs either a verified strengthening or a proof that the desired approximating vectors can be taken with every coordinate nonzero. That bridge was **not** independently discharged by this audit. It must not be hidden behind the word “Oppenheim.”
