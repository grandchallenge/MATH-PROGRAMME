## The question in plain language

A set of positive integers is *multiplicatively Sidon* in the sense of Erdős Problem 425 if every product of two **different** elements has a unique representation as such a product. More precisely, for a finite set $A$, whenever $a<b$ and $c<d$ are pairs drawn from $A$, equality $ab=cd$ must imply that the pairs are identical. The order within a pair does not count as a new representation.

Let $F(n)$ be the largest possible number of elements in such a set $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$. Write $\pi(n)$ for the number of primes not exceeding $n$. Erdős asks for a sharp asymptotic formula for the excess $F(n)-\pi(n)$:

$$
F(n)=\pi(n)+(c+o(1))
\frac{n^{3/4}}{(\log n)^{3/2}}
$$

with some fixed constant $c$. A separate, harder-looking extension imposes uniqueness of products of $r$ distinct selected elements and asks for a corresponding upper bound. The questions concern infinite growth rates, not merely constructing one large finite set.

## A small example

A set consisting of $1$ and all primes up to $n$ satisfies the pair-product condition by unique prime factorization. Two different primes multiply to a number with a unique pair of prime factors; multiplying $1$ by a prime instead produces that prime, which cannot equal a product of two distinct primes. Thus $F(n)\geq\pi(n)+1$ for sufficiently large $n$. Erdős's historical work establishes a substantially stronger lower bound.

At a small scale, consider

$$
A=\{1,2,3,4,5,7\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,8\}.
$$

There are $\binom62=15$ unordered pairs of different elements. Direct exact-integer multiplication shows their fifteen products are all distinct. Exhaustively inspecting every one of the $2^8=256$ subsets of $\{1,\dots,8\}$, and rejecting any subset with a repeated unordered-pair product, gives $F(8)=6$. A similar complete search of the $2^{10}=1024$ subsets of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ gives $F(10)=7$. These are independently calculated finite certificates, not evidence for a particular constant $c$.

The predicate for the finite search is transparent: map each two-element subset $\{a,b\}$ to its integer product $ab$, and test whether that map is injective. If two different unordered pairs share a product, the set fails. This definition must be kept separate from variants that allow repeated factors $a=a$ or compare ordered products, because those variants have different trivialities and different possible extremal sizes.

## What is known

The problem has a long history in additive and multiplicative number theory, including Erdős's work in 1938 and 1968, and the account in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 84 of the [historical monograph](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf).

The maintained [Erdős Problems #425](https://www.erdosproblems.com/425) page records a powerful result attributed to Erdős: there are positive constants $c_1\leq c_2$ such that

$$
\pi(n)+c_1\frac{n^{3/4}}{(\log n)^{3/2}}
\leq F(n)\leq
\pi(n)+c_2\frac{n^{3/4}}{(\log n)^{3/2}}
$$

for sufficiently large $n$. The earlier 1938 work supplied the lower-bound direction. These inequalities determine the **order of magnitude** of the excess, but they do not prove that the normalized excess converges to any single constant. That remaining limiting-coefficient question is why the main problem is still listed **OPEN**.

A further question considers sets for which all products of $r$ distinct members are different, and asks whether their size is bounded by

$$
\pi(n)+O\bigl(n^{(r+1)/(2r)}\bigr).
$$

This extension is not obtained merely by substituting an integer $r$ into the pairwise bound. It changes the uniqueness hypothesis and the extremal problem.

There is also a related problem for real numbers. Erdős once conjectured that a large set of real numbers in an interval with all products of four distinct factors separated by at least one must have size $o(x)$. Alexander's construction, described in later sources, disproved that assertion by producing sets of size comparable to $x$. It is a **different** real-number question and should not be treated as a resolution of the integer $F(n)$ asymptotic.

## What remains

A useful bounded task is to compute $F(n)$ exactly for $1\leq n\leq15$ by two independent implementations. The first enumerates all subsets as bitmasks and checks unordered-pair products. The second performs backtracking, maintaining the set of products already used. For each $n$, record the maximum cardinality, one lexicographically specified maximizing set and the number of maximizing sets. The two implementations must agree, including the known fixtures $F(8)=6$ and $F(10)=7$.

The source-concordance component should inspect the 1938 and 1968 primary results and verify the precise hypotheses and constants behind the two-sided asymptotic bounds. It must also keep the $r$ fold extension and Alexander's real-number construction distinct. Acceptance requires exact finite agreement and a theorem-by-theorem source table. Falsification includes an unreported product collision, wrong repeated-factor convention, or inventing a limiting constant from coarse order bounds. The task stops at finite certificates and source normalization; the asymptotic problem remains for mathematical research.
