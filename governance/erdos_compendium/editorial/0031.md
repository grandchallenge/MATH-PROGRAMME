## The question in plain language

An additive complement to a set $A$ is another set $B$ such that the sumset $A+B$ contains every sufficiently large integer. [Erdős Problem #31](https://www.erdosproblems.com/31), proposed by Erdős and Straus, asks whether **every infinite** set of nonnegative integers $A$ has such a complement $B$ of **natural density zero**.

The answer is **yes**, proved by G. G. Lorentz in 1954. The complementary set may depend on $A$, and density zero does not mean finite.

## A small example

Let $A=\{0,2,4,6,\ldots\}$ consist of all even nonnegative integers, and let $B=\{0,1\}$. Their sumset is every nonnegative integer: an even number comes from adding zero and an odd number from adding one. The finite set $B$ has density zero.

This illustration is much easier than the general theorem. An arbitrary infinite $A$ may be extraordinarily sparse, and its complement $B$ need not be finite.

## What is known

Lorentz, *On a problem of additive number theory*, *Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society* **5** (1954), 838–841, proved the affirmative statement. The result is an existence theorem about arbitrary infinite sets: it does not say that the same sparse $B$ works for all $A$, or that $A+B$ covers every nonnegative integer without a finite exceptional prefix.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1300](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1300#issuecomment-6121342304) records the universal-then-existential quantifier order and the parity example. It also notes that the maintained problem registry reports an external Lean verification, but GCL has not replayed its dependencies and axioms. Documentary recognition of Lorentz's theorem is distinct from GCL mathematical certification.

## What remains

The historical problem is settled. Independent formalization work could establish the precise natural-density and eventual-coverage predicates and replay Lorentz's full argument. No new solution to the original question is claimed here.
