## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, form the harmonic sum

$$
H_n=1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac1n.
$$

Let $L_n=\mathrm{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,n)$, the smallest positive integer divisible by every number from $1$ through $n$. Since each denominator divides $L_n$, there is an integer $a_n$ with $H_n=a_n/L_n$. Erdős and Graham ask whether both of the following events happen for infinitely many values of $n$:

$$
\gcd(a_n,L_n)=1
\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad
\gcd(a_n,L_n)>1.
$$

Here “infinitely many” means that no matter how far out one searches, another such $n$ exists. It is not a claim about a large but finite computation.

There is a useful denominator reformulation. Write $H_n=c_n/d_n$ in lowest terms, so $\gcd(c_n,d_n)=1$ and $d_n>0$. Then $d_n$ divides $L_n$, $a_n=c_nL_n/d_n$, and therefore

$$
\gcd(a_n,L_n)=\frac{L_n}{d_n}.
$$

Thus the first half asks whether $d_n=L_n$ infinitely often: the reduced harmonic denominator retains every prime power present in the least common multiple. The second half asks whether $d_n<L_n$ infinitely often.

The original source is not a modern web page but Erdős and Ronald L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, L’Enseignement Mathématique Monographie 28 (1980), p. 34. The book’s wording uses $a/L_n$ and asks for the two gcd behaviours; the later catalogue number “291” is an indexing label, not wording from that page.

## A small example

At $n=2$,

$$
H_2=\frac32,\qquad L_2=2,\qquad a_2=3,\qquad \gcd(a_2,L_2)=1.
$$

At $n=6$,

$$
H_6=\frac{49}{20},\qquad L_6=60,\qquad a_6=147,\qquad \gcd(a_6,L_6)=3.
$$

These examples show both behaviours at small indices, but they do not address infinitude. I independently checked the arithmetic with exact rational numbers in Python, not floating-point approximations:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import gcd, lcm

def values(n):
    h = sum((Fraction(1, k) for k in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
    L = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        L = lcm(L, k)
    a = h.numerator * (L // h.denominator)
    return h, L, a, gcd(a, L)

for n in (2, 6):
    print(n, values(n))
```

The exact output was `(2, Fraction(3, 2), 2, 3, 1)` and `(6, Fraction(49, 20), 60, 147, 3)`. The same calculation verified the unconditional pattern used below for all $n$ in the first five intervals $6\le n<9$, $18\le n<27$, $54\le n<81$, $162\le n<243$, and $486\le n<729$.

## What is known

The second half has an elementary proof. Fix $j\ge1$ and take

$$
2\cdot3^j\le n<3^{j+1}.
$$

The largest power of $3$ not exceeding $n$ is $3^j$, and the only denominators among $1,\ldots,n$ with exactly this power of $3$ are $3^j$ and $2\cdot3^j$. After multiplying $H_n$ by $L_n$, every term whose denominator has a smaller power of $3$ vanishes modulo $3$. The two remaining contributions are proportional modulo $3$ to

$$
1+\frac12\equiv0\pmod 3.
$$

Consequently $3\mid a_n$. Since $n\ge6$, also $3\mid L_n$, so $\gcd(a_n,L_n)>1$. The displayed intervals are disjoint and contain arbitrarily large integers; hence the second event occurs infinitely often, unconditionally.

The first event is different. Peter Shiu’s revised paper describes $d_n=L_n$ infinitely often as a conjecture, and does not prove it. Bing-Ling Wu and Xiao-Hui Yan prove a nearby conditional result: their Theorem 2 states that the set of $n$ with $d_n<L_n$ has upper asymptotic density $1$, assuming that $1/\log p$ is linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$ for every finite set of distinct primes. This is a conditional strengthening of the already-known second half, not a proof that $d_n=L_n$ infinitely often. In particular, neither paper closes the original two-part question.

## What remains

The central unresolved target is the first event: prove that $d_n=L_n$ for infinitely many $n$, or find a mathematically sound obstruction showing that this cannot happen. The elementary $p=3$ argument does not help with that target, because it deliberately produces common divisibility.

The source audit records a useful finite interface. For each prime $p\le n$, let $q=p^e$ be the largest power of $p$ with $q\le n$, and let $k=\lfloor n/q\rfloor$. Then the divisibility of $a_n$ by $p$ can be tested through the numerator of the shorter harmonic sum $H_k$. This reduces a prime-by-prime finite computation, but it does not establish an infinite set of indices with no such prime divisor. The audit also notes a boundary exception: the informal phrase “leading digit $p-1$” applies to odd primes, not to $p=2$.

A bounded successor task is therefore to compute exact $H_n$, $L_n$, $a_n$, and their gcd for $1\le n\le N$ by two independent integer-arithmetic paths, while checking the prime-prefix criterion for every $p\le n$. Agreement can expose implementation or statement errors and produce a reproducible finite certificate. It cannot be promoted into a proof of infinitude, a resolution of the first half, or GCL certification.
