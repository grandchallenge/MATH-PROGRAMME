**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #517 is **OPEN**. It asks whether an entire function with increasingly sparse, nonzero Taylor coefficients must assume **every complex value infinitely often**. Stronger summability assumptions were treated by Fejér and Biernacki, and Pólya settled an important finite-order case. Neither result automatically answers the unrestricted Fabry-gap question.

## The question in plain language

Consider a transcendental entire function with a sparse power series,

$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_kz^{n_k},
\qquad a_k\ne0,
$$

where the exponents are strictly increasing positive integers. The condition

$$
\boxed{\frac{n_k}{k}\longrightarrow\infty}
$$

is called a **Fabry-gap condition**. It says that the number of nonzero Taylor coefficients up to degree $N$ is negligible compared with $N$, in a precise asymptotic sense.

Must it follow that **every** $w\in\mathbb C$ has infinitely many distinct solutions to

$$
f(z)=w?
$$

The order of quantifiers is significant: one fixed function must assume **each** prescribed complex value infinitely often. This is stronger than saying $f$ is surjective or has infinitely many zeros. The function is not assumed to have finite order, nor must its gaps grow monotonically.

The nonzero-coefficient condition matters: inserting infinitely many zero coefficients can produce an artificial sparse index without changing the function. The exponent list must enumerate **actual nonzero terms**.

## A small example

For $f(z)=e^z$, the value $0$ is never attained. Thus the conclusion is **false for arbitrary transcendental entire functions**. But $e^z$ has a nonzero Taylor coefficient in every degree and does not satisfy Fabry gaps.

Even $e^{z^m}$, which omits zero, is not a counterexample. Its positive nonzero exponents form $n_k=mk$, giving

$$
\frac{n_k}{k}=m,
$$

a constant rather than a sequence tending to infinity.

A more instructive sparse exponent sequence is

$$
n_k=k\bigl(\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor+1\bigr).
$$

The factor in parentheses is the **binary bit length** of $k$. It grows without bound, so $n_k/k\to\infty$, and $n_k$ increases strictly. However,

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n_k}=\infty.
$$

Indeed, for the dyadic block $2^j\le k<2^{j+1}$, the bit length equals $j+1$. Since $k<2^{j+1}$, every term in the block exceeds or equals $1/[2^{j+1}(j+1)]$. There are $2^j$ terms, so

$$
\sum_{k=2^j}^{2^{j+1}-1}\frac1{n_k}
\ge\frac{1}{2(j+1)}.
$$

Summing the block bounds produces a divergent harmonic series. Thus **Fabry gaps do not imply the stronger Fejér reciprocal-exponent condition**. Invoking Biernacki's theorem without checking this distinction would be invalid.

One can make a concrete entire series with exactly this support:

$$
g(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n_k}}{n_k!}.
$$

Each exponent occurs once, and all displayed coefficients are nonzero. Because these terms form a subseries of the exponential power series in absolute value,

$$
|g(z)|\le\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{|z|^m}{m!}
=e^{|z|}.
$$

So this example has finite order at most one. Pólya's **finite-order** theorem applies to it; it is not a counterexample to #517. Its purpose is to separate two different kinds of sparsity exactly.

## What is known

### Fejér, Biernacki and Pólya

Erdős poses the problem in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed **p.250, Problem IV.8**, [original scan](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=30). That page was visually inspected. Erdős attributes to **Lipót Fejér** (1908) the result that if

$$
\sum_k\frac1{n_k}<\infty,
$$

then an entire function with those nonzero exponents assumes every complex value **at least once**. He attributes to **Miecislas Biernacki** (1928) the stronger conclusion that every value is assumed **infinitely often** under the same summability hypothesis.

The original references are Fejér, *Über die Wurzel vom kleinsten absoluten Betrage einer algebraischen Gleichung*, *Mathematische Annalen* **65** (1908), 413–423, and Biernacki, *Sur les équations algébriques contenant des paramètres arbitraires*, thesis, Paris (1928). Their complete proofs were not independently retrieved during this audit.

The [maintained Erdős #517 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/517), last edited December 29, 2025, also credits **George Pólya**, *Lücken und Singularitäten der Potenzreihen*, *Mathematische Zeitschrift* **29** (1929), 549–640, with an important special case: **if the entire function has finite order**, unbounded exponent gaps suffice for all complex values to occur infinitely often. A source report gives the hypothesis $\limsup(n_{k+1}-n_k)=\infty$.

There is an important distinction between this finite-order theorem and Erdős's unrestricted question. The Fabry hypothesis $n_k/k\to\infty$ permits **infinite-order** entire functions. The exponential-domination example above has finite order and is already covered by the historical partial theory.

### The Picard-theorem reduction

One general complex-analytic principle identifies the remaining obstruction. By the **Great Picard Theorem at infinity**, a transcendental entire function assumes every finite complex value infinitely often, with **at most one exception**. An exceptional value may be omitted entirely or assumed only finitely many times.

Consequently #517 asks whether a transcendental **Fabry-gap** entire function can possess even **one** such exceptional value. An argument excluding the exceptional value would settle the universal claim.

For comparison, $e^z$ has the exceptional value zero. Exponentials with arithmetic-progression exponents have $n_k/k$ bounded and fall outside the Fabry class.

If $f-w$ has only finitely many zeros, it has the representation

$$
f(z)-w=P(z)e^{h(z)},
$$

where $P$ captures the finite zero divisor and $h$ is entire. After factoring out those zeros, the remaining function is nowhere zero and admits an entire logarithm on $\mathbb C$. The central difficulty is excluding such a representation while maintaining infinite-order Fabry gaps.

This is a **reduction**, not a solution. A truncated series is a polynomial and does not preserve the infinite-series condition.

## What remains

The immediate bounded task is to verify a sequence satisfying Fabry gaps but **not** reciprocal-exponent summability. The dyadic inequality above can be checked exactly for the first ten blocks, while its proof gives divergence over all blocks.

The genuine research boundary is to rule out a **Picard exceptional value** for infinite-order lacunary entire functions. Finite coefficient tests cannot decide that analytic question. The protected-ready GCL research backlog must not be launched or reclassified through this editorial pass.
