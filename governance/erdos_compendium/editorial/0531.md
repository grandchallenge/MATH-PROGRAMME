**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #531 is **OPEN** as a question about the quantitative growth of finite Folkman numbers. Folkman's theorem guarantees the desired monochromatic finite-sums pattern for every fixed set size. The problem is **how large an interval is necessary**. Known lower and upper bounds remain extraordinarily far apart.

## The question in plain language

Colour each positive integer from $1$ to $N$ **red** or **blue**, with no restrictions on the colouring.

For a set $A=\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}$ of **$k$ distinct positive integers**, let its nonempty finite-sums set be

$$
\operatorname{FS}(A)=
\left\{
\sum_{i\in I}a_i:
\varnothing\ne I\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
\right\}.
$$

Folkman's theorem says that, if $N$ is sufficiently large depending on $k$, every two-colouring of $[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}$ contains some $A$ for which **all** numbers in $\operatorname{FS}(A)$ are the **same colour**.

Define $F(k)$ as the **least** such $N$:

$$
\boxed{
F(k)=\min\left\{N:\
\forall\chi:[N]\to\{0,1\},\
\exists A\subseteq[N],\ |A|=k,\
\operatorname{FS}(A)\subseteq[N],\
\chi\text{ constant on }\operatorname{FS}(A)
\right\}.
}
$$

The inclusion $\operatorname{FS}(A)\subseteq[N]$ is indispensable. Colouring is defined only on $[N]$, so the larger subset sums **cannot be ignored**. Since all $a_i$ are positive, that inclusion is equivalent to $\sum_{a\in A}a\le N$.

The monochromatic sums need not be **different integers**. Several nonempty subsets can have the same sum. The theorem only demands that every *distinct value* obtained from those subsets receive the same colour.

The question is not whether $F(k)$ is finite. That is proved. It is to **estimate its growth** as $k$ becomes large.

## A small example

For $k=1$, the assertion simply requires one integer with a colour, so

$$
F(1)=1.
$$

For $k=2$, we need distinct $a<b$ satisfying $a+b\le N$ such that

$$
\boxed{\chi(a)=\chi(b)=\chi(a+b).}
$$

This is a **strict Schur triple**: unlike ordinary Schur's equation, $a=b$ is *not* allowed because $A$ must have two distinct elements. That distinction changes the small exact threshold.

An exhaustive finite check gives

$$
\boxed{F(2)=9.}
$$

For $N=8$, there is a two-colouring with **no** strict monochromatic triple. For $N=9$, all $2^9=512$ binary colourings contain at least one triple with $1\le a<b$ and $a+b\le9$. This is an exact, finite and reproducible statement, not an estimate for large $k$.

For $k=3$, a candidate set $A=\{a,b,c\}$ requires all seven *subset positions*

$$
a,\ b,\ c,\ a+b,\ a+c,\ b+c,\ a+b+c
$$

to have the same colour. Some numerical values may coincide, but any distinct values must be coloured consistently and **the total sum must remain at most $N$**.

This rapid growth in the number of required monochromatic sums explains why the quantitative problem becomes difficult.

## What is known

### Erdős's original formulation and Folkman's theorem

The primary source is Erdős, *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), Chapter 12, [printed p.122](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=6). The **original scanned page was visually inspected**.

Erdős credits Sanders and Folkman with the finite monochromatic-sums theorem and observes that it follows from earlier results of Rado (1933). He explicitly asks for useful numerical upper and lower bounds on the least interval length. Thus the **existence theorem was not open in 1973**, even though its quantitative form was.

The finite theorem extends to any fixed finite number of colours. For the present problem we restrict to two. It also differs from the related **infinite** problem #532, where one seeks an infinite set whose *all finite sums* share one colour. Hindman's theorem later proved that infinite result. A proof of an infinite existence theorem does not, by itself, yield an efficient quantitative estimate for $F(k)$.

### Erdős and Spencer's 1989 lower bound

P. Erdős and J. Spencer, *Monochromatic sumsets*, *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A* **50** (1989), 162–163, [DOI 10.1016/0097-3165(89)90011-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(89)90011-3), proved for an absolute constant $c>0$ that

$$
\boxed{F(k)\ge2^{c k^2/\log k}}
$$

for sufficiently large $k$. This was an advance beyond the qualitative existence theorem, but did not determine the correct growth.

### The 2017 double-exponential lower bound

József Balogh, Sean Eberhard, Bhargav Narayanan, Andrew Treglown and Adam Zsolt Wagner, *An improved lower bound for Folkman's theorem*, *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* **49** (2017), 745–747, [DOI 10.1112/blms.12058](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms.12058), obtained the much larger bound

$$
\boxed{F(k)\ge 2^{\,2^{k-1}/k}.}
$$

The **complete five-page author preprint** [arXiv:1703.02473v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02473) was downloaded and its abstract, formal definition and main theorem checked directly. This is a peer-reviewed published result.

One useful insight is why non-unique subset sums complicate a straightforward random-colouring argument. For any fixed set $A$, if the $m$ **distinct values** of $\operatorname{FS}(A)$ are independently coloured with fair signs, then

$$
\mathbb P(\operatorname{FS}(A)\text{ is monochromatic})
=2^{1-m}.
$$

If all subset sums are distinct, $m=2^k-1$, giving probability

$$
2^{\,2-2^k}.
$$

But when the sums **collide**, $m$ is smaller and the probability of monochromaticity is **larger**. Treating all generator sets as though they have $2^k-1$ distinct sums makes a naive union bound invalid.

Balogh and collaborators instead introduce a colouring constructed along the chains of integers with the same **odd part**, with the rule that $\chi(2x)$ and $\chi(x)$ are opposite. If a finite-sums set contains both $x$ and $2x$, it is automatically not monochromatic. This helps address the colliding-sums obstruction. Their rigorous proof then shows how the remaining dissociated configurations can be controlled.

This explanation is an outline of the published mechanism, not a GCL reconstruction of the complete random-colouring proof.

### The enormous upper bound

An upper bound can be extracted from a quantitative finite-unions theorem of A. D. Taylor, *Bounds for the Disjoint Unions Theorem*, *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A* **30** (1981), 339–344, [DOI 10.1016/0097-3165(81)90031-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(81)90031-5).

The classical consequence reported by Erdős and Spencer is that $F(k)$ is at most a **tower of threes of height $4k-3$**. That is, start with $3$ and repeatedly raise $3$ to the power of the previous expression, approximately $4k-3$ times. The full original Taylor proof has not been independently reconstructed in this audit.

Therefore the best bounds identified in the checked published sources leave a dramatic gap:

$$
\boxed{
2^{\,2^{k-1}/k}
\ \le F(k)\
\le \underbrace{3^{3^{\cdot^{\cdot^{3}}}}}_{\text{tower height }4k-3}.
}
$$

A double exponential is already enormous, but a growing-height exponential tower is qualitatively much larger. Determining the correct growth demands new structural Ramsey-theoretic methods.

### Small computational results are a separate evidentiary category

An [independent July 2026 working report](https://erdosproblemaday.com/report/531) reports $F(2)=9$, $F(3)=54$ and a colouring showing $F(4)\ge201$. It describes an LRAT-checked SAT refutation for $F(3)$ and direct verification of the larger colouring.

Those latter computations are **external evidence**, not a published theorem or a GCL-replayed certificate here. GCL independently repeats only the small $F(2)=9$ enumeration, using the precise generator and subset-sum conditions. We do not silently borrow the independent report's larger computational certifications.

## GCL's current state

The authenticated [#531 original source return](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1802#issuecomment-6227304361) records the original 1973 statement, the 1989 and 2017 proof provenance and the finite-versus-infinite Ramsey distinction.

A bounded exact fixture exhaustively checks every two-colouring of $[N]$ for $N\le9$ and demonstrates $F(2)=9$, with an independent direct Schur-triple kernel. It also checks distinct-value subset sums, the condition $\sum A\le N$, and elementary odd-part colourings. This is not used to infer a bound growing with $k$.

There is no #531 statement in the admitted protected Formal Conjectures snapshot and no GCL Lean certification.

## What remains

The mathematical challenge is to compress the gap between the double-exponential lower bound and the tower upper bound. A meaningful next research interface would be a quantitative finite-unions lemma with substantially less tower growth, or a new colouring mechanism beating the current double-exponential lower construction.

The finite acceptance task is intentionally narrower: exact $k=2$ enumeration, correct quantifiers and an auditable source-boundary receipt. Nothing about those bounded checks implies a new result for all $k$.
