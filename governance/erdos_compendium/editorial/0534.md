**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #534 is **SOLVED**. It asks for the largest possible collection of integers between $1$ and $N$ that **contains $N$** and in which every two *different* members have a common prime factor. Erdős and Graham proposed an initial formula that was later disproved. Ahlswede and Khachatrian established their corrected family of extremal constructions in 1996.

## The question in plain language

Fix an integer $N\ge2$ and choose a set $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ with $N\in A$. Require

$$
\gcd(a,b)>1\qquad\text{for all distinct }a,b\in A.
$$

How large can $A$ be? Call the maximum $M(N)$. The condition is **pairwise non-coprimality**. It does not require a *single* prime to divide every member, though choosing all multiples of a fixed prime provides an obvious construction.

The condition $N\in A$ matters: every other member must itself share a prime factor with $N$. If $N$ is prime, the only possible family is $\{N\}$, so $M(N)=1$. If $N$ is even, the family of all even numbers up to $N$ shows $M(N)\ge N/2$.

### A historical transcription trap

The original [Erdős 1973 discussion, printed p.123](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=7), writes $(a_i,a_j)=1$. This literally means the integers are **pairwise coprime**, contradicting the later interpretation and the constructions the problem seeks to optimize. The maintained catalogue explicitly identifies that occurrence as a typo: the intended condition is $\gcd(a_i,a_j)>1$.

There is **another difference** in the old conjecture's second example. The original describes even numbers $2t$ with $\gcd(t,N)=1$; the maintained modern description uses $\gcd(2t,N)>1$. These are not interchangeable conditions. They must not be silently identified as the same formula. The corrected final theorem below is stated independently of these old alternatives.

## A small example

Take $N=30$. Choosing all multiples of two gives

$$
A=\{2,4,6,\ldots,30\},\qquad |A|=15.
$$

Every pair has gcd at least two, and $30\in A$. This is an extremal construction for this particular $N$.

For a more general construction, write the distinct prime factors of $N$ in increasing order,

$$
N=\prod_{i=1}^{r}q_i^{e_i},
\qquad q_1<q_2<\cdots<q_r.
$$

For each $1\le j\le r$, form the list of generators

$$
D_j=\{2q_1,2q_2,\ldots,2q_j,\ q_1q_2\cdots q_j\},
$$

and take **all** integers up to $N$ divisible by at least one member of $D_j$:

$$
A_j=\{x\in[1,N]: \exists d\in D_j,\ d\mid x\}.
$$

Every $A_j$ contains $N$, since $\prod_{i=1}^{j}q_i\mid N$.

Why are all pairs in $A_j$ non-coprime? The generators $2q_i$ and $2q_k$ have a common factor two; $2q_i$ and $q_1\cdots q_j$ have the common factor $q_i$; two multiples of the product share that product. Thus any two members of $A_j$ have gcd greater than one.

For $N=30$, whose distinct prime factors are $2,3,5$, the first family is the set of all even integers, of size fifteen. Other $j$ give different, still admissible families, but none larger for this example.

## What is known

The central claim proved by Rudolf Ahlswede and Levon Khachatrian is that **one of the families just described attains the maximum**:

$$
\boxed{M(N)=\max_{1\le j\le r}|A_j|.}
$$

Their 1996 work also records that the **initial Erdős–Graham guess was false**, with a counterexample communicated to Erdős in 1992. Erdős then refined the conjectured structure to the $A_j$ families, and the refined statement was proved.

Two original publications by the same authors are relevant: *Sets of integers and quasi-integers with pairwise common divisor*, *Acta Arithmetica* **74** (1996), 141–153, [DOI 10.4064/aa-74-2-141-153](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-74-2-141-153); and *Sets of integers with pairwise common divisor and a factor from a specified set of primes*, *Acta Arithmetica* **75** (1996), 259–276, [DOI 10.4064/aa-75-3-259-276](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-75-3-259-276). Both original publisher records and download links were located. Direct retrieval of their complete PDFs returned HTTP 403, so this audit does **not** assert independent examination of a particular theorem number or the full proof.

The [maintained #534 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/534), updated April 8, 2026, explicitly attributes both the counterexample to the original formula and the proof of the corrected conjecture to Ahlswede and Khachatrian. The protected source register also classifies #534 as SOLVED.

### Why the theorem is more than an elementary gcd observation

It is straightforward to verify that each $A_j$ has the required common-divisor property. The difficult direction is to show that **every** admissible set $A$ has size at most the largest $A_j$. The optimality theorem constrains all possible ways of sharing primes between different integers, even when the family has no single common prime divisor.

An elementary way to visualize the question is a graph: make a vertex for each $x\in[1,N]$ with $\gcd(x,N)>1$, connect $x$ and $y$ if $\gcd(x,y)>1$, and ask for the largest clique. Every clique can be enlarged by the vertex $N$, which connects to every vertex in this graph. The theorem identifies the optimal clique size via the number-theoretic families $A_j$. A short brute-force computation can verify this identity for selected $N$, but cannot prove it for all integers.
