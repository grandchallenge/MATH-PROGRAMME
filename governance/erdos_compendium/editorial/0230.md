## The question in plain language

Choose complex coefficients $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ with **modulus one**, and form

$$
P(z)=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k z^k,\qquad |a_k|=1.
$$

Erdős and Newman asked whether there is a universal $c>0$ such that for **every choice of coefficients** and every $n\ge2$,

$$
\max_{|z|=1}|P(z)|\ge(1+c)\sqrt n.
$$

The choice of **complex phases** is crucial. Requiring coefficients to be only $+1$ or $-1$, as in #228, is a different problem.

## A small example

Parseval's identity gives

$$
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^2\,d\theta=n,
$$

so the maximum modulus is always at least $\sqrt n$. For $n=2$, the polynomial $P(z)=z+z^2$ has maximum modulus two, a ratio $\sqrt2$ above the Parseval scale. A positive gap at any **fixed** finite $n$ does not imply a gap by a constant uniform in all $n$.

## What is known

**DISPROVED.** Jean-Pierre Kahane (1980) constructed *ultraflat polynomials* with unimodular complex coefficients whose **absolute values** satisfy

$$
|P(z)|=(1+o(1))\sqrt n
\qquad\text{uniformly for }|z|=1.
$$

Thus no single positive $c$ can improve Parseval's lower bound for every degree. Körner (1980) had obtained two-sided bounds within fixed constant factors. Bombieri and Bourgain (2009) quantitatively sharpened the ultraflat construction, giving the uniform modulus estimate

$$
|P(z)|=\sqrt n+
O\!\left(n^{7/18}(\log n)^{O(1)}\right).
$$

**Source-fidelity correction:** the [maintained #230](https://www.erdosproblems.com/230) displays the Kahane and Bombieri–Bourgain statements with $P(z)$ rather than **$|P(z)|$** on the left. Read literally, that cannot hold for complex $P$ across the full unit circle: this polynomial has zero constant coefficient, so its average over the circle is zero and cannot converge uniformly to a positive constant. The primary meaningful statement concerns the modulus.

## What remains

A bounded task computes the exact Parseval identity for arbitrary unimodular coefficient vectors, checks the vanishing mean of $P$ (as opposed to $|P|$), and samples polynomial magnitudes for controlled complex phases. Acceptance: the integral $\int P(e^{i\theta})\,d\theta$ is exactly zero, and no finite sample is called an ultraflat proof. A proof-level replay should locate the modulus estimates in Kahane's and Bombieri–Bourgain's originals.
