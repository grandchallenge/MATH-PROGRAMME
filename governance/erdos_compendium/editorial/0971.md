## The question in plain language

For a positive modulus $d$ and a residue class $a$ with $\gcd(a,d)=1$, let $p(a,d)$ be the **least prime congruent to $a$ modulo $d$**. Dirichlet's theorem assures that it exists. Erdős asks whether **absolute** constants $c,C>0$ exist such that, for **every sufficiently large $d$**,

$$
\#\{0\le a<d:\gcd(a,d)=1,\ 
p(a,d)>(1+c)\varphi(d)\log d\}
\ \ge C\varphi(d).
$$

The $\gg\varphi(d)$ in the [maintained statement](https://www.erdosproblems.com/971) means a fixed positive proportion of all reduced classes for all large moduli. It is **not** merely the existence of one exceptionally late class, nor a subsequence of favorable moduli.

The catalogue's status is **OPEN**. But two independent AI-assisted **affirmative proof claims** appeared in 2026, including one claimed complete Lean 4 development. Neither has been independently reviewed, accepted by the catalogue or rebuilt by GCL. That evidence must be recorded without silently elevating it to a protected GCL mathematical theorem.

## A small example

*A small, exact example*

For $d=5$, the reduced residue classes are $1,2,3,4$, with least primes

$$
p(1,5)=11,\quad p(2,5)=2,\quad
p(3,5)=3,\quad p(4,5)=19.
$$

If $c=1/10$, the threshold is

$$
(1+c)\varphi(5)\log5=4.4\log5\approx7.0815.
$$

Exactly the classes $1$ and $4$ have least primes larger than this threshold. This finite illustration says nothing about the demanded **uniformly positive proportion for every large modulus**.

The reduced-class qualification is necessary. If $\gcd(a,d)>1$, the congruence class contains at most one possible prime and may contain none, so a least prime need not exist. A computational verifier that includes those nonunits changes the problem.

## A rigorous result just below the conjectured threshold

Fix any $0<\varepsilon<1$ and set

$$
T_d=(1-\varepsilon)\varphi(d)\log d.
$$

At most $\pi(T_d)$ reduced residue classes can have *any* prime at most $T_d$: each prime lies in only one residue class. Consequently

$$
\#\{a\bmod d:(a,d)=1,\ p(a,d)>T_d\}
\ge\varphi(d)-\pi(T_d).
$$

The standard lower bound $\varphi(d)\gg d/\log\log d$ implies $\log T_d=(1+o(1))\log d$, while the prime number theorem gives

$$
\pi(T_d)
=\frac{T_d}{\log T_d}(1+o(1))
=(1-\varepsilon+o(1))\varphi(d).
$$

Therefore, for all sufficiently large $d$,

$$
\boxed{
\#\{a\bmod d:(a,d)=1,\
p(a,d)>(1-\varepsilon)\varphi(d)\log d\}
\ge\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\varphi(d).}
$$

This is an unconditional **near-threshold** observation (using two established standard number-theoretic results). It is **not the conjecture**. Crossing from a threshold coefficient strictly **below one** to strictly **above one** is precisely where the elementary first-moment count becomes insufficient: the number of primes being distributed then exceeds the number of available reduced classes.

## What is known

*Established historical partial results*

Erdős's 1949 paper, *On some applications of Brun's method*, pp.57–63, proved the proposed $1+c$ lower bound for a suitable **infinite sequence** of moduli $d$. That does not prove the same statement for all sufficiently large moduli.

He also proved that for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, a positive proportion (with a constant depending on $\varepsilon$) of reduced classes satisfy

$$
p(a,d)<\varepsilon\varphi(d)\log d
$$

for every large $d$. This asserts a high frequency of **early** first primes, not of the required **late** first primes; an uneven distribution of primes can exhibit both substantial early-class concentration and too few late classes. The original results are summarized in the [source catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/971).

The related maximum $\max_{(a,d)=1}p(a,d)$ is a different question. A single very late class need not supply positive density of late classes.

## July 2026: Han's moment-method manuscript

KyungMin Han's July 25–26 2026 manuscript, *A Complete Candidate Proof of Erdős Problem 971*, claims the full affirmative statement. The proposed proof counts primes in reduced residue classes by factorial moments. For a cutoff $X$, write

$$
N_a(X)=\#\{p\le X:p\equiv a\pmod d\},\qquad
M_2=\sum_{(a,d)=1}\binom{N_a(X)}2,\qquad
M_3=\sum_{(a,d)=1}\binom{N_a(X)}3.
$$

A sufficiently large second factorial moment, controlled third moment, near-one average prime count and small increment when the cutoff is increased are meant to force a positive proportion of empty classes. The analytic inputs involve a **pointwise-in‐$d$** Friedlander–Goldston variance lower bound and a **uniform three-prime-form sieve**, plus a singular-series average.

Those qualifications cannot be dropped. Bounds only averaged over moduli, or with constants depending on $d$, do not imply Erdős's all-large‐$d$ assertion. The exact normalization and parameter range of the second moment, and the third-moment singular-series bounds for moduli with many small prime factors, are specified by the claimant as review-critical.

Han's Lean 4 file proves an **abstract finite combinatorial implication** from stipulated moment bounds, but **does not prove the necessary analytic hypotheses**. Thus even if that file compiles without placeholders, it is not a Lean verification of the full conjecture. [Claim review and scope](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/integer_sequences/E0971/claims/2026_07_25_han).

## September 2026: Li's claimed complete Lean development

Shisheng Li announced a separate development on 28 September 2026: [daizisheng/erdos-971-lean](https://github.com/daizisheng/erdos-971-lean). It claims to establish the exact Formal Conjectures target with the answer set to **true**:

$$
\exists c,C>0\quad\forall^\text{eventually}d,\qquad
C\varphi(d)\le
\#\{a<d:(a,d)=1,\ 
p(a,d)>(1+c)\varphi(d)\log d\}.
$$

According to the author, this proof follows the same second-/third-moment skeleton but replaces the variance input by a correlation between primes and rough numbers. The account credits GPT-6 for the mathematical route and Claude for formalization, and gives credit for the earlier candidate to Han.

The repository reports Lean **4.33.1**, Mathlib **v4.33.1**, zero unresolved proof placeholders and no nonstandard project axioms. Its claimed theorem has axiom closure limited to the usual three:

$$
\mathrm{propext},\quad\mathrm{Classical.choice},\quad\mathrm{Quot.sound}.
$$

It relies on external source-level formalizations of the prime number theorem, Bombieri–Vinogradov, and related inputs; it claims the relevant sieve lemma is proved internally. A particular reproducibility concern is a **patch that vendors ten files** from a prime-number-theorem dependency to repair an inconsistent source pin. Source replacement and patched dependencies need exact hashes and audit receipts, not merely a successful build assertion.

The independent research corpus has **not built or audited** Li's development, and Li's own README reportedly calls it **unreviewed**. It is a potentially decisive claim with concrete replay instructions, **not yet GCL-certified proof**. [Source and integrity analysis](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/integer_sequences/E0971/claims/2026_09_28_li).

The Google DeepMind [Formal Conjectures reference file](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/main/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/971.lean) itself remains tagged open with a proof placeholder. This is not evidence that a separate proof cannot exist; conversely, a source repository claiming a complete proof does not by itself justify changing the community catalogue's status. A meaningful formal comparison must match reduced classes, $p(a,d)$, strict inequality, both positive constants, and **eventually all $d$**, with no hidden extra analytic hypotheses.

## Why the problem is difficult

At the cutoff $\varphi(d)\log d$, the prime number theorem suggests roughly one small prime per reduced residue class on average. It is tempting to model their placements as independent occupancy trials. But arithmetic progressions are correlated, particularly for moduli with many small prime factors. To prove that a fixed fraction of classes stay empty beyond $(1+c)\varphi(d)\log d$, one needs control of prime **collisions** within classes and of extreme concentration, not simply an average prime count.

A moment inequality whose error depends adversely on the modulus may hold for special moduli yet fail exactly where this problem requires uniformity.
