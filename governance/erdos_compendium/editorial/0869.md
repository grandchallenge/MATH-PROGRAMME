## The question in plain language

Suppose we have two disjoint sets of positive integers, each rich enough that every sufficiently large integer can be expressed as the sum of **two** of its members. Such a set is an *asymptotic additive basis of order two*. Their union is certainly also a basis. Must the union contain a **minimal** basis, meaning a subset that still represents every sufficiently large integer, but loses that property whenever any one element is removed?

[Erdős Problem #869](https://www.erdosproblems.com/869) asked exactly this. Its surprising feature is that the union contains two independent supplies of representations. It is tempting to remove redundant elements one by one until none remain. That finite intuition fails for an infinite basis: infinitely many successive deletions need not preserve coverage at the limit.

Formally, write $2A=\{a+b:a,b\in A\}$. A set $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ is a basis of order two when $2A$ contains all sufficiently large positive integers. A subbasis $D\subseteq A$ is minimal when $D$ is a basis but $D\setminus\{d\}$ is not a basis for every $d\in D$. Equivalently, each element $d$ must be essential for infinitely many targets, not merely for one small integer.

## A small example

For disjoint finite sets $B,C$, the unordered representation counts satisfy the exact identity

$$
r_{B\cup C}(n)=r_B(n)+r_C(n)+\#\{(b,c)\in B\times C:b+c=n\}.
$$

The cross term counts each mixed representation exactly once. Thus, if both $B$ and $C$ cover a finite target interval, their union has at least two distinct representations of each target in that interval. This is a finite version of the apparent redundancy that motivated the question.

A reproducible seeded partition of $\{1,\ldots,160\}$ into sets of sizes $87$ and $73$ was checked. Each part separately represents every target in $[60,160]$ as a two-term sum. The identity above was verified for every $2\le n\le320$ by comparing independent pair enumeration with mixed-pair counting. A deletion algorithm then found a 51-element inclusion-minimal **finite** cover of $[60,160]$, with an explicit lost-target certificate for each remaining element.

This is not a counterexample to the infinite problem. Indeed, *every finite coverage instance has an inclusion-minimal subcover*: repeatedly delete an element while the finite target set remains covered; this process must terminate. The infinite construction must defeat precisely this termination argument.

## What is known

The original question appears as Problem 4 in Part 3 of [Erdős and Nathanson, *Partitions of Bases into Disjoint Unions of Bases* (1988), printed page 8](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1988-25.pdf). Earlier work by Härtter and Nathanson had already shown that some bases contain no minimal subbasis, but had not imposed the additional requirement of decomposition into two disjoint bases.

Daniel Larsen's [*Three Questions of Erdős–Nathanson on Asymptotic Bases of Order 2*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.03472), arXiv:2603.03472v1, dated 3 March 2026, gives a negative answer. Theorem 1 asserts that all eight truth-value combinations of three properties occur: divergence of the representation function, decomposition into two disjoint bases, and containment of a minimal subbasis. In particular, there are bases that **do** decompose into two disjoint bases but **do not** contain any minimal basis.

The mechanism is more informative than the verdict. Theorem 2 constructs two disjoint bases by filling rapidly growing intervals while reserving special *canary targets*. Each sufficiently late canary is arranged so that its representations must use a selected finite control set. The selection process eventually tests every eligible configuration. Any proposed subbasis that covers all canaries must retain enough of one component that one of its elements can be removed without destroying eventual coverage. The paper also handles the ordinary targets by a randomized interval construction and concentration bounds.

This is a source-reported mathematical resolution, not a new GCL proof. The maintained catalogue records the problem as disproved, with its page last edited 2 May 2026.

## GCL's current state

The original [source assignment #2140](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2140) now has an authenticated `RESULT/1` source-audit comment. This article remains an unreviewed editorial draft and has not been protected-merged or admitted.

A bounded independent check reproduced the finite decomposition identity and showed why a finite minimization experiment cannot establish the infinite counterexample. The March paper's Theorems 1 and 2, including the special-target coverage mechanism, were read from the primary preprint. Its probabilistic estimates, all stages of the construction, and the non-minimality conclusion have **not** been independently re-proved or formally certified by GCL.

The important source distinction is between (a) the existence of two disjoint asymptotic bases, (b) the fact that their union is not itself minimal, which is immediate, and (c) the much stronger assertion that their union contains **no** minimal subbasis. Only (c) resolves #869.

## What remains

The next bounded mathematical audit should isolate Theorem 2's *canary-to-deletion* interface. Inputs are the pinned March 2026 preprint, the precise definition of eligible control sets, the special sequence $N_i$, and the stagewise representation bounds. The target is a stand-alone lemma: for any asymptotic subbasis $D$ of the constructed union, prove the existence of an element $d\in D$ such that $D\setminus\{d\}$ remains an asymptotic basis. This would establish the required universal non-minimality from clearly stated source hypotheses.

The audit must check separately that every sufficiently large ordinary target survives deletion and that every sufficiently large canary survives deletion. Acceptance requires a source-page-locked proof with explicit quantifiers and no unstated interchange of infinite limits. A counterexample to an intermediate claim must be recorded as an exception. Stop if the randomized construction's hypotheses cannot be matched to the deletion lemma.
