## The question in plain language

For an integer $k\geq 3$, let $f_{k,3}(x)$ count the integers $n\leq x$ that can be written as a sum of three nonnegative kth powers:

$$
n=a^k+b^k+c^k,
$$

where $a,b,c$ are nonnegative integers. Erdős Problem #325 asks whether, for every such fixed $k$, the number of represented integers grows at least on the natural scale $x^{3/k}$. In standard asymptotic notation, the first proposed form is

$$
f_{k,3}(x)\gg x^{3/k}.
$$

The notation means that a positive constant may depend on $k$, but not on sufficiently large $x$. The problem also asks the weaker alternative $f_{k,3}(x)\gg_\epsilon x^{3/k-\epsilon}$ for every $\epsilon>0$. These are asymptotic statements: checking many small values of $x$ cannot settle them, and the quantifier “for every $k\geq 3$” is essential.

The exponent $3/k$ is the heuristic scale suggested by choosing three variables of size about $x^{1/k}$. Collisions are unavoidable, however: different triples can give the same sum. The question is whether enough distinct integers survive those collisions for a power-sized lower bound.

## A small example

Take $k=3$ and $x=100$. The cubes of nonnegative integers that do not exceed $100$ are

$$
0,1,8,27,64.
$$

An exact finite calculation forms every ordered triple from this five-element list, keeps sums at most $100$, and removes duplicates. It gives

$$
\left|\{a^3+b^3+c^3\leq 100: a,b,c\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\}\right|=29.
$$

For a check on the arithmetic, $91$ occurs because

$$
91=0^3+3^3+4^3=0+27+64.
$$

This is a reproducible exact-arithmetic finite check: the only operations are integer powers, integer addition, comparison with $100$, and set deduplication. It illustrates what $f_{3,3}(100)$ means under the nonnegative convention used by the problem statement and the pinned Lean definition. It does not establish a lower bound for all large $x$, does not test every $k$, and does not distinguish the two asymptotic claims.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry for #325](https://www.erdosproblems.com/325) gives the question in the same form and records earlier binary-power work of Erdős and Mahler. Its bibliography identifies Erdős and Mahler, “On the number of integers which can be represented by a binary form,” *Documenta Mathematica* (2019), 475–481, as [ErMa38], and Trevor D. Wooley, “Sums of three cubes, II,” *Acta Arithmetica* 170 (2015), 73–100, as [Wo15].

Wooley’s primary paper is the relevant unconditional result for $k=3$. Theorem 1.1 on printed p. 73 states that if $N(X)$ counts integers not exceeding $X$ represented as sums of three cubes of natural numbers, then

$$
N(X)\gg X^{0.91709477}.
$$

The paper explains that this follows from a sixth-moment estimate for smooth cubic Weyl sums, stated as Theorem 1.2 and developed through the later sections. It is a lower bound for the cubic case, not a proof of the all-k assertion in #325. The wording “natural numbers” also deserves a convention check against the problem’s explicit “nonnegative” variables; the nonnegative formulation is the one used in the Lean predicate, and this audit does not silently identify conventions that the source does not define.

The live entry says the problem has been formalised, but neither it nor Wooley’s paper supplies a solution for every $k\geq 3$. The available primary evidence therefore supports a strong solved special case while leaving the general catalogue question open.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-semantic bridge: compare the exact quantifiers in the catalogue wording with the formal theorem, settle whether “integers” includes $0$, and record the convention for “natural numbers” in Wooley’s $N(X)$. The bridge should then isolate the fixed k = 3 theorem from the unresolved all-k claim and from the weaker epsilon-variant.

A bounded formalisation task could next prove the finite counting lemma for a chosen $k$ and $x$ in the exact Lean definitions, then state the cubic Wooley interface without filling its proof. Such work would improve reproducibility and expose coercion or quantifier mismatches, but it would not replay Wooley’s analytic argument. Any broader claim that the original all-k problem is solved would require a primary theorem covering those quantifiers, or a separately verified reduction to one.
