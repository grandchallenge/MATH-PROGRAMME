## The question in plain language

Take a finite set $A$ of positive integers. Can one always find two members $a,b$ of $A$ for which the common divisor $gcd(a,b)$ is small compared with $a$ and the number of available choices? Erdős Problem #402 asks for the precise inequality

$$
\gcd(a,b) \leq \frac{a}{\lvert A\rvert}.
$$

The two entries in the pair need not be different, although a diagonal pair is useful only in very small cases. Equivalently, after multiplying by $lvert A\rvert$ and dividing by the gcd, the question asks whether some ratio $a/\gcd(a,b)$ is at least the size of the set. The asymmetry matters: the numerator is the first chosen element, so the order of the pair is part of the witness.

The problem arose as a conjecture of R. L. Graham. A related sharper question classifies the sets for which the largest such ratio is exactly $lvert A\rvert$. This is the source of the reciprocal-set examples and of the exceptional set ${2,3,4,6}$.

## A small example

Consider $A=\{2,3,4,6\}$. It has four elements. The exact pair check over all sixteen ordered pairs gives the unique qualifying witness $(a,b)=(4,3)$:

$$
\gcd(4,3)=1=\frac{4}{4},
\qquad
\frac{4}{\gcd(4,3)}=4.
$$

This was verified with integer gcds and rational arithmetic, not floating-point rounding: the complete enumeration returned the witness list $[(4,3,1,1,4)]$, where the entries record $a$, $b$, $gcd(a,b)$, $a/\lvert A\rvert$, and $a/\gcd(a,b)$. Thus the bound is attained exactly for this ordered pair, and the maximum ratio in the enumeration is $4$.

The calculation illustrates the theorem's finite meaning and why this set appears in the equality discussion. It does not prove the assertion for arbitrary finite sets, does not establish the equality classification, and does not replace the published argument. It also does not show that a finite computer check has searched beyond this one set.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems #402 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/402), accessed 2026-10-13, gives the statement above and records: Graham's original conjecture; independent sufficiently-large-set results of Mario Szegedy and Alexandru Zaharescu; and an all-sets result of R. Balasubramanian and K. Soundararajan. The live page says it was last edited 2026-04-08 and has no proof exposition or proof claim. Its bibliography identifies Szegedy, *The solution of Graham's greatest common divisor problem*, *Combinatorica* 6 (1986), 67–71, DOI [10.1007/BF02579410](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579410), and Zaharescu, *On a conjecture of Graham*, *Journal of Number Theory* 27 (1987), 33–40, DOI [10.1016/0022-314X(87)90048-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-314X(87)90048-5).

The all-sets primary source is Balasubramanian and Soundararajan, *On a conjecture of R. L. Graham*, *Acta Arithmetica* 75 (1996), 1–38, [the accessible article PDF](https://matwbn.icm.edu.pl/ksiazki/aa/aa75/aa7511.pdf), DOI [10.4064/aa-75-1-1-38](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-75-1-1-38). On printed page 2, Theorem 1.1 states that for $N\geq 5$, if $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_N\}$ has increasing positive entries and total gcd $1$, then some $i,j$ satisfy

$$
\frac{a_i}{\gcd(a_i,a_j)} \geq N.
$$

The theorem further says the inequality is strict when both $A$ and its reciprocal set differ from ${1,\ldots,N}$. The paper notes that $N\leq4$ is trivial. Dividing an arbitrary positive set by its total gcd preserves all ratios, so this normalized theorem supplies the registered statement for general finite positive sets.
