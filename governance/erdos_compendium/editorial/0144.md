## The question in plain language

Do almost all positive integers have **two different divisors near one another**? Erdős #144 asks whether the set of integers $n$ that possess divisors $d_1,d_2\mid n$ satisfying

$$
d_1<d_2<2d_1
$$

has natural density one. “Density one” means the proportion of such integers among $1,\ldots,N$ tends to one, not that the property holds for every integer. The inequalities are **strict**; equality $d_2=2d_1$ does not qualify.

## A small example

The divisors of $12$ include $2$ and $3$, which satisfy $2<3<4$. Thus 12 qualifies. A prime $p$ has only divisors 1 and $p$, so it does not qualify for any $p\ge2$. These infinite exceptions are compatible with density one because the primes themselves have density zero.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Helmut Maier and Gérald Tenenbaum (1984) established the stronger statement that for **any fixed** constant $c>1$, almost all integers have two divisors obeying $d_1<d_2<c d_1$. The maintained [Erdős #144](https://www.erdosproblems.com/144) also records a refined shrinking-gap threshold involving $(\log n)^{-\beta}$: the density-one result holds in the relevant regime $\beta<\log3-1$, while Erdős–Hall established density zero above that threshold. The theorem for fixed $c$ must not be mistaken for a bound with $c\to1$ arbitrarily quickly as $n$ grows.

## What remains

Enumerate divisors for $1\le n\le10^4$ with exact integers and test the existence of strict $d_1<d_2<2d_1$. Acceptance: 12 passes and every prime fails; both exhaustive pair testing and sorted-neighbour testing agree. A finite frequency table cannot prove natural density one. A later formal task may compare fixed-ratio and shrinking-ratio quantifiers.
