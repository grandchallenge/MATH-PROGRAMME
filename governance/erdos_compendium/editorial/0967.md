## The question in plain language

Let
\[
1<a_1<a_2<\cdots
\]
be a **strictly increasing infinite sequence of positive integers** satisfying
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac1{a_j}<\infty.
\]
Erdős and Ingham asked whether the following complex Dirichlet series can **never vanish** on the vertical line \(\Re s=1\):
\[
F(t)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} a_j^{-(1+it)},\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
\]
**The answer is no.** Fredy Yip proved in December 2025 that, for **every fixed nonzero** real \(t\), one can choose a suitable summable-reciprocal sequence for which \(F(t)=0\). The maintained [Erdős Problem #967](https://www.erdosproblems.com/967) is classified **DISPROVED (LEAN)**. This negative result is not the assertion that one fixed sequence makes \(F(t)=0\) for all \(t\).

## Why the analytic expression is well defined

For real \(t\) and any integer \(a>1\), use the unambiguous real logarithm:
\[
a^{-(1+it)}=\frac{1}{a}\exp(-it\log a).
\]
Hence
\[
\left|a^{-(1+it)}\right|=\frac1a.
\]
The hypothesis \(\sum_j1/a_j<\infty\) ensures the series defining \(F(t)\) converges **absolutely for every real \(t\)**, in fact uniformly in \(t\) by the Weierstrass majorant. Thus the conjecture is a genuine nonvanishing assertion, not a question about the interpretation of a conditionally convergent series.

A useful elementary barrier is
\[
|F(t)|\ge 1-\sum_j\frac1{a_j}.
\]
Therefore **every sequence with total reciprocal mass less than one satisfies the desired nonvanishing for all \(t\)**. Any counterexample must have total mass at least one. At \(t=0\), every summand is positive and \(F(0)>1\), so a counterexample necessarily uses **nonzero** \(t\). Neither observation resolves the general question, because the allowed convergent reciprocal sum can exceed one.

## The historically troublesome finite example

Erdős and Ingham mentioned the finite set \(S=\{2,3,5\}\) as already difficult. Its reciprocal mass is
\[
\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{31}{30}>1,
\]
so the trivial reverse-triangle lower bound gives no nonvanishing conclusion. At \(t=0\),
\[
1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{61}{30}>0.
\]
This does not rule out a zero at some \(t\ne0\). In fact, the **finite-set version remains open** even after Yip's infinite-sequence counterexample. Replacing an infinite series by an arbitrary finite sum is not a harmless truncation for exact equality to zero.

## The stronger negative theorem

Yip's [*On a problem of Erdős and Ingham*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16528), arXiv:2512.16528v1 (18 December 2025), Theorem 1.3, proves a representation theorem: for each fixed real \(t\ne0\) and **every complex target** \(\lambda\in\mathbb C\), there is a subset \(S\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge2}\) such that
\[
\sum_{n\in S}\frac1n<\infty,\qquad
\sum_{n\in S}\frac1{n^{1+it}}=\lambda.
\]
In a refinement stated immediately after the theorem, the set may be chosen **infinite**, above any prescribed positive-integer cutoff, and with reciprocal mass at most \(|\lambda|+\delta\) for any chosen \(\delta>0\). This infinite-set refinement is important for precise equivalence with Erdős's strictly increasing **infinite sequence**. The printed argument sketches its proof but does not spell out every nonterminating-block choice; the cited independent source study supplies the missing schedule separately and distinguishes that addition from the original preprint.

Now fix \(t=1\) and choose \(\lambda=-1\). Enumerating the infinite set \(S\) increasingly as \(a_1<a_2<\cdots\), the theorem and infinite-set refinement give
\[
\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac1{a_j}<\infty,\qquad
1+\sum_{j=1}^\infty a_j^{-(1+i)}=0.
\]
This is a counterexample to the original universal nonvanishing statement. No explicit small numerical list of the \(a_j\) is claimed to certify the infinite equality.

## How the iterative construction works

The key local result, Lemma 2.1 in Yip's four-page preprint, says that for fixed \(t\ne0\) a target \(c\in\mathbb C\) can be approximated with a **finite set of integers above an arbitrary cutoff**. The approximation has quadratic error in the target size:
\[
\left|c-\sum_{n\in E}n^{-(1+it)}\right|
\le (1+|1+it|)\,|c|^2,
\qquad
\sum_{n\in E}\frac1n\le |c|.
\]
The proof chooses integers in a suitable short interval at a scale whose \(\log n\) gives the desired phase \(e^{-it\log n}\). For a suitably small residual \(c\), replacing \(c\) by the unrepresented remainder contracts the error **quadratically**. Iterating with increasing cutoffs gives mutually disjoint finite blocks and an absolutely convergent sum approaching the prescribed complex target exactly. Larger initial targets are handled before entering the small-residual regime.

The construction's **mass budget** is indispensable: without \(\sum_{n\in E}1/n\) control, the successive blocks could formally sum to \(-1\) but violate the premise of #967. Conversely, approximating \(-1\) arbitrarily closely by a finite sum is not by itself an exact zero. The infinite-block and limit arguments are what establish the actual counterexample.

An independent source review of arXiv v1 identifies two minor notation misprints on printed p.3: one block subscript is shifted, and one residual symbol is undefined. Its correction note interprets these using the recurrence and geometric error estimate. Such repairs should be recorded explicitly in any GCL theorem reconstruction rather than silently rewritten as author-original text. ## The separate finite conjecture

Yip explicitly leaves open:
\[
\boxed{\quad
1+\sum_{n\in S}n^{-(1+it)}\ne0
\quad\text{for every **finite** }S\subseteq\{2,3,\ldots\},
\quad \text{every }t\in\mathbb R.
\quad}
\]
This is Conjecture 3.1 in his preprint; Question 3.2 singles out \(S=\{2,3,5\}\). The infinite theorem neither proves nor disproves the finite assertion. This is a crucial status division: **#967 as originally formulated is disproved, while the related finite-sequence version remains open**.

Erdős and Ingham originally formulated the series question as an interface for an arithmetical Tauberian theorem. Their equivalence links nonvanishing to whether a corresponding asymptotic functional equation for nonnegative, nondecreasing, locally bounded functions forces \(f(x)\sim x\). This contextual equivalence is a published historical result, not an extra analytic hypothesis needed for Yip's counterexample.
