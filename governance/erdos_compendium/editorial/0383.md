## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 383 asks whether a striking pattern can persist for arbitrarily long finite stretches. Let $P^+(n)$ mean the largest prime factor of a positive integer $n$. For every natural number $k$, are there infinitely many primes $p$ for which

$$
P^+\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(p^2+i)\right)=p?
$$

The factor with index $0$ is $p^2$, so the product already has $p$ as a prime factor. The equality therefore says that every prime factor of every later number $p^2+1,p^2+2,\ldots,p^2+k$ is at most $p$. The difficulty is not finding an isolated example for one value of $k$; it is proving that, for each fixed length, infinitely many prime starting points work.

The quantifier order matters. The prime $p$ may depend on $k$, but one needs infinitely many choices of $p$ after $k$ has been fixed. The question does not ask for one prime that works for all $k$, nor does a finite search establish the claim. The live problem page also records a heuristic suggesting a positive answer, and notes a connection with the second part of Problem 382. Those are motivation, not proof.

## A small example

A finite calculation gives a useful scale without pretending to settle the infinitary question. The live discussion reports the prime $p=9,188,057$. I checked its displayed factorizations by exact integer multiplication and deterministic primality tests. The largest prime factor of each term $p^2+i$ for $0\leq i\leq 10$ is as follows:

| $i$ | largest prime factor of $p^2+i$ |
| ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 9,188,057 |
| 1 | 43,261 |
| 2 | 166,273 |
| 3 | 574,003 |
| 4 | 496,229 |
| 5 | 4,027 |
| 6 | 166,643 |
| 7 | 228,299 |
| 8 | 349,913 |
| 9 | 305,441 |
| 10 | 2,185,369 |

For example, the last entry is verified by the exact equality

$$
9,188,057^2+10=11\cdot23\cdot179\cdot853\cdot2,185,369=84,420,391,435,259,
$$

and $2,185,369<9,188,057$. The analogous factorizations for the preceding ten values have all prime factors no larger than $p$, so this one prime witnesses the finite conditions through $k=10$. It fails immediately at the next term: the displayed factorization gives

$$
9,188,057^2+11=2^2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot1,407,006,523,921,
$$

whose final factor exceeds $p$. This verifies a finite example, not infinitely many primes for any fixed $k$; it also says nothing about whether other primes work beyond $10$.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980). On printed page 73, in the discussion of greatest prime factors of products of consecutive integers, they ask precisely whether, for every $k$, infinitely many values of $p$ satisfy $P^+(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(p^2+i))=p$. The source poses the question; the audited source passage does not supply a proof or a theorem answering it.

The current [Erdős Problems page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/383), accessed 2026-10-13, lists two ordinary discussion comments and one proof claim. The proof-claim page explicitly warns that appearance is no guarantee of correctness. Its summary, submitted 2026-07-25, reports an alleged partial bound for fixed $k\geq1$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ of the form $P^+(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(p^2+i))\leq p^{2-1/(2k)+\varepsilon}$ for infinitely many primes, based on a theorem of Dartyge, Martin, and Tenenbaum. Even if that report is later verified with all hypotheses and the index convention aligned, its exponent is not $1$ and so it does not establish Problem 383.

The page also records a separate finite search up to $10^8$ and a larger reported witness. Those reports are useful leads, but they are not external proof and were not treated as certified mathematical results here.

## What remains

The smallest substantive gap is an unconditional infinitude theorem at the exact threshold $p$: for each fixed natural $k$, prove that infinitely many primes have no prime divisor larger than $p$ anywhere in the product. A bounded successor should first normalize the original product against the partial-result convention $\prod_{i=1}^{k}(p^2+i)$, then recover the exact hypotheses and conclusion of the cited Dartyge–Martin–Tenenbaum theorem. It should check the restriction to primes near the size parameter, the dependence on $k$ and $\varepsilon$, and whether the claimed bound is genuinely uniform over infinitely many primes.

Acceptance should be either a source-quoted theorem with page or theorem identifiers and a line-by-line specialization proving the exact Problem 383 conclusion, or a rigorous obstruction showing why the proposed specialization cannot reach exponent $1$. A finite factorization may be accepted as a reproducible computational certificate only for its stated range. Stop when the source text, parameter translation, or prime-selection step is unavailable; do not turn an unverified proof claim into a solved status.
