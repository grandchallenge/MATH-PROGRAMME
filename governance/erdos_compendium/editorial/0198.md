## The question in plain language

A **Sidon set** $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ has the property that each integer has at most one representation as $a+b$ with $a,b\in A$ and $a\le b$. Erdős and Graham asked whether the **complement** of every Sidon set must contain some infinite arithmetic progression

$$
\{a,a+d,a+2d,\ldots\},\qquad d\ge1.
$$

This would require one entire arithmetic progression to **avoid** $A$. An infinite Sidon set meeting every infinite progression is enough to disprove the assertion.

## A small example

The increasing sequence

$$
A=\{(n+1)!+n:n\ge0\}=\{1,3,8,27,124,\ldots\}
$$

is a concrete counterexample, attributed on the maintained site to AlphaProof. Its terms grow so rapidly that all unordered sums are distinct. For every residue class $a\bmod d$, take $n=a+d$; the term $(n+1)!+n$ is congruent to $a\bmod d$ and is at least $a$. Thus **every infinite positive arithmetic progression intersects $A$**, and none lies wholly in its complement.

A Sidon set must count the diagonal sums $2a$ as well. Merely checking that distinct-element sums are unique would establish a weaker property.

## What is known

**DISPROVED.** Erdős and Graham attributed the negative result to James E. Baumgartner, [*Partitioning vector spaces*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(75)90016-3), *Journal of Combinatorial Theory A* 18 (1975), 231–233. Its vector-space theorem meets every infinite progression while avoiding three-term arithmetic progressions. For subsets of $\mathbb N$, a simpler direct argument enumerates all countably many infinite progressions $P_1,P_2,\ldots$ and chooses a point $a_n\in P_n$ with $a_n>2a_{n-1}$. This produces a Sidon set meeting every progression.

The factorial formula supplies an **explicit** witness without an enumeration of all infinite progressions. The maintained [Erdős #198](https://www.erdosproblems.com/198), updated April 2026, reports an external Lean formalization. That formalization was not independently kernel-replayed under the GCL provider lock.

## What remains

A bounded task computes the first 12 factorial terms, enumerates all unordered pair sums, and verifies unique representations. For moduli $d\le12$ and residue classes $r$, produce the explicit term $(r+d+1)!+(r+d)$ and check its residue and lower bound. Acceptance: all checks pass and the argument for **arbitrary** $d$ follows from factorial divisibility, separately from finite tests.
