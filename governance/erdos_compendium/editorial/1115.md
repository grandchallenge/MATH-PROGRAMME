## The question in plain language

An *entire function* is a complex function analytic everywhere on the complex plane. Some entire functions become very large along paths that escape to infinity.

[Erdős Problem #1115](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1115) asks how **short** such an escape path can be for an entire function of finite order. If $\Gamma$ is a path along which $|f(z)|\to\infty$, let $\ell(r)$ be the length of the part of $\Gamma$ inside the disk $|z|<r$, and let

$$
M(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.
$$

In particular, is there *always* an escape path with length at most a constant times $r$?

The universal short-path assertion is **false**.

## A small example

For the entire function $f(z)=e^z$, take the positive real ray $\Gamma(t)=t$, $t\ge0$.

Along it,

$$
|f(\Gamma(t))|=e^t\to\infty.
$$

Furthermore, $M(r)=e^r$ and the portion of the ray inside $|z|<r$ has length exactly $\ell(r)=r$.

This shows that *some* entire functions have very simple linear-length escape curves. It cannot prove that **every** finite-order entire function has one.

## What is known

The question is attributed variously to Hayman and Erdős in its historical sources.

Hayman obtained positive results under quite restrictive growth assumptions. According to the maintained catalogue, **Gol'dberg and Eremenko (1979)** disproved the universal assertion. For every auxiliary function $\phi(r)\to\infty$, they constructed entire functions with growth as small as

$$
\log M(r)\ll\phi(r)(\log r)^2
$$

that admit **no** escape path with $\ell(r)\ll r$.

Their construction was also reported to allow prescribed finite order, including order zero. Consequently, finite order by itself does not imply the existence of a linear-length escape path.

This does not automatically determine an exact minimal length for every entire function; the negative universal assertion and the optimization problem are distinct.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2386](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2386#issuecomment-6168872310) separates the universal theorem from the elementary $e^z$ example.

**#1115 is a predetermined critical-review sample.** The complex-analytic counterexample construction and its growth estimates have not yet been independently reproduced from the original paper. The critical review remains incomplete.

## What remains

Read Hayman's original statements and Gol'dberg–Eremenko's 1979 proof. Verify finite-order and order-zero scope, the exact rectifiability and arclength conventions, the quantifier over $\phi$, and the constants in the growth estimates before completing critical review.
