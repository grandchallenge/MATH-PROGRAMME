## The question in plain language

Can we find an integer $n$ so that subtracting **every power of two smaller than $n$ but larger than one** always produces a prime?

[Erdős Problem #1142](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1142) asks whether infinitely many integers satisfy

$$
n-2^k\text{ is prime for every }k\text{ with }1<2^k<n.
$$

Even the existence of a qualifying integer **larger than $105$** remains open.

## A small example

The known numbers are

$$
4,\ 7,\ 15,\ 21,\ 45,\ 75,\ 105.
$$

A complete exact integer-primality check for $3\le n\le10000$ reproduces this list, with no additional qualifying integers.

There is a small logical convention at the lower endpoint. The literal condition for $n=2$ is **vacuously true**, because no power of two lies strictly between one and two. The traditional list implicitly restricts attention to a nonempty test range, for example $n\ge3$.

For $n=15$, the relevant differences are $15-2=13$, $15-4=11$ and $15-8=7$, all prime.

## What is known

**Mientka and Weitzenkamp (1969)** proved that there are no further qualifying integers through $2^{44}$, according to the maintained catalogue. GCL has not independently replayed that published proof.

**Vaughan (1973)** established a strong upper bound on the number of eligible $n\le N$, of the form

$$
N\exp\left(-c\frac{\log\log\log N}{\log\log N}\log N\right)
$$

for some $c>0$ in its asymptotic range. This establishes sparseness but does **not** prove there are only finitely many such $n$.

Erdős also posed a stronger quantitative conjecture about the number of prime shifts $n-2^k$ for a typical or arbitrary $n$. That is distinct from this all-prime requirement.
