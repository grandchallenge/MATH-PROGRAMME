## The question in plain language

Does there exist an integer $m\ge1$ relatively prime to six such that **every** integer

$$
2^k3^\ell m+1,\qquad k,\ell\ge0,
$$

is composite? The exponents $k,\ell$ vary **independently**, including zero. The requirement $\gcd(m,6)=1$ is not decorative: admitting divisibility by two or three changes the residue and parity structure.

This generalizes the idea of a *Sierpiński number*, an odd integer $m$ for which $2^km+1$ is composite for every $k\ge1$. The two-base question needs one common integer $m$ defeating every pair of exponents.

## A small example

For $m=5$, the choice $k=\ell=0$ gives $m+1=6$, composite, but the choice $k=1,\ell=0$ yields $11$, prime. Thus $m=5$ fails. For $m=1$, the zero-exponent term equals two, prime, so it also fails.

An experiment that checks finitely many pairs $(k,\ell)$ can rule out a candidate by finding one prime, but **cannot certify** a candidate that survives the test: infinitely many pairs remain. A covering-system argument would have to cover all exponent pairs and ensure that the designated divisor is proper for each value.

## What is known

The question remains **OPEN**, according to [Erdős #203](https://www.erdosproblems.com/203), last edited January 2026. The original question is from Erdős and Graham (1980), page 27. Ordinary Sierpiński examples solve a **one-base** version only.

The catalogue also records a broad variant with arbitrary fixed distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ and a variant where the primes $q_i$ range through those congruent to one modulo four. A current comment observes a **trivial $m=1$ case** for the latter unless additional restrictions are imposed. This does not resolve the displayed two-base question, for which the zero-exponent term $m+1$ remains binding.

## What remains

A bounded successor checks all $1\le m\le1000$ coprime to six against exponent pairs $0\le k,\ell\le8$, using an exact primality test and reporting a prime witness for each eliminated candidate. Acceptance: $m=1$ fails at $(0,0)$ and $m=5$ fails at $(1,0)$; any surviving candidate is labelled **finite-window survivor**, never a solution. A separate proof task would develop a genuine covering of all exponent pairs.
