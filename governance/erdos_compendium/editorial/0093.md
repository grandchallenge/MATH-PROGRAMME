## The question in plain language

Place $n$ distinct points in strictly convex position in the plane, meaning every point is a vertex of their convex polygon. Count the different Euclidean distances between unordered pairs of these points.

Erdős #93 asks for a universal lower bound of

$$
\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor
$$

distinct distances. The assertion concerns all pairs of vertices together, not the number of distances from a *single* designated point. Convexity is essential to the theorem's geometric argument.

## A small example

Four vertices of a square determine only two distances, the side length and the diagonal length, exactly $\lfloor4/2\rfloor=2$. A regular pentagon also determines just two lengths: side and diagonal. This meets the bound $\lfloor5/2\rfloor=2$.

Counting the multiplicities of equal distances is unnecessary for the statement. For a regular polygon, symmetries cause many different pairs to share a single length.

## What is known

**PROVED.** E. Altman proved the required convex-polygon theorem in [*On a Problem of P. Erdös*](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.1963.11990057), *American Mathematical Monthly* 70 (1963), 148–157. The publisher record confirms the original article and pages. The maintained [Erdős #93](https://www.erdosproblems.com/93) identifies this as Altman's result.

A later third-party Lean proof is reported on the maintained problem page and identified in a contemporary formal-proofs repository. This reporting is not equivalent to independent verification under GCL's protected source lock. The pinned Formal Conjectures snapshot for this cohort has no admitted #93 statement file; later upstream versions are not silently promoted.

The stronger statement that **one particular polygon vertex** must determine $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ distinct distances is a separate open problem, #982. It cannot be inferred from Altman's theorem by moving quantifiers.

## What remains

A bounded verification task is to generate regular convex polygons for $3\le n\le12$, enumerate exact chord-length classes by cyclic separation, and show the number is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Acceptance: an algebraic proof that chord lengths for separations $1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ are pairwise distinct and the other separations repeat these lengths. This establishes sharp examples but not Altman's universal lower bound for arbitrary convex polygons. Independently auditing the deep theorem is a separate proof task.
