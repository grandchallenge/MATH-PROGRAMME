## The question in plain language

For a prime number $p$, start with the sequence

$$
0!+1,\quad 1!+1,\quad 2!+1,\quad3!+1,\ldots
$$

and stop at the first term divisible by $p$. Call its factorial index $f(p)$. [Erdős Problem #1072](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1072) asks two questions: are there infinitely many primes for which the first successful index is as late as $p-1$? And is $f(p)$ usually much smaller than $p$, in the precise sense that $f(p)/p\to0$ for a density-one set of primes?

The word *usually* matters. It means all but a zero proportion **among the primes**, not a zero proportion of all integers.

## A small example

For $p=7$, the first successful index is $3$, since

$$
3!+1=7.
$$

Thus $f(7)=3$, considerably smaller than $p-1=6$. For $p=13$, none of the earlier factorials works, but $12!+1$ is divisible by $13$, so $f(13)=12$.

Exact modular calculation for primes through $150$ gives $f(p)=p-1$ at

$$
p=3,5,13,17,31,37,41,53,73,89,97,101,107,113.
$$

That finite list is not evidence of an infinity theorem. There is also an easily missed boundary case: when the minimization permits $n=0$, one has $f(2)=0$ because $0!+1=2$. A convention requiring positive $n$ would instead give $f(2)=1$.

## What is known

**Wilson's theorem** states that

$$
(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p
$$

for every prime $p$. Consequently $f(p)$ always exists and, under the nonnegative convention, satisfies $0\le f(p)\le p-1$.

That universal upper bound does not settle either distributional question. The historical formulation is attributed to Erdős, Hardy and Subbarao; their 2002 article also discusses how rarely the endpoint $f(p)=p-1$ might occur. The maintained catalogue continues to regard the two main questions as open.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2343](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2343#issuecomment-6157194686) compares the original problem description with the [frozen Lean translation](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/1072.lean), which uses a nonnegative minimum and leaves both distribution conjectures unproved.

The finite modular factorial examples were independently calculated. The original 2002 Hardy–Subbarao paper has not yet been page-checked, and a later reported bound on a subsequence of ratios has not been accepted as an independently verified proof.

## What remains

The required mathematical result is an infinitude theorem, a counterexample, or a density statement with rigorous quantifiers. The next bounded source task is to read the original 2002 article, reconstruct its precise conjectures, and reproduce a larger prime table by two independent algorithms. No finite table can prove the asymptotic assertion.
