## The question in plain language

Let $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be the increasing sequence of positive **powerful numbers**: every prime divisor of $a_i$ appears to exponent at least two. Erdős asks whether there are only finitely many indices $i$ such that three **consecutive members of the powerful-number sequence** form an arithmetic progression:

$$
a_i+a_{i+2}=2a_{i+1}.
$$

The question is open. "Consecutive" refers to positions in the powerful-number sequence, **not** three consecutive integers. [Erdős Problem #938](https://www.erdosproblems.com/938).

## A small example

*A nontrivial finite example*

The integers $1728=2^6 3^3$, $1764=2^2 3^2 7^2$, and $1800=2^3 3^2 5^2$ are powerful and form an arithmetic progression of common difference $36$. There are no other powerful integers strictly between $1728$ and $1800$. Hence these are three consecutive terms of the powerful-number sequence, not merely three arbitrarily chosen powerful numbers.

This illustrates why an isolated arithmetic progression does not resolve a finiteness conjecture. Finite verification of the interval can be done by factoring each integer or by testing whether it has the representation $u^2v^3$ with $v$ squarefree.

## An exact characterization

A positive integer $m$ is powerful if and only if $m=u^2v^3$ for integers $u,v\ge1$. To see this, any exponent $e\ge2$ can be written as $2a+3b$ with $a,b\ge0$: use $b=0$ when $e$ is even and $b=1$ when $e$ is odd. Selecting $b\in\{0,1\}$ for each prime produces squarefree $v$. The converse is immediate by valuation. This gives an exact finite enumeration procedure without relying on unproved distribution estimates.

One must also check the absence of **other** powerful integers between the first and third terms. A solution of $x+z=2y$ in powerful numbers does not automatically solve #938.

## What is known and the recent proposal

The maintained catalogue continues to label #938 **open** and links its statement to Erdős [Er76d]. It distinguishes the question from [Problem #364](https://www.erdosproblems.com/364), which concerns three consecutive *integers* that are powerful.

A May 2026 discussion comment suggests Pell-equation constructions of powerful-number progressions, combined with a density statement about intervals containing exactly three powerful numbers, as a route toward infinitely many **consecutive-term** progressions. That last combination has not been proved in the cited comment: a positive-density family of intervals need not intersect a prescribed exponentially sparse Pell sequence infinitely often. It remains a proposal, not a disproof of Erdős's finiteness question. [Problem discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/938).

## GCL's current state

The numerical example and the $u^2v^3$ characterization are independently checkable. The existence of infinitely many consecutive-term progressions, or alternatively a global finiteness bound, is **not established**. A finite search can produce examples but cannot distinguish the two possibilities.
