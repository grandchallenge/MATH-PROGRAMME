## The question in plain language

Choose an integer $u\geq2$. Imagine making larger numbers using only prime factors that already divide $u$. How far must one travel beyond $u$ to find the next such number? Call that number $f(u)$:

$$
f(u)=\min\{v>u:\text{every prime factor of }v\text{ divides }u\}.
$$

These are sometimes called numbers *smooth over the prime divisors of* $u$. For example, if $u=6=2\cdot3$, allowed numbers have the form $2^a3^b$ for nonnegative integers $a,b$. The first one strictly greater than $6$ is $8$, so $f(6)=8$.

The original 1980 Erdős–Graham question is open-ended: **estimate $f(u)$**. It does not request one uniquely specified asymptotic formula. The maintained catalogue's **SOLVED** label records substantive bounds and typical behavior; it should not be read as a claim that $f(u)$ has the same pointwise growth for all integers.

## A small example

For a prime $u=31$, the only positive integers with prime factors drawn from $u$ are powers of $31$. Therefore $f(31)=31^2=961$. In sharp contrast, $u=30$ has prime factors $2,3,5$, and $32=2^5$ is allowed, so $f(30)=32$. Similarly, $f(14)=16$ and $f(126)=128$.

The exact finite regression fixture checks

$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
u&2&3&6&14&30&31&62&126\\\hline
f(u)&4&9&8&16&32&961&64&128
\end{array}
$$

by trial division and greatest-common-divisor elimination. The examples reveal why a universal pointwise asymptotic would be too optimistic: near neighbors can have radically different collections of prime factors.

## What is known

There are simple **exact universal bounds**:

$$
u+2\leq f(u)\leq u^2\qquad(u\geq2).
$$

For the lower bound, $u+1$ is coprime to $u$ and exceeds $1$, so it cannot be built from primes dividing $u$. For the upper bound, $u^2$ uses only prime factors of $u$. Both inequalities are attained infinitely often. For every prime $p$, $f(p)=p^2$. For $u=2^k-2$ with $k\geq2$, the integer $u$ is even and $u+2=2^k$ is allowed, so $f(u)=u+2$. Thus no single pointwise growth rate describes all $u$.

The stronger result concerns **typical** rather than exceptional integers. For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $\delta>0$, there is an $X$ such that, for all $x\geq X$, at least $(1-\delta)x$ positive integers $u\leq x$ satisfy

$$
f(u)<(1+\varepsilon)u.
$$

Equivalently, $f(u)/u\to1$ in natural density. This does **not** mean $f(u)/u\to1$ along every sequence: the prime sequence supplies counterexamples.

Here is the main idea of the density-one proof attributed to Stijn Cambie in the maintained problem discussion. Given $\delta>0$, choose a fixed cutoff $M$ so that the proportion of integers divisible by **at least two distinct primes $p,q\leq M$** exceeds $1-\delta/2$. Such an $M$ exists: the sum of reciprocals of primes diverges, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives the exact density of divisibility patterns for each fixed finite prime set. In particular, the density of integers divisible by at most one prime from that set can be made arbitrarily small.

For any two distinct primes $p,q$, the ratio $\log p/\log q$ is irrational: a rational ratio would imply $p^a=q^b$ for positive integers $a,b$. The resulting irrational-rotation argument shows that the multiplicative semigroup

$$
\{p^a q^b:a,b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\}
$$

meets every interval $(t,(1+\varepsilon)t)$ once $t$ is sufficiently large. One can see the mechanism by taking a finite $\eta$‑net of residues $a\log p$ modulo $\log q$, then choosing a sufficiently large nonnegative exponent of $q$ to put a semigroup logarithm inside any desired interval of length $\log(1+\varepsilon)$.

There are only finitely many pairs $p,q\leq M$, so their thresholds have a common finite maximum. Consequently, for all sufficiently large $u$ divisible by at least two of those primes, some $p^a q^b$ lies between $u$ and $(1+\varepsilon)u$ and uses only prime factors of $u$. This proves the stated density-one estimate. The elementary argument establishes the quantifiers and the mechanism; a formal Lean replay remains a separate obligation.
