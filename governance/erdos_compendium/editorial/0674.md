## The question in plain language

Can three integers greater than one satisfy the surprising equation

$$

x^x y^y=z^z\,?

$$

[Erdős Problem #674](https://www.erdosproblems.com/674) asks about integer solutions. The answer to the **existence** question is **yes**: explicit solutions, indeed an infinite family, were known from Ko's work. The deeper historical issue of classifying *all* nontrivial solutions is different from finding one.

## A small example

The maintained catalogue gives the exact integers

$$

x=2^{12}\cdot3^6=2\,985\,984,\qquad

y=2^{8}\cdot3^{8}=1\,679\,616,\qquad

z=2^{11}\cdot3^7=4\,478\,976.

$$

All three bases involve only the prime factors $2$ and $3$. To check the enormous-power equation we therefore need only match **prime valuations**.

The exponent of $2$ in $x^x y^y$ is $12x+8y=49\,268\,736$, while in $z^z$ it is $11z=49\,268\,736$. The exponent of $3$ in the left-hand side is $6x+8y=31\,352\,832$, while in the right-hand side it is $7z=31\,352\,832$. Because no other prime divides either side, equality of these two valuations proves the entire identity exactly.

## What is known

The catalogue attributes infinitely many examples to Ko. Its parameterization can be put in a readable form. Choose an integer $r\geq2$ and let

$$

u=2^r,\qquad v=u-1,\qquad

T=2^{2u(v-r)}v^{2v}.

$$

Define

$$

x=Tu^2,\qquad y=Tv^2,\qquad z=2Tuv.

$$

All quantities are positive integers. The condition $r\geq2$ ensures $v-r\geq0$. For $r=2$, $u=4$, $v=3$ and $T=2^8 3^6$, recovering the concrete example above.

**A direct verification of the family.** The desired equation is equivalent, by taking logarithms, to

$$

x\log x+y\log y-z\log z=0.

$$

Substituting the three definitions and dividing by the positive number $T$ gives

$$

(u-v)^2\log T

+2(u-v)(u\log u-v\log v)

-2uv\log2.

$$

Since $u-v=1$, $u=2^r$ and

$$

\log T=2u(v-r)\log2+2v\log v,

$$

the expression becomes zero term by term. Exponentiation proves $x^x y^y=z^z$ for every $r\geq2$. This is an independent elementary **verification of the existing catalogue-listed formula**, not a newly discovered infinite family.

An additional property follows immediately:

$$

z^2=4xy.

$$

This relationship describes the Ko examples, but **does not imply** that every possible solution has it.

The source reports partial structural results by Schinzel, Mills, Dem'janenko and Uchiyama, including restrictions involving $4xy$ compared with $z^2$ and the prime divisors of $x,y,z$. Their precise hypotheses matter. Results classifying certain regimes cannot automatically be combined into a complete classification without inspecting their original statements and proofs.
