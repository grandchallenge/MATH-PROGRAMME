## The question in plain language

Start with Euler's totient function $\phi(n)$, the number of integers from $1$ through $n$ that are coprime to $n$. Apply it repeatedly: $\phi_1(n)=\phi(n)$ and $\phi_k(n)=\phi(\phi_{k-1}(n))$. Since the values decrease until they reach $1$, define

$$
f(n)=\min\{k\geq 1:\phi_k(n)=1\}.
$$

Erdős Problem 408 asks three related questions. Does the normalized quantity $f(n)/\log n$ have a limiting distribution as $n$ ranges over the positive integers? Is it concentrated near one constant for almost all $n$, in the natural-density sense? And, after about $\log\log n$ iterations, how large can the largest prime factor of $\phi_k(n)$ still be?

The first question means asking for a nondecreasing function $F$ whose values are the limiting densities of the sets where $f(n)/\log n\leq c$. The precise modern formulation uses continuity points of $F$, because a distribution function need not have a density value that behaves continuously at a jump. The second asks whether some constant $\alpha$ satisfies $|f(n)/\log n-\alpha|<\varepsilon$ for a density-one set of $n$, for every $\varepsilon>0$. The third is deliberately less formal: “$k=\log\log n$” is not an integer for every $n$, and the intended asymptotic comparison needs a convention.

## A small example

Here is an exact calculation that can be checked using only the totient formula. For $n=72$,

$$
72\mathbin{\longmapsto}24\mathbin{\longmapsto}8\mathbin{\longmapsto}4\mathbin{\longmapsto}2\mathbin{\longmapsto}1.
$$

Indeed, factoring $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ gives $\phi(72)=72(1-1/2)(1-1/3)=24$; then $\phi(24)=8$, $\phi(8)=4$, $\phi(4)=2$, and $\phi(2)=1$. Thus the exact finite certificate is $f(72)=5$. The same calculation gives $f(32)=5$ from $32\mapsto16\mapsto8\mapsto4\mapsto2\mapsto1$.

More generally, $\phi(2^j)=2^{j-1}$ for $j\geq1$, so the chain from $2^j$ reaches $1$ after exactly $j$ steps. This is a useful warning about informal logarithmic bounds: any asserted strict upper bound of the form $f(n)<\log_2 n$ fails on powers of two under the convention $k\geq1$. The finite calculation verifies neither a limiting distribution nor an “almost always” statement. It only checks the definition and exposes an endpoint normalization that matters in later theorem statements.

## What is known

Pillai's iterates paper gives, for every $n\geq2$, the corrected bounds recorded by the formal source,

$$
\left\lfloor\frac{\log n-\log 2}{\log 3}\right\rfloor+1\leq f(n)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\log n}{\log 2}\right\rfloor+1.
$$

The maintained problem page instead displays the shorter asymptotic-looking inequalities $\log_3 n<f(n)<\log_2 n$ and its bibliography labels Pillai as pages 832–836. The primary iterates reference is identified elsewhere, and in the formal source, as *Bull. Amer. Math. Soc.* 35 (1929), pages 837–841. The discrepancy should remain visible until a coordinator reconciles the catalogue metadata against the paper.

Erdős, Granville, Pomerance, and Spiro's primary paper *On the normal behavior of the iterates of some arithmetic functions* (1990) gives the central positive evidence. Its Theorem 2.2, printed page 171, says that the iteration length has normal order $\alpha\log n$ for some $\alpha>0$, under the paper's strong hypothesis $B_\varepsilon$, a form of the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture. This conditional result supports the second question and, in the usual interpretation, the first as well; it is not an unconditional solution.

For the largest-prime-factor direction, Ford, Konyagin, and Luca's primary paper *Prime chains and Pratt trees*, Theorem 5, proves that for every $\varepsilon,\delta>0$ there is an integer $k$ such that, for all sufficiently large $x$, at least $(1-\delta)x$ integers $n\leq x$ satisfy $P^+(\phi_k(n))\leq x^\varepsilon$. This is a genuine unconditional result, but its quantifiers do not directly settle the particular choice $k=\log\log n$. The maintained page's “likely true” sentence is therefore best described as an open extrapolation, not as a theorem supplied by that paper.
