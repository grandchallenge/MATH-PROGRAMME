## The question in plain language

Given $n$ distinct points $A\subset\mathbb R^2$, let $d_1,\ldots,d_k$ be their distinct **positive** pairwise distances. For each distance $d$, define the **unordered-pair** multiplicity

$$
m_A(d)=\#\bigl\{\{x,y\}\subset A:x\ne y,\ \|x-y\|=d\bigr\}.
$$

Erdős asked whether the simultaneous conditions

$$
k=n-1,\qquad
\{m_A(d_1),\ldots,m_A(d_k)\}=\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
$$

characterize configurations of equally spaced points on a straight line or a circular arc. **The characterization is false**, including for arbitrarily large $n$. Clemen, Dumitrescu and Liu published a family of counterexamples in 2025. The maintained [Problem #958](https://www.erdosproblems.com/958) labels the problem **DISPROVED (LEAN)**, but the published geometric result and a reported Lean proof of a particular finite witness must be kept distinct.

## A four-point counterexample with exact arithmetic

Consider the four points

$$
A=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)\}.
$$

Their six unordered squared distances, counted once per unordered pair, are

$$
1,1,1,\quad 2,2,\quad 4.
$$

Thus the three distances are $1,\sqrt2,2$, with respective multiplicities $3,2,1$. In particular $k=3=n-1$ and their set of multiplicities is exactly $\{1,2,3\}$. Yet this set is **not collinear**, because $(1,0)$ is off the vertical line containing the other three points. It is **not cocircular**, because three distinct collinear points cannot all lie on a nondegenerate Euclidean circle. The counterexample therefore disposes of the unqualified all-$n$ characterization.

It does **not** by itself refute a formulation restricted to sufficiently large $n$. The 2025 paper supplies that stronger refutation. Treating the four-point example alone as a proof against every large-$n$ formulation would be a logical error.

## An important source-fidelity correction: ordered versus unordered pairs

The public #958 page currently says that $f(d)$ counts **ordered** pairs, while simultaneously requiring the multiplicities $\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$. These two clauses are incompatible: every positive-distance unordered pair $\{x,y\}$ contributes both $(x,y)$ and $(y,x)$, so the ordered multiplicity is $2m_A(d)$ and is always **even**. In particular no ordered-pair multiplicity can equal $1$.

Under the page's literal ordered wording, even equally spaced collinear configurations fail the stated test. For example $n$ equally spaced points on a line have unordered multiplicities $n-1,n-2,\ldots,1$ but ordered multiplicities $2(n-1),2(n-2),\ldots,2$. Therefore the meaningful theorem from Erdős and the subsequent paper uses **unordered** pairs. This is a documented statement normalization, not a silent editorial rewrite. The independent [statement-fidelity assessment](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/distance_problems/E0958) also flags the error.

There is a second historical distinction. In [Er84c, p.135], Erdős describes small exceptions and expects the characterization to hold beyond them. The site's remark that Erdős conjectured that other configurations must exist conflicts with this original wording. The manuscript follows Erdős's source and the later peer-reviewed paper rather than attributing the opposite conjecture to him.

## What has been proved

Felix Christian Clemen, Adrian Dumitrescu and Dingyuan Liu, *On multiplicities of interpoint distances*, **Acta Mathematica Hungarica 177** (2025), pp.231–245, DOI [10.1007/s10474-025-01562-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10474-025-01562-y), establish an explicit counterexample family for arbitrarily large point sets. One construction uses $n-1$ equally spaced points along a **short circular arc** together with the center. It has distance-multiplicity profile $(n-1,n-2,\ldots,1)$ but does not lie wholly on one line or one circle. Their result refutes Erdős's asymptotic characterization, not merely its unrestricted four-point version.

The paper also exhibits point sets whose distance multiplicities are all distinct but whose profile is **not** $(n-1,\ldots,1)$. These are separate statements: all distinct multiplicities do not force exactly $n-1$ different distances or a specific multiplicity list.

The catalogue's "(LEAN)" label refers to a reported formal proof of a small counterexample. The independent research dossier identifies a Lean artifact attributed to Boris Alexeev and Aristotle (Harmonic) for the four-point configuration; that file has **not** been independently compiled, equivalence-checked or kernel/axiom-audited by GCL. A verified *small* counterexample cannot, without the published family construction, settle a quantified large-$n$ version.

## Why the counterexample works conceptually

Equally spaced collinear points produce multiplicities $n-j$ for distances equal to $j$ spacing units: each of the $n-j$ pairs whose positions differ by $j$ contributes once. The tempting inference is that the multiplicity profile reveals the geometry uniquely. It does not. Different geometric configurations can have the same multiset of pair-distance frequencies. The arc-and-center example arranges equal-radius center-to-arc distances alongside distinct chord-length families, recreating the profile without preserving collinearity or cocircularity.

This illustrates a general inverse-problem failure: knowing how **often** each distance occurs loses which points realize it.
