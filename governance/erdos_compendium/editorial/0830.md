## The question in plain language

Amicable pair a,b is positive integers with sigma(a)=sigma(b)=a+b; count A(x)=#{(a,b):1<=a<=b<=x, amicable}. Determine whether infinitely many such pairs exist, and stronger whether A(x)>x^(1-o(1)); 'a<=b' includes diagonal only if any such positive self-amicable interpretation satisfies sigma(a)=2a (perfect numbers), a possible source-semantic issue! Usually amicable pairs require DISTINCT a≠b, despite formula a<=b. Must not silently count perfect numbers.

## A small example

sigma(220)=504=sigma(284)=220+284. Positive perfect number 6 satisfies sigma(6)=12=6+6, meaning literal a=b=6 meets displayed equations but is NOT an amicable pair under distinct-member convention. The equality is an explicit source-normalization hold.

The example can be checked exactly. A finite witness does not replace the universal, asymptotic, or infinitely-often quantifiers in the original question.

## What is known

Maintained https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/830 OPEN, example 220/284; Erdős [Er55b] shows A(x)=o(x); Pomerance [Po81] upper x exp(-(log x)^(1/3)), improved [Po15] x exp(-(1/2+o(1))sqrt(log x loglog x)). These upper bounds are compatible with x^(1-o(1)) lower, and none settles infinitude. The printed 'a<=b' perhaps allows perfect diagonals; standard amicable pair definition excludes equality. Original source theorem scopes must be locked.

The defining equation is

$$

\sigma(a)=\sigma(b)=a+b.

$$

It holds for the classical distinct pair $(220,284)$ since both divisor sums equal 504. However, it also holds for the diagonal pair $(6,6)$ because $\sigma(6)=12$. Thus the printed inequality $a\leq b$ and the conventional requirement that amicable numbers be **distinct** must be reconciled before accepting the definition of $A(x)$. The Pomerance bounds do not establish the infinitude question.
