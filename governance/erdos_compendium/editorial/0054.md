## The question in plain language

Color every element of an infinite set of positive integers with one of two colors. Can every sufficiently large integer be written as a sum of distinct elements all of one color? Erdős asked how sparse such a set can be. Conlon, Fox and Pham determined the optimal quadratic-logarithmic counting scale.

Registered statement:

> A set of integers $A$ is Ramsey $2$-complete if, whenever $A$ is $2$-coloured, all sufficiently large integers can be written as a monochromatic sum of elements of $A$.



## A small example

Let $S$ be a finite sequence of positive integers, and let $\Sigma(S)$ be its distinct-element subset sums. For positive integers $m,q$,

$$
|\Sigma(S)\cap[1,m]|
\le
2^{m/q}\prod_{a\in S\cap[1,m]}(1+2^{-a/q}).
$$

To verify it, expand the product. Each chosen subcollection with sum $s\le m$ contributes

$$
2^{m/q}2^{-s/q}=2^{(m-s)/q}\ge1.
$$

Hence the weighted total counts every representable $s\le m$ at least once. Finally,

$$
1+z\le e^z
$$

gives

$$
|\Sigma(S)\cap[1,m]|
\le
2^{m/q}\exp\!\left(\sum_{a\in S\cap[1,m]}2^{-a/q}\right).
$$

For example, with $S=\{3,5\}$, $m=8$, and $q=2$,

$$
\Sigma(S)\cap[1,8]=\{3,5,8\},
$$

so the left side is $3$, while the displayed upper bound is larger, as required. CFP use this lemma in their lower-bound coloring argument.

## What is known

Burr and Erdős introduced Ramsey completeness using sequences and subset sums. Their notation $P(A)$ means sums of distinct terms of the sequence $A$.

They proved two things:

1. Every Ramsey-complete sequence must have at least order $(\log N)^2$ terms up to $N$ along an unbounded sequence of cutoffs; this is not a pointwise lower bound at every $N$.
2. They constructed an entirely Ramsey-complete sequence with growth of order at most $(\log N)^3$.

Their paper also explains that repeated values are convenient but not necessary; Theorem 1c gives a strictly increasing version. [Burr–Erdős, 1985](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1985-05.pdf)

Thus the open issue was whether the cubic upper bound could be reduced to the quadratic scale suggested by the lower bound.

Conlon, Fox, and Pham proved the stronger general theorem:

$$
\exists C>0\ \forall r\ge2\ \exists A
$$

such that $A$ is $r$-Ramsey complete and

$$
|A\cap[1,N]|\le Cr(\log N)^2
$$

for all sufficiently large $N$. They also proved that a sequence satisfying

$$
|A\cap[1,N]|\le cr(\log N)^2
$$

eventually cannot be $r$-Ramsey complete, for an absolute $c>0$.

Setting $r=2$ gives an $O((\log N)^2)$ construction, together with a universal $\limsup_{N\to\infty}|A\cap[1,N]|/(\log N)^2>0$ lower bound. Thus the quadratic-logarithmic growth order is optimal in this upper-versus-limsup-lower sense; a pointwise $\Theta((\log N)^2)$ bound for every Ramsey-complete set does not follow. Problem 54 is solved in this extremal order-of-magnitude sense, although the optimal constants are not determined. [Theorem 1.1](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~dconlon/subset_sums.pdf)

The key ingredient is CFP Lemma 2.8.

For large $x$, choose roughly $C r\log x$ integers from $[x,2x)$ having no small prime factor. The lemma says that one can choose them so that every subcollection containing at least a $1/r$ fraction has subset sums covering a long interval of integers around $x\log x$.

In any $r$-coloring, one color occupies at least a $1/r$ fraction of the selected block. Therefore that color alone represents the whole interval.

Now repeat this independently in every dyadic interval

$$
[x_i,2x_i),\qquad x_i=2^ix_0.
$$

The represented intervals overlap because their lengths and locations grow on the same $x_i\log x_i$ scale. Concatenating the blocks gives a Ramsey-complete sequence. There are $O(r\log x_i)$ elements in block $i$, and only $O(\log N)$ relevant blocks below $N$, so

$$
A(N)=O\!\left(r\sum_{i\le O(\log N)}i\right)
=O(r(\log N)^2).
$$

This is an existence proof: the block selection is obtained probabilistically, not as a short explicit formula.
