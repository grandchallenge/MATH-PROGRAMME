## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $N$, choose any collection $A$ of denominators from $1$ through $N$, and add their reciprocals. Problem 311 asks how closely such a sum can approach $1$ without being exactly $1$. Define $delta(N)$ to be the smallest positive value of

$$
\left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}\right|
$$

as $A$ ranges over subsets of ${1,\ldots,N}$. The empty set is allowed, but a zero difference is omitted. The proposed asymptotic question is whether there is a constant $c$ with $0<c<1$ for which

$$
\delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N}
$$

as $N$ tends to infinity through the positive integers. Equivalently, the quantity $-\log(\delta(N))/N$ should converge to $c$.

The formulation cited from Erdős and Graham has an extra admissibility condition: $A$ must not contain a subset $S$ whose reciprocal sum is exactly $1$. The live problem page attributes the observation that this is equivalent to the simpler formulation above to Vjekoslav Kovac. Intuitively, a set containing such an $S$ has a remaining positive reciprocal contribution, at least $1/N$; the exact equality of the two finite minima is reported on the page and in the formal file, but is not a new GCL-certified result.

## A small example

The case $N=3$ can be checked without rounding. Enumerating all eight subsets gives the following non-zero differences:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
A & \sum_{n\in A}1/n & |1-\sum_{n\in A}1/n|\\
\varnothing&0&1\\
\{2\}&1/2&1/2\\
\{1,2\}&3/2&1/2\\
\{3\}&1/3&2/3\\
\{1,3\}&4/3&1/3\\
\{2,3\}&5/6&1/6\\
\{1,2,3\}&11/6&5/6
\end{array}
$$

The subset ${1\}$ gives the excluded zero. Thus exact comparison of the remaining fractions yields $delta(3)=1/6$. This is a reproducible finite check, not evidence that the limiting constant exists, nor a computation of its value. It also illustrates why the problem is delicate: many different subsets can have sums near $1$, while exact equality must be separated from a very small non-zero gap.

## What is known

There is an immediate lower bound. Every difference is a non-zero rational whose denominator divides $L_N=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,N)$, so

$$
\delta(N)\geq\frac{1}{L_N}.
$$

The prime number theorem gives $\log L_N=(1+o(1))N$, hence this says only that $delta(N)$ is at least $e^{-(1+o(1))N}$. It does not provide the conjectured lower bound with a constant strictly below $1$.

A primary source document for a substantial upper bound is Quanyu Tang’s *A Note on Problem #311*, version 2, dated 15 January 2026. Its Theorem 4.1 on PDF page 7 states that there are absolute constants $c_0>0$ and $N_0$ such that, for every $N\geq N_0$,

$$
\delta(N)\leq\exp\left(-c_0\frac{N}{(\log N)^3(\log\log N)^3}\right).
$$

Tang constructs an admissible set whose reciprocal sum is $1-1/t$, with $t$ a suitable least common multiple, and then lower-bounds $t$. This is much smaller than the trivial reciprocal-LCM lower-bound scale, but it is still far weaker than an upper bound of the form $e^{-cN}$ with fixed positive $c$. The page also records Tang’s earlier version with $(\log\log N)^4$ and the later stronger exponent shown above.

The external comments contain further observations, including heuristic and partial lower-bound discussions, but they are not presented here as settled theorems. In particular, the live page still labels the problem open and reports no proof exposition or proof claim.

## What remains

The central unresolved issue is the asymptotic lower side: neither the existence of a limit nor a matching $e^{-cN}$ lower bound with $0<c<1$ has been established by the sources read here. A bounded next task would first normalize the admissible and unrestricted definitions, then formalize a finite exact evaluator for $delta(N)$ for a stated small range, and separately package Tang’s Theorem 4.1 with its exact hypotheses and dependency on Liu–Sawhney. Acceptance would require a source-linked proof transcript or a precisely delimited formal theorem; a finite table alone would not count. The task should stop if the original Erdős–Graham page 40 or the cited dependency cannot be inspected, rather than silently treating a transcription as primary evidence.
