## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 388 asks about collisions between two products of consecutive positive integers. Write

$$
P(m,k)=(m+1)(m+2)\cdots(m+k).
$$

The main question is whether there are only finitely many quadruples $(m_1,k_1,m_2,k_2)$ such that $k_1,k_2>3$, the second block starts after the first ends, $m_1+k_1\leq m_2$, and $P(m_1,k_1)=P(m_2,k_2)$. “Classify” asks for more: describe every such quadruple, not merely prove that their number is finite.

The displayed problem on the maintained page does not spell out the domains of $m_1$ and $m_2$. The original source discusses positive integers, while the current formal interface chooses natural numbers. That choice matters: allowing negative starting points introduces sign and symmetry phenomena that are not the intended separated positive-block question. This article therefore treats the nonnegative-natural-number interface as the working normalization, while recording the domain choice as a formalization decision rather than silently attributing it to the source.

The same page gives a broader variant with fixed positive multipliers $a$ and $b$, but it does not specify all domain, length, or separation hypotheses. That variant is useful context, not a substitute for the exact main statement.

## A small example

There is a genuine separated collision:

$$
8\cdot9\cdot10\cdot11\cdot12\cdot13\cdot14
=17{,}297{,}280
=63\cdot64\cdot65\cdot66.
$$

It has $(m_1,k_1,m_2,k_2)=(7,7,62,4)$. The left block ends at $14$, so $m_1+k_1=14\leq62=m_2$, and both lengths exceed $3$. An exact-arithmetic Python check using integer `prod` returned `17297280 17297280 True`; a second check returned `True True True` for equality, separation, and the two length conditions. This verifies one solution, not its uniqueness and not finiteness of the full four-parameter family.

One elementary restriction is already informative. Under the separation condition, every right-hand factor is larger than every left-hand factor. If $k_2\geq k_1$, the first $k_1$ right-hand factors already have product strictly larger than the left-hand product. Therefore any solution must have $k_1>k_2$. This is a proved local lemma for the normalized positive setting, not a solution of the problem.

## What is known

The primary source is Erdős’s 1976 paper, *Problems and results on number theoretic properties of consecutive integers and related questions*. In its discussion around equation (8), printed on the early pages of the paper, Erdős asks whether separated products of consecutive integers have only finitely many solutions. The following paragraphs discuss a rational-ratio generalization and then a related “same prime factors” question. The paper presents these as conjectural questions and records substantial difficulty; it does not provide a resolution of #388.

The maintained page associates the question with Erdős’s 1976 paper, the Erdős–Graham 1980 monograph, and Erdős’s 1992 survey. Those references establish provenance for the question, but a bibliography entry is not a proof of the modern formulation. The live discussion has ten user comments and no proof exposition. One comment reports an attempted fixed-pair finiteness argument using polynomial Diophantine-equation machinery; another explicitly says that an automated check judged the proof incorrect. These are useful leads for a future source-critical review, not accepted results. In particular, they do not settle the global question where both lengths vary.

The exact example above is an independently checked finite calculation. Search reports in the discussion are not treated as exhaustive certificates here because their code, bounds, and reproducibility record are not part of the protected source packet. Likewise, a plausible smooth-number obstruction—large prime factors on the upper block cannot occur in an equality—is a useful observation, but it does not by itself prove that only finitely many blocks can occur.

## What remains

The first need is a source-locked semantic review: compare the original equation (8), the maintained transcription, and the natural-number formalization, including whether “positive integers” and the separation inequality are intended in every variant. The broader $aP(m_1,k_1)=bP(m_2,k_2)$ statement needs its missing hypotheses recorded before any theorem is transferred to it.

A bounded mathematical successor should then test one fixed pair of lengths, with a primary theorem in hand and a complete check that its hypotheses match the polynomial products here. A separate exact-arithmetic search can enumerate a declared finite box and emit all hits with independently recomputed products. Either outcome is useful: a verified reduction would isolate a theorem-sized residual, while a counterexample would falsify any uniqueness claim. Neither task should be described as resolving the varying-length problem unless it actually controls all lengths.
