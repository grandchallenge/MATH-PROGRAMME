## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, write $\omega(n)$ for the number of **distinct** prime factors of $n$. Let $j(n)$ be the smallest positive integer $m$ such that **every** interval of $m$ consecutive integers contains an integer relatively prime to $n$. Define the *Jacobsthal extremal function*

$$
h(k)=\sup_{\substack{n\ge1\\\omega(n)\le k}}j(n).
$$

Erdős asked for its order of magnitude, in particular Jacobsthal's conjecture

$$
h(k)\ll k^2.
$$

The universal quantification over *all moduli with at most $k$ distinct prime factors* matters: the problem is not solely about a primorial. The catalogue [Erdős #970](https://www.erdosproblems.com/970) lists the quadratic question as **OPEN**, but a 25 September 2026 OpenAI mathematical preprint (released 6 October 2026) **claims a stronger affirmative result**, together with a Lean formalization. Independent mathematical and exact-head kernel/statement review has not been completed in GCL. Its honest current classification is **CATALOGUE OPEN / PENDING EXTERNAL RESOLUTION CLAIM**, not "no result exists" and not "GCL certified proved".

## A small example

*A concrete example and the correct off-by-one*

For $n=6=2\cdot3$, the integers $2,3,4$ are all noncoprime to six. The next integer, $5$, is coprime to six. The reduced residue classes modulo six are $1,5$. The maximum run of consecutive noncoprime integers has length three, so

$$
j(6)=4.
$$

In fact $h(1)=2$ and $h(2)=4$. For $k=1$, two consecutive integers cannot both be divisible by the same prime, and the modulus two supplies a one-term noncoprime run. For $k=2$, if one prime is two, among any four consecutive integers the two odd positions are separated by two and cannot both be divisible by the other prime $p\ge3$. If both primes are odd, at most one is three; among four consecutive integers three can divide at most two and any prime at least five can divide at most one, so the two primes cannot cover all four positions. The example $n=6$ achieves the bound.

Write $L(n)$ for the longest run of integers each having a common factor greater than one with $n$. Then

$$
j(n)=L(n)+1.
$$

Accordingly $h(k)$ is one **more** than the greatest length of a run that can be covered by at most $k$ selected prime divisors. The shift must be retained in finite certificates and Lean theorem comparisons.

A useful elementary lower bound is $h(k)\ge k+1$. Select $k$ distinct primes $q_1,\ldots,q_k$. By the Chinese remainder theorem there is a starting integer $b$ with

$$
b+i\equiv0\pmod{q_i}\qquad(1\le i\le k).
$$

Then $b+1,\ldots,b+k$ are all noncoprime to $n=\prod_iq_i$, so no universal forcing interval of length at most $k$ is possible. This lower bound is weaker than the best published asymptotic examples.

## The residue-class covering equivalent

A run $b+1,\ldots,b+L$ contains **no** integer coprime to $n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}$ precisely when every position $j\in\{1,\ldots,L\}$ lies in one of the $r$ residue classes

$$
j\equiv -b\pmod{p_i}.
$$

Prime powers do not matter; the set of coprime integers depends only on distinct prime divisors. Conversely, for **any** chosen one residue class modulo each of $r$ distinct primes, the Chinese remainder theorem supplies a shift $b$ realizing those classes simultaneously. Hence the conjecture is equivalently a **uniform anti-covering theorem**: no choice of at most $k$ primes and one forbidden residue class per prime can cover $Ck^2$ consecutive positions, for one absolute $C$.

It is not sufficient to show that the *average* shift has an uncovered integer. The adversarial residue configuration must work for **every** prime set and **every** interval location.

## What is known

*Established classical bounds*

Iwaniec [Iw78] proved

$$
h(k)\ll(k\log k)^2,
$$

a uniform fixed-constant bound. Ford, Green, Konyagin, Maynard and Tao [FGKMT18], through long prime-gap constructions, give large *uncovered-interval* obstructions yielding the lower estimate recorded on the maintained page:

$$
h(k)\gg
k\,\frac{(\log k)(\log\log\log k)}
{(\log\log k)^2}
$$

for sufficiently large $k$. Independent later work revisiting the precise primorial-to‐$k$ parameter conversion asserts a stronger bound; this article retains the catalogue's explicitly attributed estimate until the sharper dependence is audited against the original theorem.

These bounds leave the quadratic conjecture unresolved *within the pre-2026 refereed literature*. No elementary or short combinatorial proof eliminating Iwaniec's $(\log k)^2$ loss follows from either estimate.

## September–October 2026 resolution claim

OpenAI's paper [*A quadratic bound for Jacobsthal's function*](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/A-quadratic-bound-for-Jacobsthals-function-September-25-2026/paper.pdf), dated 25 September 2026 and released publicly 6 October 2026, states Theorem 1.1:

$$
h(k)\ \le\ C\,
\frac{k^2}{\bigl(\log\log(3k)\bigr)^2}
\qquad(k\ge1)
$$

with one absolute constant $C>0$. In particular it **implies** the originally conjectured $O(k^2)$ bound. Its technical approach is described as a refinement at the boundary of the linear sieve, retaining the relevant boundary term and using discrepancy/inverse estimates and stopped-count control to handle individually selected residue classes.

This is **an externally claimed theorem**, not a GCL reconstruction of its 83-page proof. The release's formalization catalogue lists a Lean theorem named $\texttt{OAI.Erdos970.Erdos970Final.erdos\_970\_quadratic}$, with comparator challenge *Jacobsthal*, in $\texttt{OAI/NumberTheory/Jacobsthal/Conclusions/QuadraticBound.lean}$. A source-fidelity inspection reports that the theorem quantifies over **all positive $n$** with at most $k$ distinct prime factors and **all integer starting positions**, and witnesses an interval length bounded by $Ck^2$. A separate Lean target, $\texttt{erdos\_970\_iterated\_log}$, corresponds to the stronger logarithmic improvement. The inspector **did not independently rebuild either file**. [Statement and formal-scope inspection](https://vibemathed.com/problem/jacobsthal-function-quadratic-bound).

It is crucial to distinguish:
1. A public PDF *claiming* the $k^2/(\log\log k)^2$ theorem.
2. A release **reporting** Lean verification of its quadratic and improved conclusions.
3. Independent proof review, a pinned rebuild, an axiom report and theorem-fingerprint comparison **by GCL** — none of which is established in this article.

The maintained catalogue still says OPEN; the separate 2026 claim must **not** be dropped merely because its status has not yet been incorporated there.

## What remains open regardless

Even assuming the stronger 2026 theorem passes all reviews, its upper bound does **not determine the true order of $h(k)$**. The published lower estimates are substantially smaller than $k^2/(\log\log k)^2$. Thus the broad "determine the order of magnitude" question remains, while its narrower quadratic-upper-bound conjecture has a **claimed affirmative solution awaiting independent adjudication**.
