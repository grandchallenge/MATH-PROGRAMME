## The question in plain language

Imagine two teams of points, each consisting of $n$ different positions in the plane. Count only the distances between a member of the first team and a member of the second. Can these **cross distances** take dramatically fewer different values than ordinary distances among a comparable number of points?

In [Erdős Problem #661](https://www.erdosproblems.com/661), the question is whether for every sufficiently large $n$ there are two disjoint planar sets

$$
X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\},\qquad
Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_n\}
$$

such that

$$
D(X,Y)=\bigl|\{\lVert x_i-y_j\rVert:1\leq i,j\leq n\}\bigr|
=o\left(\frac n{\sqrt{\log n}}\right).
$$

The symbol $o$ is stronger than $O$: it asks that the ratio of the number of cross distances to $n/\sqrt{\log n}$ tend to **zero**, not merely remain bounded.

The maintained two-dimensional question remains **OPEN**.

## A small example

Put four points equally spaced around the unit circle, and four around a concentric circle of radius $2$:

$$
x_i=(\cos(2\pi i/4),\sin(2\pi i/4)),
$$

$$
y_j=2(\cos(2\pi j/4),\sin(2\pi j/4)),
\qquad 0\leq i,j<4.
$$

The two sets are disjoint. A direct calculation gives

$$
\lVert x_i-y_j\rVert^2
=1+4-4\cos\left(\frac{2\pi(i-j)}4\right).
$$

The cosine takes only the three values $1,0,-1$. Thus the 16 cross pairs determine only

$$
D(X,Y)=3,\qquad
\text{distances }\{1,\sqrt5,3\}.
$$

For arbitrary $n$, two concentric regular $n$-gons of different radii similarly determine exactly $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ different cross distances. That is **linear** in $n$. It demonstrates the benefit of symmetry but does not achieve the target $o(n/\sqrt{\log n})$.

## What is known

**Why bipartite distances are different.** Within-team distances do not count in $D(X,Y)$. This makes it possible to reuse many cross lengths even when the two separate teams each have complex geometry. The cross-distance matrix has $n^2$ entries, but many can coincide.

Define

$$
F(2n)=\min_{\substack{|X|=|Y|=n\\X\cap Y=\varnothing}}
D(X,Y),
\quad
f(2n)=\min_{|Z|=2n}
\bigl|\{\lVert z-z'\rVert:z\ne z'\in Z\}\bigr|.
$$

If we start with a configuration $Z$ that minimizes the usual all-pairs distance count and divide its $2n$ points into two groups of size $n$, its cross distances are a subset of its complete distance set. Therefore

$$
F(2n)\leq f(2n).
$$

The accompanying question is whether the ratio $F(2n)/f(2n)$ actually tends to **zero**. Merely proving $F\leq f$ tells us nothing about that asymptotic improvement.

A 2026 correction in the [maintained statement history](https://www.erdosproblems.com/history/661) fixed a reversed comparison: the meaningful question is **$F=o(f)$**, not $f=o(F)$.

**A higher-dimensional construction that does not transfer.** In four-dimensional Euclidean space, choose $n$ points on the unit circle inside the coordinate plane spanned by the first two axes and $n$ on the circle inside the orthogonal plane spanned by the remaining axes. Every first-team vector is orthogonal to every second-team vector, so

$$
\lVert x_i-y_j\rVert^2
=\lVert x_i\rVert^2+\lVert y_j\rVert^2=2.
$$

All $n^2$ cross distances are exactly $\sqrt2$. This is the Lenz construction mentioned by the catalogue. But two mutually orthogonal two-dimensional linear subspaces require four ambient dimensions. It is not a planar solution and cannot be imported without a new argument.

**The scale $n/\sqrt{\log n}$.** Ordinary two-dimensional lattice point sets provide relatively few distinct distances, governed by representation counts for quadratic forms such as $u^2+v^2$. Splitting a suitably sized finite lattice subset into two groups gives some bipartite configurations with at most $O(n/\sqrt{\log n})$ cross distances, based on published number-theoretic counting estimates. That explains why the question asks for a **little-o improvement**. The lattice estimate alone does not deliver one.

## What remains

A useful bounded investigation would choose one explicit pair of planar point-set families and count their **distinct cross-distance values** using exact arithmetic. The required report must demonstrate that both teams contain $n$ distinct points, verify that they are disjoint, and give an actual asymptotic ratio.

A result of the form

$$
D(X_n,Y_n)\leq C\frac n{\sqrt{\log n}}
$$

for a fixed $C>0$ is *insufficient*. A successful affirmative construction must instead give

$$
\frac{D(X_n,Y_n)\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

A negative result, in turn, would require a universal lower bound incompatible with that convergence. Finite computations or geometrically elegant higher-dimensional arrangements cannot substitute for a planar asymptotic argument.
