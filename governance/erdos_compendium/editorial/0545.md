**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #545 is **OPEN in its intended large-edge form**. Erdős and Graham asked whether, among graphs with a given number of edges and no isolated vertices, the graph with those edges concentrated into an almost-complete graph has the largest two-color Ramsey number. A literal assertion covering *every* small edge count is false: two disjoint edges already give a counterexample. The difficult **eventual** extremal comparison remains unresolved.

## The question in plain language

For a graph $G$, its ordinary two-color Ramsey number $R(G)$ is the smallest integer $N$ such that every coloring of the edges of $K_N$ in red and blue contains a **monochromatic copy** of $G$. The copy need not be induced: extra edges among its chosen vertices do not matter.

Fix the number of edges $m\ge1$ and write it uniquely as

$$
m=\binom n2+t,\qquad 0\le t<n.
$$

Construct $H_{n,t}$ by taking a complete graph $K_n$, then adding one more vertex joined to exactly $t$ of its $n$ vertices. For $t=0$, omit the extra isolated vertex: graphs under comparison must have **no isolated vertices**.

The Erdős–Graham proposal is

$$
\boxed{R(G)\le R(H_{n,t})}
$$

for every graph $G$ with $m$ edges and no isolated vertices, at least for **all sufficiently large $m$**.

The conjectural intuition is that concentrating the edges into a nearly complete graph makes it hardest to force a monochromatic copy. It is much more precise than a claim that all $m$-edge graphs have Ramsey numbers of roughly the same exponential size.

## A small example

Take $m=2$. Then $n=2$, $t=1$, and the prescribed nearly complete graph $H_{2,1}$ is the path $P_3$ with two adjacent edges. Its Ramsey number is

$$
R(P_3)=3.
$$

Every red-blue coloring of the three edges of a triangle uses some color at least twice. Those two edges share a vertex, giving a monochromatic $P_3$; two vertices cannot contain a copy of $P_3$.

But let $G=2K_2$, the graph of **two disjoint edges**. It has the same number of edges and no isolated vertices, and

$$
\boxed{R(2K_2)=5>3=R(P_3).}
$$

Here is a short proof of the second equality. Color the six edges of $K_4$ as a red three-edge star and a blue triangle on its three leaves. Neither color contains two disjoint edges, so $R(2K_2)>4$.

Conversely, in a red-blue coloring of $K_5$, suppose neither color contains two disjoint edges. Then each color class is a family of **pairwise intersecting edges**. On five vertices, such a family has at most four edges: either every edge shares a common center (a star, at most four), or all edges lie in a triangle (at most three). Both colors together could then cover at most eight of the ten edges, which is impossible. Therefore $R(2K_2)\le5$.

This completely settles the **small counterexample**, not the asymptotic question. The [maintained #545 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/545) likewise records further failures for some edge counts through nine, while retaining the conjecture's OPEN status.

## What is known

### The original Erdős–Graham conjecture

P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *On partition theorems for finite graphs*, *Infinite and Finite Sets* (Keszthely 1973), *Colloquia Mathematica Societatis János Bolyai* **10** (1975), 515–527, raised the problem, particularly at printed p.526. Erdős revisited it in *On some problems in graph theory, combinatorial analysis and combinatorial number theory*, *Graph Theory and Combinatorics* (1984), 1–17, p.11. **The complete original 1975 and 1984 papers were not obtained** here, so a purported quotation of their precise all-$m$ versus eventually-$m$ wording would be unwarranted.

The subsequent primary literature firmly identifies the central large-edge conjecture. In Benny Sudakov's *A conjecture of Erdős on graph Ramsey numbers*, *Advances in Mathematics* **227** (2011), 601–609, [complete author's PDF](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~sudakovb/erdos-ramsey-problem.pdf), printed p.602, the author states that Erdős and Graham conjectured the following **triangular-edge special case**:

$$
\boxed{
e(G)=\binom n2,\quad G\text{ has no isolated vertices}
\ \Longrightarrow\ R(G)\le R(K_n).
}
$$

The original 2011 page was directly inspected. Its discussion calls the conjecture difficult and distinguishes it from a weaker exponential bound. The triangular case $t=0$ alone is already an unresolved central obligation for large $n$.

### What Sudakov actually proved

Theorem 1.1 of Sudakov's complete original 2011 paper states

$$
\boxed{R(G)\le 2^{250\sqrt m}}
$$

for **every** finite graph $G$ with $m$ edges and no isolated vertices. This resolved Erdős Problem #546, the weaker challenge of bounding all $m$-edge graph Ramsey numbers by

$$
R(G)\le 2^{O(\sqrt m)}.
$$

The power $\sqrt m$ is the correct exponential scale up to constants, since a complete graph with $m\approx n^2/2$ edges already has Ramsey number exponential in $n\asymp\sqrt m$.

**This does not prove #545.** The target $R(H_{n,t})$ has its own value, and a broad upper estimate like $2^{250\sqrt m}$ is not sharp enough to compare it with $R(H_{n,t})$. Substituting the weaker theorem for the extremal claim would misclassify an open problem as proved.

Earlier Alon–Krivelevich–Sudakov work obtained a bound of form $2^{O(\sqrt m\log m)}$ for general graphs and the desired exponential scale when $G$ is bipartite. Those are valuable context, but likewise do not settle the extremal comparison.

### Partial finite results and their limitations

The maintained catalogue notes explicit small-$m$ failures, including $m=2$, as well as a positive computation for $m=6$ reported in its discussion. An independent [July 2026 finite working report](https://www.erdosproblemaday.com/report/545) also discusses the eight-edge concentrated graph and a 1989 theorem concerning cases with two added edges.

Those external finite computations and the cited 1989 source were **not independently reproduced or checked against a complete original article** in this tranche. They are not used as accepted general theorems here.

Even proving the comparison for every graph with $m$ edges for thousands of individual values would leave the eventual statement open. For a mathematical resolution, one must either show uniform domination by $R(H_{n,t})$ at every sufficiently large $m$, or produce infinitely many distinct edge counts for which another graph has a larger Ramsey number.

## What remains

The principal outstanding theorem is the sharp **triangular case** for all large $n$: among all graphs with $\binom n2$ edges and no isolated vertices, does $K_n$ maximize the ordinary two-color Ramsey number? The general remainder class $0<t<n$ requires a further comparison with $H_{n,t}$.

A bounded constructive task is to search for a precise containment or Ramsey-embedding mechanism turning an arbitrary $m$-edge graph into a sharp comparison with $H_{n,t}$. Its first falsification test should include matching graphs, disconnected graphs and the known small edge-count failures. A method that establishes only a common exponential order, however, will not answer the conjecture.
