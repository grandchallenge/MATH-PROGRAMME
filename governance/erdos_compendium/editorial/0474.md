## The question in plain language

Imagine assigning one of three colors to **every pair of distinct points in the plane**. We do not color the points themselves. The requirement is that **every uncountable collection of points contains pairs of all three colors**.

Could one choose a single coloring with that extraordinary property?

There are as many points in the plane as real numbers. Thus their geometry can be forgotten and the question becomes one about coloring the edges of a complete graph on a set of size

$$
\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0},
$$

the cardinality of the continuum. Write $[X]^2$ for the set of unordered, two-element subsets of $X$. The property is

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\exists c:[\mathbb R^2]^2\longrightarrow\{0,1,2\}
\quad\forall A\subseteq\mathbb R^2,\ 
|A|\geq\aleph_1:\ 
c[[A]^2]=\{0,1,2\}.}
$$

A pair from $A$ must occur in **each** color; it is not enough for just one pair or one particular color to occur.

Erdős Problem #474 asks **which axioms of set theory make such a coloring possible**. Unlike a finite graph-coloring puzzle, the answer can change between models of set theory. Under the **continuum hypothesis** (CH), Erdős's reported result gives the desired coloring. In other consistent set-theoretic universes, no such coloring exists.

It is important not to equate *undecidable from the stated axioms* with *unknown whether a particular mathematical fact is true*. The precise task is to compare the consequences of explicit additional axioms and the models in which those axioms hold.

## A small example

There is a useful **finite analogy**, but it is not a proof of the uncountable result.

Take four vertices labelled $0,1,2,3$ and color the six edges of the complete graph $K_4$ in three perfect matchings:

| Color | Edges |
|---|---|
| Red | $\{0,1\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ |
| Blue | $\{0,2\}$ and $\{1,3\}$ |
| Green | $\{0,3\}$ and $\{1,2\}$ |

Every selection of **three** vertices makes a triangle with one edge of each color. That is the finite analogue of asking every sufficiently large subset to see the full palette.

Now try to give $K_5$ a three-edge-coloring in which **every triangle** sees all three colors. Choose a vertex $v$. Its four incident edges must have four distinct colors: if two had the same color, the triangle containing their other endpoints would already have two edges of one color, leaving too few edges to display all three. Four different incident colors are impossible when only three colors are allowed.

So $K_4$ allows the condition and $K_5$ does not. These calculations illustrate quantifier sensitivity, but neither settles the original infinite-cardinal question. The sets demanded by Erdős are **uncountable**, not three-element sets.

## What is known

### The exact partition statement

Set theorists write

$$
\mathfrak c\longrightarrow[\aleph_1]^2_3
$$

when **every** coloring of unordered pairs from a continuum-sized set with three colors has an $\aleph_1$-sized subset on which **at least one of the colors is absent**.

Therefore the coloring sought by Erdős is the **negation** of that arrow statement:

$$
\mathfrak c\not\longrightarrow[\aleph_1]^2_3.
$$

Notice the square brackets. The positive arrow does *not* require an uncountable monochromatic set: it only guarantees a set that **omits one color**, potentially using the other two.

An $\aleph_1$-sized subset is sufficient for the quantifier because every uncountable set has a subset of that cardinality (using choice). Also, $|\mathbb R^2|=|\mathbb R|$; geometric distance does not enter into this formulation.

### Under the continuum hypothesis

The continuum hypothesis says

$$
2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1.
$$

According to Erdős's historical account, the desired three-coloring exists under CH. Earlier work by Sierpiński and Kurepa established related two-color partition phenomena.

These results are statements **under specified set-theoretic hypotheses**. A theorem under CH does not automatically establish the same coloring in every model where CH fails.

### Shelah's 1988 countermodel

In *Was Sierpiński right? I* (1988), Saharon Shelah directly considers the square-bracket three-color relation. On printed page 356, he states a forcing consistency result in which

$$
2^{\aleph_0}\longrightarrow[\aleph_1]^2_3
$$

holds. Thus **every** three-coloring admits an uncountable subset missing a color. In that model, the desired Erdős coloring does not exist.

The 1988 proof is a forcing argument starting with strong large-cardinal assumptions. Those assumptions are important for understanding what the paper establishes by a **relative consistency proof**, rather than confusing the result with a theorem obtained by elementary finite reasoning.

### A newer 2026 development

Shelah's January 2026 preprint *Consistency of square bracket partition relation*, arXiv:2601.02923v1, states a substantially stronger consistency theorem.

Its Theorem 0.1 says that starting with a ground universe satisfying the generalized continuum hypothesis (GCH), there is a countable-chain-condition forcing extension satisfying

$$
2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_6
$$

and, for every integer $n\geq3$,

$$
\aleph_5\longrightarrow[\aleph_2]^2_{n,2}.
$$

The subscript $(n,2)$ means that **every** coloring by $n$ colors has a subset of cardinality $\aleph_2$ on which at most **two** colors appear.

This statement has an immediate consequence relevant to Erdős #474. Given a three-coloring of pairs from a continuum-sized set, restrict it to any subset of size $\aleph_5$. Apply the new theorem with $n=3$. We obtain a subset of size $\aleph_2$ on which at most two colors appear. It has an $\aleph_1$-sized subset with the same restriction. Consequently,

$$
\aleph_6\longrightarrow[\aleph_1]^2_3.
$$

Because $\mathfrak c=\aleph_6$ in this extension, the required all-three-colors coloring **does not exist** there.

The advance is in the reported **consistency strength**: unlike the older construction, the 2026 theorem as stated does **not require a large cardinal**. The full forcing proof has not been independently checked by GCL, so the manuscript presents this as an author-stated primary research result, not an independently certified theorem.

It also does **not** answer the finer question posed in the maintained catalogue: can the **negative** arrow, hence the successful Erdős coloring, hold when the continuum is specifically $\aleph_2$? The example $\mathfrak c=\aleph_6$ is not a model with $\mathfrak c=\aleph_2$.

The example using $K_4$ and the impossibility of the analogous $K_5$ three-coloring can be checked by finite combinatorics. These examples are for pedagogy, **not evidence about independence over ZFC**.
