## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 352 asks whether there is one finite number that is large enough to force a unit-area triangle inside every sufficiently large measurable subset of the plane. In symbols, the set is a measurable $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, its Lebesgue measure is at least some $c>0$, and the desired conclusion is that three points of $A$ are the vertices of a triangle whose area is exactly $1$.

The quantifiers matter. The same constant $c$ must work for every measurable set, including disconnected, unbounded, and highly irregular sets. The triangle may be chosen anywhere in the set; no convexity or boundedness is assumed. The problem asks whether some finite $c$ exists, not whether the historically suggested value is already proved.

There is also an endpoint issue. Several sources formulate the forcing condition with measure strictly greater than $c$, while the live problem page and the pinned formal statement use measure at least $c$. Those formulations should not be silently identified. The open disk whose largest possible inscribed triangle has area $1$ has area $4\pi/\sqrt{27}$, but its boundary is absent, so no triangle in that open disk has area exactly $1$. Thus this disk rules out the non-strict assertion at that particular endpoint; it does not rule out the existence of some larger finite threshold.

## A small example

The discussion attached to the live problem introduces a useful discrete model. For a finite set $S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^2$, call it $N$ avoiding when every triple from $S$ has $\lvert \mathrm{area}(\triangle)-N\rvert$ greater than the triangle's Euclidean diameter. This is a scaled finite model, not an equivalent theorem by itself.

Here is an exact check for $N=2$. Take the four corners

$$
S=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}.
$$

Among all $4^3=64$ ordered triples, the ordinary triangle area is either $0$ or $1/2$. The largest possible diameter is $\sqrt{2}$. In the first case, $\lvert 0-2\rvert=2>\sqrt{2}$; in the second, $\lvert 1/2-2\rvert=3/2>\sqrt{2}$, since $9/4>2$. This verifies the predicate using only integer arithmetic after squaring the positive quantities. It is a reproducible finite witness for the toy model. It does not prove a bound for arbitrary measurable planar sets, and it says nothing about the asymptotic growth of the largest sets avoiding $N$.

## What is known

The historical sources cited by the catalogue record Erdős's observation that an unbounded measurable set of positive measure, and in particular a set of infinite measure, contains triangles of prescribed positive area. The difficult case is therefore a bounded finite-measure set. Erdős suggested the constant $c_0=4\pi/\sqrt{27}\approx 2.418$ because an equilateral triangle of area $1$ is inscribed in a disk of that area. The disk calculation gives a sharp obstruction to any smaller threshold, with the endpoint caveat described above.

R. Daniel Mauldin states the problem as Problem 5.1 in his 2013 paper *Some Problems and Ideas of Erdős in Analysis and Geometry* (PDF pp. 6–7, publication pp. 371–372). He records the reduction to unions of interiors of finitely many compact convex sets as Problem 5.2. The same discussion reports that the conjectured constant is optimal for one, two, and three convex bodies. These are substantial restricted results, but they do not settle the unrestricted measurable-set question. The live catalogue also attributes to Freiling and Mauldin a result saying that outer measure greater than $c_0$ forces a triangle of area greater than $1$; that statement is useful evidence, but “greater than $1$” is not automatically “equal to $1$” for an arbitrary disconnected measurable set.

The live Erdős Problems page currently labels the problem OPEN and says that no complete or partial solution is registered there. Its forum contains finite-model observations and computational claims, but the page explicitly warns that comments are not verified mathematical results. They are therefore context, not proof authority.

## What remains

The smallest useful next step is a source-locked semantic audit of the restricted convex-union theorem. It should transcribe the exact strictness of each measure hypothesis, the meaning of “contains,” the convexity and compactness assumptions, and whether each conclusion gives area exactly $1$ or merely greater than $1$. A bounded accompanying lemma can verify that, inside a convex set, a triangle of area greater than $1$ can be continuously reduced along a segment to one of area exactly $1$. Acceptance would require a line-by-line match to the cited pages and a proof of that interpolation lemma; a mismatch in endpoint, set class, or area conclusion would falsify the package. The task should stop after the source comparison and lemma check, without presenting the restricted result as a solution of the full problem.

For the unrestricted question, what remains is a genuinely global argument controlling arbitrary measurable sets, or a counterexample showing that every proposed finite threshold fails. Finite lattice searches can test models and produce intuition, but they cannot decide this continuum statement. No source inspected here supplies a complete resolution.
