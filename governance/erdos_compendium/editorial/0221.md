## The question in plain language

Can one choose a sparse subset $A$ of the positive integers so that **every sufficiently large integer** is the sum of a power of two and an element of $A$? Specifically, Erdős asks for

$$
|A\cap[1,N]|\ll\frac{N}{\log N}
$$

and representations $m=2^k+a$ with $k\ge0$ and $a\in A$. The power of two is one summand, not a product or an arbitrary smooth number.

## A small example

If $A$ contains every positive integer, then any sufficiently large $m$ has the representation $m=1+(m-1)$, but $|A\cap[1,N]|=N$ fails the required sparseness. A useful construction must simultaneously ensure coverage of all sufficiently large integers and retain only about one out of every $\log N$ candidates.

For $m\ge2$, every possible representation has $2^k<m$ and $a=m-2^k>0$. Checking a single power of two would be incomplete; the covering condition is existential over all nonnegative $k$.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Ruzsa (1972) found a set meeting the conjectured order. The maintained [Erdős #221](https://www.erdosproblems.com/221) displays an ingenious construction based on numbers $5^t m$ selected when $5^t$ exceeds a small constant times $\log m$, plus two adjacent shifts. The key number-theoretic ingredient is that **two is a primitive root modulo $5^t$** for every $t\ge1$.

Lorentz (1954) had established the weaker cardinality bound $N\log\log N/\log N$. In a later refinement, Ruzsa (2001) constructed an **exact additive complement** with

$$
|A\cap[1,N]|\sim\frac{N}{\log_2 N}.
$$

Here $\log_2N$ means the base-two logarithm, so the main term is $(\log 2)N/\log N$. This sharp asymptotic cardinality is stronger than the original big-oh statement.

## What remains

A bounded task verifies that $2$ generates the units modulo $5^t$ for $t=1,2,3,4$ by exact order calculations, and checks finite coverage of sample targets for a correctly normalized Ruzsa construction. Acceptance: the shifts $\{0,1\}$ are included, $k=0$ is allowed, and finite coverage is not misreported as an asymptotic density result. The full source proof requires a separate audit of the sparse counting bound.
