**Editorial status:** Unaccepted mathematical explanation of an established identity and its later generalizations. The 1970 and 2005–2006 primary articles have been inspected. External Lean source claims are not certified by GCL.

## The question in plain language

A famous sequence of positive integers starts

$$
1,2,3,4,6,9,13,19,27,38,54,77,109,\ldots
$$

It comes from a deceptively simple instruction: start at $a_1=1$, and repeatedly multiply the previous number, plus one half, by $\sqrt2$, then discard the fractional part.

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
a_1=1,\qquad
a_{n+1}=\left\lfloor\sqrt2\left(a_n+\frac12\right)\right\rfloor
\quad(n\ge1).
}
$$

Graham and Pollak discovered that this nonlinear recurrence can **read the binary digits of $\sqrt2$**. Compute the differences

$$
d_n=a_{2n+1}-2a_{2n-1},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots
$$

and every $d_n$ equals either $0$ or $1$. Together the differences give the binary expansion of $\sqrt2$, in the convention where its **leading integer digit** counts as digit 1:

$$
\sqrt2=(1.011010100\ldots)_2,\qquad
d_1,d_2,d_3,\ldots=1,0,1,1,0,1,0,1,\ldots
$$

This is already a theorem, proved by **Ronald Graham and Henry Pollak** in 1970. The remaining part of Erdős Problem #482 was a request for **analogous recurrences for other algebraic numbers**, not a conjecture that the very same recurrence works for every square root.

**Thomas Stoll's 2005 and 2006 papers provide extensive generalizations**, using parameterized recurrences that extract digits of many positive real numbers in arbitrary integer bases.

## A small example

To see the mechanism, write the first few terms next to their extracted digits.

| $n$ | $a_{2n-1}$ | $a_{2n+1}$ | $a_{2n+1}-2a_{2n-1}$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 3 | **1** |
| 2 | 3 | 6 | **0** |
| 3 | 6 | 13 | **1** |
| 4 | 13 | 27 | **1** |
| 5 | 27 | 54 | **0** |
| 6 | 54 | 109 | **1** |

The digits in the last column begin $101101$, which agrees with the first six binary digits of $\sqrt2$ when its leading digit is included.

For example,

$$
d_3=a_7-2a_5=13-2(6)=1,
\qquad
d_4=a_9-2a_7=27-2(13)=1.
$$

There is an indexing trap: the *first digit after the binary point* of $\sqrt2$ is $0$, not $1$. Graham and Pollak count the leading integer $1$ as the first digit, so $d_1=1$. An implementation that instead starts with the first fractional digit will appear to disagree even when the recurrence is correct.

The recurrence can be computed **exactly, with integers only**. For a nonnegative integer $a$, we have

$$
\left\lfloor\sqrt2\left(a+\frac12\right)\right\rfloor
=
\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{(2a+1)^2}{2}}\right\rfloor
=
\operatorname{isqrt}\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{(2a+1)^2}{2}\right\rfloor
\right).
$$

Here $\operatorname{isqrt}(N)$ is the largest integer whose square is at most $N$. This avoids rounding errors that would eventually become a problem if the rapidly growing sequence were computed using ordinary floating-point values.

## What is known

### The original Graham–Pollak formula

The complete three-page original article is **R. L. Graham and H. O. Pollak, *Note on a Nonlinear Recurrence Related to $\sqrt2$*, Mathematics Magazine 43 (1970), 143–145**.

On printed page 143 the authors establish an explicit formula for the sequence. In their general version the starting value is any positive integer $m$. For our starting value $m=1$, the formula simplifies to

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
a_n=
\left\lfloor
2^{(n-1)/2}+2^{(n-2)/2}
\right\rfloor\quad(n\ge2).
}
$$

Their proof uses properties of irrational Beatty sequences and floor functions, detailed on printed pages 143–145. The final page explicitly identifies the binary digits as the odd-indexed differences.

The closed formula is much easier to analyze than the original recursion. It explains why the sequence contains powers of two and makes the digit extraction completely transparent.

### Why the digit identity works

Let

$$
z=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=0.1011010100\ldots{}_2.
$$

Apply the Graham–Pollak closed formula separately at odd indices. For every $n\ge1$,

$$
a_{2n+1}=2^n+\left\lfloor2^n z\right\rfloor,
$$

and also

$$
a_{2n-1}=2^{n-1}+
\left\lfloor2^{n-1}z\right\rfloor.
$$

The second identity also holds at $n=1$, since $a_1=1$ and $0<z<1$.

Subtracting gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_n
&=a_{2n+1}-2a_{2n-1}\\
&=\left\lfloor2^nz\right\rfloor
-2\left\lfloor2^{n-1}z\right\rfloor.
\end{aligned}
$$

For any $z\in[0,1)$, this last expression is exactly its **$n$th binary fractional digit**. To see this, write $z=(0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots)_2$. Multiplying by $2^{n-1}$ moves the first $n-1$ bits to the integer part. The next multiplication by two adds one new bit; subtracting twice the preceding integer part leaves precisely $b_n$.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
d_n=\left\lfloor
2^{n-1}\sqrt2
\right\rfloor\bmod2
\ \in\{0,1\}.
}
$$

Because $\sqrt2=2z$, the $n$th fractional binary digit of $z$ is the $n$th binary digit of $\sqrt2$ **when counting from its leading integer digit**.

This is a complete deduction of the digit result **from Graham and Pollak's proven closed formula**. It does not replace their original proof of that formula, which establishes that the nonlinear recurrence really has the claimed closed form.

### Stoll's generalizations

Erdős and Graham, on printed page 96 of their 1980 monograph, asked for similar identities for numbers such as $\sqrt m$ and other algebraic numbers. Two principal sources answer the request in a much broader form.

In **2005**, Thomas Stoll published *On Families of Nonlinear Recurrences Related to Digits* in the *Journal of Integer Sequences*, volume 8, article 05.3.2. The paper describes two infinite families of recurrences that encode digit expansions, with extensions beyond square roots.

In **2006**, Stoll published *On a problem of Erdős and Graham concerning digits*, *Acta Arithmetica* **125**, 89–100. Its **Theorem 3.1**, printed page 92, constructs recurrences in any integer base $g\ge2$ for a positive real target $w$, with a required discrete parameter triple $(m,l,k)$ and an admissible interval for the offset parameter $\varepsilon$.

The recurrence is allowed to alternate coefficients between odd and even steps:

$$
u_{n+1}=
\begin{cases}
\lfloor a(u_n+\varepsilon)\rfloor,&n\text{ odd},\\
\lfloor b(u_n+l/(g-1))\rfloor,&n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
$$

Here the theorem sets $ab=g$ and imposes further explicit arithmetic inequalities and a divisibility condition on the parameter triple. Under **those stated conditions**,

$$
u_{2n+1}-g\,u_{2n-1}
$$

equals the $n$th base-$g$ digit of $w$.

The **Theorem 3.3** binary family, on printed page 93, specializes to the original Graham–Pollak result when $w=\sqrt2$, $(m,l,k)=(1,0,0)$, and $\varepsilon=1/2$. Stoll remarks that a whole interval of offsets also yields the digits in that specialization.

This generalization is stronger in spirit than asking only about $\sqrt m$, because it treats **arbitrary positive real target values** through carefully designed recurrence parameters. It would, however, be incorrect to remove the parameter constraints and claim that *every choice* of affine coefficients reads the target number's digits.

GCL's [authenticated original-issue #482 audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1752#issuecomment-6169299792) inspected the original Erdős–Graham printed page 96, the **complete 1970 Graham–Pollak three-page proof**, and the original 2005 and 2006 Stoll papers.

The PDF checksums were recorded in the issue-bound audit. The full primary papers support the status **SOLVED** for the open-ended generalization request, without implying that all possible analogous recurrences have been classified.

Independent exact-integer calculations reproduced the first 40 binary digits from the original recurrence and checked them against a separately computed expression for the digits of $\sqrt2$. The same checks confirmed the Graham–Pollak closed formula over the tested range. They are reproducibility fixtures, not a computer proof of an all-index identity.

The formalization status requires care. The **pinned** GCL Formal Conjectures snapshot at commit `85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020` has **no Erdős #482 source file**. A newer upstream snapshot does have a statement: it represents the digit as a zero-indexed binary digit of $\sqrt2/2$, which is compatible with Graham–Pollak's convention once the shift is checked. However, the upstream file still uses theorem-body placeholders and cites an external Lean development by Trevor Morris in `gotrevor/lean-gallery`.

The external repository provides source-level proof statements and reports a standard-axiom trust base. **GCL has not independently compiled that complete development or inspected the transitive proof dependencies and kernel axiom output.** External claimed proof availability is not a protected GCL MathCert.

This article remains an **unaccepted draft**, and #482 is in the cohort's mandatory deterministic critical-review sample.

## What remains

The substantive historical question has been resolved in published mathematics. The remaining work concerns stronger independent verification and trustworthy exposition.

The first bounded task is to formalize the Graham–Pollak closed form from the original recurrence. An important part is the exact floor-function lemma on page 144 of the 1970 paper, not merely the easy digit identity once the closed form has been assumed. The resulting proof must handle $a_1$ and the index shift correctly.

A second task is to **replay the external Lean development** under its exact pinned toolchain and Mathlib revision. The test should check the target declaration and all transitive dependencies, record the kernel build and `#print axioms` output, and compare the digit conventions explicitly. Passing a mathematical theorem with an unused `sorry` in another file is not the same question as whether this theorem transitively depends on any unproved axiom; both facts should be reported precisely.

The third task is a semantic audit of the generalized statements against Stoll 2006 Theorems 3.1, 3.3 and 3.4. It should verify the allowed integer parameters, base $g$, target normalization $w/g^{\lfloor\log_g w\rfloor}$, inequalities for $\varepsilon$, and the precise parity of the recurrence. If any hypothesis is omitted in a claimed formal proof, the mismatch must remain a blocker rather than a source certificate.

Finally, the deterministic critical-sample review and a **real browser MathJax readback** are required for protected cohort publication. Passing finite digit tests and a local documentation build alone does not establish them.
