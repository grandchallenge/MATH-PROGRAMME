## The question in plain language

Choose a **single infinite sequence** of independent random signs $\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_1,\ldots$, each equally likely to be $-1$ or $+1$. For each degree $n$, form the real polynomial

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\varepsilon_kx^k.
$$

Let $R_n$ count its distinct real roots **over the entire real line**. Erdős asks whether, with probability one,

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_n}{\log n}=\frac2\pi.}
$$

There are two indispensable qualifications. The polynomials for different $n$ share **the same prefix of random coefficients**; this is not a sequence of freshly resampled independent degree-$n$ polynomials. Also, $R_n$ counts all real roots, **not only roots in $[-1,1]$**.

These distinctions are mathematical, not administrative. An asymptotic for the expected number of roots at each degree, or a convergence-in-probability theorem, does not establish almost-sure convergence along one coupled sequence of polynomials.

## A small example

Take the polynomial

$$
P_2(x)=1+x-x^2.
$$

Its two real roots are $(1-\sqrt5)/2$ and $(1+\sqrt5)/2$. The first lies in $(-1,1)$ and the second lies outside $[-1,1]$.

For any degree-$n$ polynomial whose constant and leading coefficients are nonzero, define the **reciprocal polynomial**

$$
Q_n(x)=x^nP_n(1/x)
=\varepsilon_n+\varepsilon_{n-1}x+\cdots+\varepsilon_0x^n.
$$

Nonzero real roots $r$ of $P_n$ correspond exactly to roots $1/r$ of $Q_n$. In particular, roots outside the unit interval map to roots inside it, with endpoints handled separately.

At any **fixed** $n$, the reversed coefficient vector has the same probability distribution as the original iid vector. Therefore the *distribution* of the number of outer roots of $P_n$ agrees with the distribution of inner roots of another random polynomial of the same degree.

But as $n$ increases, the vectors $(\varepsilon_n,\ldots,\varepsilon_0)$ do **not** form successive prefixes of one reversed infinite sequence: adding a new sign changes the beginning of every reversed vector. Thus fixed-degree reversal symmetry does not transfer a **coupled almost-sure theorem** from the inner roots of $P_n$ to the outer roots of the *same* $P_n$.

This is the exact pitfall identified in the 2024 literature.

## What is known

### Erdős–Offord and the original question

Erdős poses the question in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.252](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=32), Section V.2. The original page was directly inspected. He recalls joint work with A. C. Offord, *On the number of real roots of a random algebraic equation*, *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society* (3) **6** (1956), 139–160, locating the typical count near $(2/\pi)\log n$.

Erdős asks for an **almost-sure asymptotic for a single nested sequence of partial sums**. His printed binary-expansion notation is potentially ambiguous about $\{0,1\}$ digits versus Rademacher $\{-1,1\}$ signs. The maintained [#521 statement](https://www.erdosproblems.com/521) explicitly uses independent fair signs. These coefficient distributions cannot be silently interchanged.

### Do's 2024 theorem concerns the unit interval

Yen Q. Do, *A strong law of large numbers for real roots of random polynomials*, [arXiv:2403.06353v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06353), March 25, 2024, established

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_n([-1,1])}{\log n}\longrightarrow\frac1\pi
\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

This holds for iid coefficients of mean zero, variance one and finite $(2+\delta)$ moment, including fair signs. It is a result about **roots inside $[-1,1]$**. The author revised the initial version and expressly warned that polynomial reversal does not transfer the theorem to all real roots for the same coupled sequence.

The proof uses maximal inequalities, lacunary subsequences and root-count concentration. The primary version-2 statement and its limitation were read, but its full proof was not independently reconstructed. This interval theorem neither establishes nor refutes the entire-real-line strong law by itself.
