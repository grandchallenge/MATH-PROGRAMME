## The question in plain language

A set of positive integers is **primitive** if no two **different** elements divide one another. Erdős, Sárközy and Szemerédi asked for a test on an arbitrary increasing sequence $b_1<b_2<\cdots$ that characterizes when there exists an infinite primitive sequence $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ with

$$
a_n\le Cb_n\qquad\text{for all }n,
$$

where the constant $C$ may depend on the chosen sequences but not on $n$. This is an **infinite sequence and uniform domination** problem, not simply finding a large primitive subset of a fixed interval.

Two further clauses appear in [Erdős Problem #892](https://www.erdosproblems.com/892): whether excluding certain gcd configurations from the sequence $b_n$ suffices, and how to characterize increasing exponents $n_i$ for which an infinite primitive set $A$ can have $|A\cap[1,2^{n_i}]|\ge c2^{n_i}$ for every $i$, with a single constant $c>0$. All three questions remain open in their generality.

## A small example

For any positive integer $N$, the upper-half interval

$$
A_N=\{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1,\ldots,N\}
$$

is primitive: if $x<y$ are both in the interval, then $2x>N\ge y$, so $x$ cannot divide $y$. It contains exactly $\lceil N/2\rceil$ elements. This is in fact the **maximum** size of a primitive subset of $[1,N]$: partition the interval into chains

$$
\{m,2m,4m,\ldots\}\cap[1,N]
$$

for odd $m\le N$. Numbers in each chain divide the following numbers, so a primitive set selects at most one per chain. There are exactly $\lceil N/2\rceil$ odd values of $m$. The finite verifier independently exhausts all subsets of $[1,N]$ for $N\le15$ and checks this optimum, with further exact chain certificates through $N=24$.

The upper-half interval does **not** solve the infinite problem: primitive sets chosen optimally at different endpoints need not combine into one infinite primitive set.

There is also a precise issue with the gcd special case. The finite sequence $B=\{2,4,9\}$ has no gcd relation of the historically **noncomparable** type, but it is not primitive because $2\mid4$. If every solution to $\gcd(b_i,b_j)=b_k$ is forbidden literally whenever the indices are not all identical, even comparable pairs are excluded and the hypothesis itself already makes $B$ primitive. The historical question is the nontrivial, weaker one.

## What is known

The original Erdős–Sárközy–Szemerédi paper [*On divisibility properties of sequences of integers*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/892), presented in Debrecen in 1968 and published in *Colloquia Mathematica Societatis János Bolyai* **2** (1970), pp.35–49, gives the general comparison question on page 37 and the gcd special case on page 42. The authors specify on page 41 that a relation of the form $\gcd(a_i,a_j)=a_r$ is discussed **only when neither input divides the other**. That convention must accompany the modern shortened wording.

Two classical necessary conditions for the comparison sequence are

$$
\sum_n\frac1{b_n\log b_n}<\infty
$$

and

$$
\sum_{\substack{b_n<x}}\frac1{b_n}
=o\!\left(\frac{\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\right).
$$

The first follows from Erdős's 1935 theorem on primitive sums; the second follows from Erdős, Sárközy and Szemerédi, [*On a theorem of Behrend*](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1967-09.pdf), *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society* **7** (1967), Theorem 1. Both are **necessary**, and neither alone is known to characterize arbitrary acceptable sequences.

Greg Martin and Carl Pomerance, [*Primitive sets with large counting functions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.1014), *Publicationes Mathematicae Debrecen* **79** (2011), pp.521–530, prove a partial converse for suitably regular target counting functions with a convergent weighted integral. It supplies significant positive instances of the first question, but not a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for **all** increasing sequences $b_n$. A July 2026 working report additionally derives a candidate equivalence for a special iterated-logarithm family using this theorem; that specialization is not independently source-verified here.

## GCL's current state

The finite chain argument above is exact: every positive integer has a unique **odd part**, and numbers with a fixed odd part form a total divisibility chain. This shows that a primitive subset of $[1,N]$ has cardinality at most the number of odd integers in the interval. The upper-half construction attains the bound. The finite program governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_892_893.py verifies this for $1\le N\le24$ using the chain decomposition and independently exhausts all subsets for $N\le15$.

The program also compares two non-equivalent finite gcd predicates: excluding all distinct-index gcd equalities versus excluding gcd equalities only for **incomparable** inputs. For $\{2,4,9\}$, the latter predicate holds but primitivity fails. This is a direct finite semantic witness explaining why the original 1970 convention is essential.

For the infinite comparison question, suppose a primitive increasing sequence $a_n$ satisfies $a_n\le Cb_n$. The 1935 primitive-sum theorem implies $\sum_n1/(a_n\log a_n)<\infty$ (after discarding initial exceptional terms). The domination then implies the first necessary condition for $b_n$. Proving its **sufficiency**, without regularity assumptions, would be a substantially different argument and is not claimed.

## What remains

A bounded successor is a **gcd-predicate source-fidelity lemma**. Define the historically qualified gcd-free condition only on incomparable pairs. Prove that the literal all-pairs interpretation forces primitivity, and produce the finite counterexample $\{2,4,9\}$ to the equivalence of the two interpretations. Formalize the odd-part chain proof of the exact finite extremum $\lceil N/2\rceil$.

Acceptance is a complete elementary proof or a pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem with actual axiom output, exact agreement of the exhaustive finite counts through $N=15$, and a page-level citation to the 1970 convention. A pair satisfying the comparable-input exclusion incorrectly, or a primitive set larger than $\lceil N/2\rceil$, falsifies the finite result.

For the genuine infinite problem, independently check Martin–Pomerance's hypotheses before transferring its approximate converse to any proposed $b_n$ family. Stop before claiming an arbitrary-sequence characterization or the positive-density-at-prescribed-powers criterion; neither follows from the finite antichain result.
