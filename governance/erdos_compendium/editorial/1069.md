## The question in plain language

Choose $n$ distinct points in the plane. A line is called *$k$‑rich* if it passes through at least $k$ of the points. How many different rich lines can there be?

[Erdős Problem #1069](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1069) asks for a bound on this number when $2\le k\le\sqrt n$. The celebrated **Szemerédi–Trotter theorem** gives the answer:

$$
L_k(P)\le C\frac{n^2}{k^3},
\qquad 2\le k\le\sqrt n,
$$

where $C$ is one universal constant. The number of lines decreases rapidly as the richness threshold rises. The sharp possible value of $C$ remains a separate problem.

## A small example

Place nine points in a $3\times3$ square lattice. Three horizontal lines, three vertical lines, and the two long diagonals each contain three points. There are precisely **eight** $3$‑rich lines.

For a $4\times4$ grid there are ten $4$‑rich lines, and for a $5\times5$ grid there are twelve $5$‑rich lines. These values can be confirmed by putting every line through a pair of integer points into its unique primitive equation $ax+by=c$, then counting its grid points.

They also illustrate a general lower construction: for a $q\times q$ grid, $n=q^2$ and $k=q$, there are at least $2q+2$ rich lines. The general theorem says there are at most a constant times $q$ such lines, because $n^2/k^3=q$.

## What is known

**The claimed rich-line bound is a published theorem.** The original primary source is Endre Szemerédi and William T. Trotter, *Extremal problems in discrete geometry*, *Combinatorica* **3** (1983), 381–392. On printed page 382, **Theorem 2** gives the precise $C n^2/k^3$ estimate.

The authors derive it from their incidence theorem, which bounds the total number of incidences between points and lines. In general the rich-line estimate also has an $n/k$ term; the pure $n^2/k^3$ expression is appropriate in the stated $k\le\sqrt n$ regime.

The later Erdős retrospective attributes the conjecture to Erdős, Croft and Purdy. Its attribution and chronology still need comparison with the original pages; the 1983 paper calls the corollary an Erdős–Purdy conjecture.

## What remains

The editorial proof review should reconstruct the short deduction from the incidence theorem to the rich-line bound, keeping the $k$ range explicit. A separate attribution audit must compare the Erdős 1987 survey with the 1983 primary paper. Better constants and the extreme lattice constructions are legitimate continuing mathematical questions, but they must not obscure that the order-of-growth theorem itself is proved.
