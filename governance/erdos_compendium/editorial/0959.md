## The question in plain language

Let $A\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a finite set of $n$ **distinct** points. For each positive distance $d$ realized by a pair of points, define its multiplicity by counting **unordered** pairs,

$$
m_A(d)=\#\bigl\{\{x,y\}\subset A:\ x\ne y,\ \|x-y\|=d\bigr\}.
$$

Sort the multiplicities, not the distances, into nonincreasing order,

$$
m_1(A)\ge m_2(A)\ge\cdots\ge m_t(A)>0,
$$

where $t$ is the number of distinct realized distances. The quantity of interest is

$$
G(n)=\max_{\substack{A\subset\mathbb R^2,\ |A|=n\\t(A)\ge2}}
\bigl(m_1(A)-m_2(A)\bigr).
$$

Erdős asked for the growth of $G(n)$. It remains **OPEN**. The important condition $t(A)\ge2$ ensures the second multiplicity exists; for example three vertices of an equilateral triangle realize only one positive distance and are not an admissible input to this difference. See [Erdős Problem #959](https://www.erdosproblems.com/959).

## A small exact example

Take the four vertices of the unit square

$$
A=\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(0,1)\}.
$$

The four sides have squared distance $1$, while the two diagonals have squared distance $2$. There are therefore exactly two distance values, with unordered multiplicities $4$ and $2$, so

$$
m_1(A)-m_2(A)=4-2=2,\qquad G(4)\ge2.
$$

This is an elementary exact calculation, not a sharp evaluation of $G(4)$.

**Counting-convention warning.** Ordered pairs double every positive-distance multiplicity and hence double the difference. The conjecture's *order of growth* is unchanged by this constant factor, but the precise finite integer $G(n)$ depends on the convention. We use unordered pairs, consistent with the convention in Clemen–Dumitrescu–Liu. The source page does not explicitly settle the convention, so it must be stated in the manuscript and computational certificates.

## Established lower bound: a superlinear multiplicity gap

Clemen, Dumitrescu and Liu proved in [CDL25] that for all sufficiently large $n$ one can arrange $n$ planar points so that

$$
m_1(A)-m_2(A)\gg n\log n.
$$

Consequently

$$
G(n)=\Omega(n\log n).
$$

Their theorem is stronger. For an integer $r$ in the range $1\le r\le\log n$, there exist $n$-point configurations with

$$
m_r(A)-m_{r+1}(A)\gg\frac{n\log n}{r}.
$$

Here $r$ indexes the **rank among distance multiplicities**, not a rank among shortest or longest geometric distances. The construction can also prescribe which distances occupy the highest multiplicity ranks, according to the authors' published abstract. These statements were published in *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* **177** (2025), pp.231–245, [DOI 10.1007/s10474-025-01562-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10474-025-01562-y).

The catalogue reports the authors' conjecture that a larger gap, of size at least $n^{1+c/\log\log n}$ for some constant $c>0$, should be achievable. That is a **conjectured improvement**, not an established lower bound. In particular the $\Omega(n\log n)$ construction does not imply any fixed-power bound $\Omega(n^{1+\delta})$ with $\delta>0$.

## A rigorous general upper bound

Let $u(n)$ be the largest possible number of unordered unit-distance pairs among $n$ planar points. Scaling every point of a configuration by $1/d$ identifies pairs at an arbitrary fixed distance $d>0$ with unit-distance pairs. Therefore, for every $A$ and every distance $d$,

$$
m_A(d)\le u(n).
$$

The classical Spencer–Szemerédi–Trotter unit-distance bound gives $u(n)=O(n^{4/3})$. Since $m_2(A)\ge1$ for admissible configurations,

$$
0\le m_1(A)-m_2(A)\le m_1(A)\le u(n)=O(n^{4/3}).
$$

Thus the unconditional comparison is

$$
\boxed{\quad n\log n\ \ll\ G(n)\ \ll\ n^{4/3}.\quad}
$$

This **does not determine the correct exponent or asymptotic size**. Even a substantially sharper theorem about the largest multiplicity $m_1$ alone need not determine the gap $m_1-m_2$, because the second-largest multiplicity could be nearly as large as the first. A proof of a sharp gap bound must control both simultaneously.

## An illuminating comparison of configurations

An equally spaced sequence of $n$ points along a line has $n-1$ distinct distances with unordered multiplicities

$$
n-1,n-2,\ldots,1.
$$

Its leading gap is only $1$, despite its largest multiplicity being linear in $n$. The regular $n$-gon also has many repeated distances, but the most frequent distances typically tie in multiplicity, producing a zero leading gap when at least two such distance classes occur. Conversely [CDL25] constructs configurations where the most popular distance dramatically exceeds the runner-up by a superlinear amount. The open question asks how uneven this frequency distribution can become.

This problem is related to [Erdős #958](https://www.erdosproblems.com/958), which asks whether an entire distance-multiplicity profile characterizes a point arrangement. Problem #958 is **disproved**; #959 is **open**. The existence of configurations with prescribed multiplicity profiles does not by itself resolve the extremal gap problem.

## Why the remaining gap is difficult

Distances interact geometrically: every point participates in many pairs and the edges of different equal-distance graphs share vertices. An upper bound on one distance's frequency does not capture competition from the second most popular distance, and naive incidence averaging need not show that the runner-up has any particular size. Constructing a large gap requires many copies of one distance while suppressing all other distance frequencies.

A computational search over integer-grid configurations is valuable for exploring these conflicts but samples only a tiny, special class of all planar point sets. It cannot certify asymptotic maximality over arbitrary real-coordinate configurations. To avoid spurious ties from floating-point arithmetic, squared distances should be computed exactly for integer or rational coordinates.
