## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $m$, form the set of values $S_m=\{k(m-k):1\leq k\leq m/2\}$, with $k$ an integer. Erdős Problem 443 asks how large an intersection $S_m\cap S_n$ can be for two parameters $m,n$. Can the intersection have arbitrarily many members? And can its size be bounded by a subpolynomial function of the parameters?

The case $m=n$ must be treated separately: then the two sets are equal and contain approximately $m/2$ distinct values, so a bound of the form $(mn)^{o(1)}$ is impossible along $m=n\to\infty$. The meaningful upper-bound question concerns **distinct** parameters, usually normalized as $m>n$. This is a material quantifier boundary, not a cosmetic change. Within each set, the expression $k(m-k)$ increases for $1\leq k\leq m/2$, so values are distinct.

The quadratic expression is also a difference of squares:

$$
4k(m-k)=m^2-(m-2k)^2.
$$

Consequently an equality $k(m-k)=\ell(n-\ell)$ can be rewritten as the difference-of-squares equation $(m-2k)^2-(n-2\ell)^2=m^2-n^2$. This connects the intersection problem to divisor factorizations of a fixed integer.

## A small example

For $m=5$, the integer indices $k=1,2$ give $S_5=\{4,6\}$. For $n=7$, the indices $\ell=1,2,3$ give $S_7=\{6,10,12\}$. Therefore $S_5\cap S_7=\{6\}$ and the intersection has exactly one member. The shared value is represented by $2(5-2)=1(7-1)=6$.

By contrast $S_5\cap S_5=S_5$ has two members. Increasing $m=n$ makes that self-intersection grow linearly, illustrating why a theorem stated for different parameters cannot be casually applied to the diagonal. A bounded exact fixture can enumerate the sets by integer multiplication, deduplicate them, and compare their intersection with the factorization identity above.

The equation of two quadratic expressions is not merely a numerical coincidence. Write $u=m-2k$ and $v=n-2\ell$. Then $(u-v)(u+v)=m^2-n^2$, where the parity and range of $u,v$ are constrained by the original indices. Each common value produces a permitted factor pair of $m^2-n^2$. This reduction is the natural bridge to divisor-function estimates.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham posed the questions in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 88. The maintained [Erdős Problems #443](https://www.erdosproblems.com/443) page records independent resolutions by Hegyvári (2025) and Cambie (an unpublished argument). They show that for distinct $m>n$ the intersection has size at most $m^{O(1/\log\log m)}$, a subpolynomial bound.

They also establish that for every fixed positive integer $s$ there exist infinitely many parameter pairs $(m,n)$ with precisely $s$ common values. Thus intersections can be arbitrarily large, even though their maximal size grows slower than every fixed positive power of the parameters in the distinct-parameter regime.

These results answer different halves of the historical question. The existence of arbitrarily large finite intersections does not contradict a subpolynomial uniform upper bound. Nor is the upper theorem an assertion about identical parameters, where the intersection is trivially large. The maintained source's qualification $m>n$ is indispensable.

## What remains

A bounded computational fixture should enumerate $S_m$ for $1\leq m\leq100$, compare every pair of distinct parameters, and record their exact common values and cardinalities. A second implementation should factor $m^2-n^2$ and enumerate compatible $u,v$ satisfying the difference-of-squares equation, checking parity and original index restrictions. The calculations must reproduce $S_5\cap S_7=\{6\}$ and exclude the diagonal from any claimed subpolynomial uniform upper bound.

The source audit should inspect Hegyvári's 2025 proof and the public mathematical description of Cambie's independent argument, identifying the $O(1/\log\log m)$ exponent and the quantified construction with exactly $s$ solutions. Success requires complete agreement of the two finite methods and faithful distinction between a published and unpublished proof. Falsification includes a missing factor-pair parity restriction, counting multiple index representations as distinct intersection values, or asserting a subpolynomial bound for $m=n$.
