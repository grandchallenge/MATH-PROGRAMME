## The question in plain language

Suppose every small part of a graph can be made easy to colour by removing relatively few edges. Does that guarantee the **whole graph** needs only a few colours?

[Erdős Problem #1092](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1092) studies this local-to-global question. Fix $r\ge2$. If every $m$-vertex subgraph can be decomposed into an $r$-colourable graph and a graph with at most $f_r(m)$ edges, when can we conclude the original graph is $(r+1)$-colourable?

The conjecture was that a **linear** deletion allowance of order $f_r(m)\gg_r m$ might suffice. This turned out to be false.

## A small example

Consider $K_4$. Split its vertices into two classes of two. Four edges run between the classes, forming the bipartite graph $K_{2,2}$. The two remaining edges lie within classes.

Deleting those two edges makes the entire graph bipartite. Yet $K_4$ itself requires four colours. This elementary example shows the decomposition concept, but does **not** refute the linear conjecture: that needs arbitrarily large graphs with stringent conditions on **every** subgraph.

## What is known

The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1092) attributes a strong disproof to Rödl (1982). For any prescribed small $\varepsilon>0$ and arbitrarily large target chromatic number, the reported construction gives graphs that remain highly chromatic even though every subgraph on $m$ vertices can be made bipartite by deleting at most $\varepsilon m$ edges.

Because $\varepsilon$ can be arbitrarily small, no fixed positive linear allowance forces bounded global chromatic number in the manner conjectured.

## What remains

Review Rödl's original theorem, the phrase “every subgraph,” and the exact dependence on $\varepsilon$ and target chromatic number. A finite local decomposition cannot replace a proof for arbitrarily small deletion coefficients.
