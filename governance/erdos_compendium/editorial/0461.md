## The question in plain language

Take $t$ consecutive positive integers, $n+1,\ldots,n+t$. For each integer, erase every prime factor at least $t$, keeping all copies of primes smaller than $t$. Call the product left behind its **$t$‑smooth component**, $s_t(m)$. For example, with $t=5$, $s_5(70)=2$ because $70=2\cdot5\cdot7$ and the primes $5$ and $7$ are not *less than* $5$.

Let

$$
f(n,t)=\left|\{s_t(n+1),\ldots,s_t(n+t)\}\right|.
$$

Erdős and Graham asked whether an absolute constant $c>0$ exists such that $f(n,t)\ge ct$ **for every** $n\ge0$ and integer $t\ge2$. This uniformity is crucial: an example for one interval or an average over intervals does not settle the question.

## A small example

For $t=5$ and $n=0$, the smooth components of $1,2,3,4,5$ are $1,2,3,4,1$. Thus $f(0,5)=4$, not $5$: the prime $5$ itself has smooth component $1$ because the cutoff is strict.

There are arbitrarily large intervals with essentially all distinct components. Put $L=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,t)$ and $n=L^2$. For each $1\le i\le t$ and prime $p<t$, $v_p(n)>v_p(i)$ whenever $p$ divides $i$, so $v_p(n+i)=v_p(i)$. Consequently

$$
s_t(L^2+i)=s_t(i),\qquad
f(L^2,t)=
\begin{cases}
t,&t\text{ composite},\\
t-1,&t\text{ prime}.
\end{cases}
$$

The finite regression reproduces this identity for $2\le t<40$. Such large values do **not** establish the minimum over all $n$.

A reported counterexample to a stronger matching approach uses $n=1\,407\,302$, $t=38$. Exact computation gives $f(n,38)=27$. Six proposed left-hand divisors $13,15,16,17,18,19$ have only five smooth-component neighbours, from offsets $9,10,13,26,28$. This refutes that particular matching argument, not Erdős's conjecture.

## What is known

The primary source, Erdős–Graham (1980), printed pages 91–92, defines $n+i=a_i b_i$ with all prime factors of $a_i$ strictly below $t$ and all prime factors of $b_i$ at least $t$. It asks whether $\min_t f(n,t)/t>\varepsilon$ for a fixed positive $\varepsilon$, and reports that they can show $f(n,t)\gg t/\log t$. The book does not provide a proof of that bound at this location. The maintained catalogue expresses the question uniformly in both $n$ and $t$ and lists it as OPEN.

Two independently checkable observations help separate easy structure from the hard question. First, if $s_t(n+i)=s_t(n+j)=s\ge t$ for $i\ne j$, then $s$ divides $|i-j|<t$, a contradiction. Hence every component at least $t$ occurs **only once** in a length‑$t$ interval. This alone cannot settle the conjecture: the maximal examples above have few or no components at least $t$.

Second, one cannot demand an arbitrarily large constant in $f(n,t)\ge ct$. Here is an exact averaging obstruction, also noted in a June 2026 public discussion. Fix $1\le y<t$, let $P_t=\prod_{p<t}p$, $L_y=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,y)$, and $Q=P_tL_y$. For each $u\le y$,

$$
\frac1Q\#\{1\le m\le Q:s_t(m)=u\}
=\frac1u\prod_{p<t}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$

Indeed, $s_t(m)=u$ precisely when $m=ur$ and $\gcd(r,P_t)=1$; $Q/u$ is a multiple of $P_t$. Since at most $y$ distinct components are at most $y$,

$$
f(n,t)\le y+\sum_{i=1}^t {\bf1}_{s_t(n+i)>y}.
$$

Average over $n$ modulo $Q$. Translation by each $i$ permutes the residue classes, so **some** $n$ satisfies

$$
f(n,t)\le y+t\left[1-H_y
\prod_{p<t}\left(1-\frac1p\right)\right],
\qquad H_y=\sum_{u=1}^y\frac1u.
$$

Take $y=\lfloor t/\log t\rfloor$. Mertens's product theorem gives $H_y\prod_{p<t}(1-1/p)\to e^{-\gamma}$, while $y/t\to0$. Therefore

$$
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ \inf_{n\ge0}\frac{f(n,t)}t
\le 1-e^{-\gamma}\approx0.4385.
$$

In particular, a uniform bound with $c>1-e^{-\gamma}$ is impossible. **This is not a disproof** of the original assertion that *some* positive $c$ exists. The asymptotic argument uses an established analytic number theory theorem, not merely finite testing.

## What remains

A bounded successor is to reconstruct the Erdős–Graham $t/\log t$ lower bound from a citable complete proof, with the strict prime cutoff and uniform quantifiers explicit. The next research obstacle is to improve that logarithmically weakened estimate to a positive constant proportion **uniformly over interval starting points**. Any attempted collision-count or matching proof must survive the $(1407302,38)$ fixture and the exact density obstruction above.
