**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #489 is **OPEN**. Its squarefree-number special case was established by Erdős in 1951. A finite-sieve periodicity calculation is proved below, but it does not settle the general sparse infinite-sieve problem.

## The question in plain language

Imagine crossing out every positive integer divisible by a member of a list $A$. The remaining increasing numbers are

$$
B=\{b_1<b_2<b_3<\cdots\}.
$$

Write $g_i=b_{i+1}-b_i$ for the gap after the $i$‑th survivor. Erdős asks whether the *average squared gap per integer*, rather than per surviving member, settles to a finite constant:

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x
\sum_{b_i<x}(b_{i+1}-b_i)^2.
$$

The list of forbidden divisors must be exceptionally sparse. In the original 1961 paper it is an increasing infinite sequence $A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots\}$ satisfying

$$
\frac{a_k}{k^2}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

Equivalently, for the corresponding infinite set, $|A\cap[1,x]|=o(\sqrt{x})$. In plain language, the number of excluded *moduli* up to $x$ must grow more slowly than the square root of $x$. This condition does not by itself bound the gaps between the surviving integers.

A **large gap is disproportionately important**: a gap of length $10$ contributes $100$, whereas ten gaps of length $1$ contribute only $10$. Knowing that each fixed gap length has a limiting frequency therefore does not automatically establish convergence of this quadratic average.

There are two quantifier pitfalls. The sum includes every $b_i<x$ and uses the *next* surviving integer $b_{i+1}$, even if $b_{i+1}\ge x$. Also, the increasing enumeration requires $B$ to be infinite. If $1\in A$, then $B$ is empty; that is an invalid input to this enumeration, not a disproof of an asymptotic assertion.

## A small example

For a finite set $A$ of integers at least $2$, the remaining numbers are periodic modulo

$$
L=\mathrm{lcm}\{a:a\in A\}.
$$

Take $A=\{2,3\}$. The surviving residue classes modulo $6$ are $1$ and $5$, so

$$
B=1,5,7,11,13,17,\ldots
$$

and its gaps alternate between $4$ and $2$. Each period of length $6$ contributes $4^2+2^2=20$ to the gap-square sum. Consequently,

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x\sum_{b_i<x}g_i^2
=\frac{20}{6}=\frac{10}{3}.
$$

More generally, list the surviving residues in one period as $1\le r_1<\cdots<r_t\le L$. Define the cyclic gaps by $g_i=r_{i+1}-r_i$ for $i<t$, and $g_t=L+r_1-r_t$. Then the exact finite-sieve formula is

$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x
\sum_{b_i<x}(b_{i+1}-b_i)^2
=\frac1L\sum_{i=1}^{t}g_i^2.}
$$

**Proof:** translating each survivor by $L$ preserves the following gap, so each complete block of $L$ consecutive possible starting positions contributes exactly the same squared-gap sum. The contribution from an incomplete block is bounded by the fixed number $\sum g_i^2$. Dividing by $x$ makes that boundary term vanish.

For the empty finite list $A$, every integer survives and the limit is $1$. For $A=\{2\}$, only odd integers survive, all gaps are $2$, and the limit is $2$. This exact formula verifies an **elementary finite-modulus subcase**, not an arbitrary infinite set.

## What is known

### The original question

Erdős posed the general question in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed pp. 236–237, Problem I.28, formula (I.28.3). The original assumption is $a_k/k^2\to\infty$. Erdős states explicitly that a weaker growth assumption of order $k^2$ does **not** suffice in general. This is not a counterexample to the actual sparse version, which the maintained [#489 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/489) still lists as **OPEN**.

### The squarefree case is proved

When $A=\{p^2:p\text{ prime}\}$, the surviving numbers are exactly the squarefree integers. Erdős proved the finite squared-gap mean in *Some problems and results in elementary number theory* (1951), *Publicationes Mathematicae Debrecen* **2**, 103–109.

The key steps appear on printed pp. 107–109. For each fixed gap length $t$, the frequency per unit interval of squarefree integers with next gap $t$ has a limit $\beta_t$ (Lemma 1, based on Mirsky). A separate tail bound (Lemma 2) controls the frequency of exceptionally large gaps. With both inputs, Erdős obtains

$$
\sum_{s_i<x}(s_{i+1}-s_i)^2=C_2x+o(x)
$$

for a **finite** constant $C_2$. The printed pages containing this proof and its bounds (23)–(29) were directly inspected. Replaying the cited Mirsky theorem and all subordinate estimates as a formal proof would be a separate task.

### Why fixed-gap frequencies alone are not enough

Suppose that, for every fixed integer $t$, the per-unit-interval frequency of following gaps equal to $t$ tends to $\beta_t$. The tempting candidate constant is

$$
\sum_{t\ge1}t^2\beta_t.
$$

Interchanging the infinite weighted sum with the limit requires control of the large‑$t$ tail. Rare but enormous gaps can contribute substantial squared weight even while every fixed-gap frequency stabilizes. Erdős's 1951 proof supplies this **uniform tail control** for the squarefree case; the arbitrary sparse family remains open.

## What remains

The bounded task is to enumerate survivors for selected finite sets of small moduli, calculate cyclic residue gaps, and compare the exact rational limit against independently enumerated truncated squared-gap sums. Tests must include the final wraparound gap and the strict condition $b_i<x$, even when the next survivor lies beyond $x$.

The remaining research challenge is to prove a squared-gap tail-control estimate for any infinite $A$ with $|A\cap[1,x]|=o(\sqrt{x})$, or construct a genuine counterexample with that sparsity and infinitely many survivors. Finite sampling does not settle this limit.
