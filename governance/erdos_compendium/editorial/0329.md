## The question in plain language

Take a set $A$ of positive integers with the Sidon property: no two different unordered pairs of elements have the same sum. Let $A(N)$ count the elements of $A$ from $1$ through $N$. Erdős Problem 329 asks for the largest possible eventual upper size of $A(N)$ when measured on the square-root scale, namely $\limsup_{N\to\infty} A(N)/\sqrt{N}$. The normalization matters. A Sidon set cannot have ordinary positive density, but it can repeatedly come close to a constant multiple of $\sqrt N$.

The obvious candidate ceiling is $1$. Erdős and Turán proved the corresponding finite estimate, while the exact question is whether some infinite Sidon set reaches that ceiling along an unbounded sequence of cutoffs. The problem is therefore an extremal question about all infinite sets, not a request to find one especially good finite set.

## A small example

The finite set $A=\{1,2,4,8,13\}$ is a useful check on the vocabulary. Enumerating unordered pairs with repetition gives

```text
(1,1),(1,2),(1,4),(1,8),(1,13),(2,2),(2,4),(2,8),(2,13),
(4,4),(4,8),(4,13),(8,8),(8,13),(13,13)
```

and their sums are

```text
2,3,5,9,14,4,6,10,15,8,12,17,16,21,26.
```

An exact-arithmetic enumeration returned `sum_count = 15` and `unique_sum_count = 15`; the ten positive differences between distinct elements were also all distinct. Thus this is a verified finite Sidon example. At $N=13$ its partial numerator is $5$, so its displayed finite ratio is $5/\sqrt{13}$. This calculation does not show that the set extends to a perfect difference set, nor does it say anything by itself about an infinite limsup. In particular, finite Sidon-ness and the asymptotic extremal problem must not be conflated.

## What is known

The primary source read for this audit is P. Erdős and P. Turán, “On a Problem of Sidon in Additive Number Theory, and on some Related Problems,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 16 (1941), pages 212–215. Its introduction states the square-root scale and the elementary difference-counting ceiling; Section II proves, for the largest size of a Sidon sequence below $n$, an estimate of the form $\sqrt n+O(n^{1/4})$. This gives the normalized upper bound $1$ in the corresponding limsup formulation. The paper also records a construction with positive square-root-scale density and explains that existence of an exact limiting constant was not proved there.

The maintained Erdős Problems page reports the later milestones: Erdős obtained $1/2$, and Krückeberg reported $1/\sqrt 2$. The page says that Erdős and Krückeberg conjectured that $1$ is possible. The Krückeberg bibliographic record is identified as F. Krückeberg, “B2-Folgen und verwandte Zahlenfolgen,” *J. Reine Angew. Math.* 1961, no. 206, 53–60. The catalogue and page agree that the main question remains open. The full Krückeberg paper was not independently read in this bounded audit, so its exact construction and hypotheses remain a reported external result here, not a new verification.

A related extension route has changed status. Alexeev and Mixon’s 2025 primary preprint reports that the finite Sidon set $\{1,2,4,8,13\}$ does not extend to any finite perfect difference set, and discusses Hall’s earlier counterexample. That result blocks the implication “every finite Sidon set extends” as a general strategy for proving the density $1$ conjecture. It does not settle Problem 329 itself: the density question concerns infinite integer sets and their limsup, whereas the extension statement concerns finite modular difference sets.
