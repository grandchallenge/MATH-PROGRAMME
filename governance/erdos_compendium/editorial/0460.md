## The question in plain language

Fix an integer $n\geq3$. Starting with $a_1=1$, greedily choose increasing integers so that the differences $n-a_i$ associated with distinct choices are pairwise coprime. Erdős's 1977 version also requires each $a_i$ to be coprime to $n$: it takes $a_0=0$ and checks $\gcd(n-a_k,n-a_i)=1$ for $0\leq i<k$. The 1980 Erdős–Graham version takes $a_0=n$ and checks only $1\leq i<k$. These rules are not identical.

The substantive modern question asks whether the **truncated reciprocal sum**

$$
S_{<n}(n)=\sum_{\substack{i\geq1\\0<a_i<n}}\frac1{a_i}
$$

tends to infinity as $n\to\infty$. Both original sources displayed the sum without the cutoff $a_i<n$. That literal version is automatically infinite for each fixed $n\geq3$, so the cutoff is a necessary clarification, not a verbatim transcription.

## A small example

For $n=6$, the 1977 rule selects $1,5$ below $6$, giving $S_{<6}^{1977}(6)=6/5$. The 1980 rule selects $1,2,3,5$ below $6$, giving

$$
S_{<6}^{1980}(6)=1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{61}{30}.
$$

These different values illustrate the source ambiguity. The local regression checks enumerate both rules for $n=6,12,30$ and verify the forced-rough inclusion for every $3\leq n\leq250$. Finite examples do not establish any limit.

## What is known

For fixed $n\geq3$ and any prime $p>n$, the value $a=n+p$ occurs in either unrestricted greedy list. Every earlier difference $n-a_i$ is nonzero and has absolute value less than $p$, so the new value is admissible and cannot be skipped. The full sum therefore dominates $\sum_{p>n}1/(n+p)$, which diverges by the divergence of prime reciprocals. This does not answer the truncated question.

Define $P^-(m)$ as the least prime factor of $m>1$, with $P^-(1)=\infty$, and put

$$
R_n=\{1\leq a<n:P^-(n-a)>a\},\qquad
f(n)=\sum_{a\in R_n}\frac1a.
$$

Every $a\in R_n$ must be selected by **both** greedy rules. If an earlier entry is $b<a$, any common prime factor of $n-a$ and $n-b$ divides $a-b<a$, contrary to $P^-(n-a)>a$. For the 1977 rule the same argument applies to $b=0$. Thus $S_{<n}(n)\geq f(n)$ pointwise.

A source-critical correction is required. An external January 2026 note claims that the average of $f(n)$ grows at least as fast as $\log\log N$, but its printed proof restricts $a$ to $[N^{1/3},N^{1/2}]$ and incorrectly treats the sum of $1/(a\log a)$ over this range as growing like $\log\log N$. Direct integration gives

$$
\int_{N^{1/3}}^{N^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t\log t}
=\log\frac{1/2}{1/3}=\log\frac32.
$$

That range yields a **constant**, not the asserted divergent factor. The displayed proof step is invalid; this does **not** itself disprove the theorem.

A weaker divergent mean bound follows rigorously from inclusion-exclusion and Mertens's product estimate. Set $A=\lfloor\sqrt{\log N}\rfloor$ and let $\Phi(x,a)$ count positive integers $m\leq x$ having no prime divisor at most $a$. Exact inclusion-exclusion gives

$$
\Phi(N-a,a)=(N-a)\prod_{p\leq a}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
+O(2^{\pi(a)}).
$$

For $3\leq a\leq A$, the product is $\gg1/\log a$ and $2^{\pi(a)}\leq2^A=N^{o(1)}$, so $\Phi(N-a,a)\gg N/\log a$ uniformly. Changing the order of summation yields

$$
\frac1N\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)
\gg\sum_{3\leq a\leq A}\frac1{a\log a}
\gg\log\log\log N.
$$

Hence $S_{<n}(n)$ is unbounded along **some** sequence of inputs. This does not prove $S_{<n}(n)\to\infty$ along **all** sufficiently large inputs. The difference between a divergent average and pointwise divergence is the central remaining difficulty.
