## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 349 asks for all positive real pairs $t$ and $\alpha$ for which the integer sequence

$$
s_n=\lfloor t\alpha^n\rfloor
$$

is complete: every sufficiently large positive integer can be written as a sum of distinct terms of the sequence. “Distinct” means that each index is used at most once and only finitely many indices are selected. The phrase “sufficiently large” permits finitely many exceptions; it does not mean that every positive integer must occur.

The quantifiers matter. This is one question about every $t>0$ and $\alpha>0$, not just about the square $0<t<1$, $1<\alpha<2$. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/349) records the full question, tags it as number theory and complete sequences, and currently displays an open status. The original GCL assignment is [issue #1619](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1619).

There is also an indexing convention to preserve. Graham’s paper writes the sequence from $n=1$, while the pinned formal file uses `Set.range (fun n : ℕ => ⌊t * α ^ n⌋)`, which includes the $n=0$ term. A finite initial term may be harmless in particular arguments, but equivalence is not assumed here without a proof.

## A small example

Take $t=1$ and $\alpha=3/2$, and calculate with exact integer division rather than decimal approximations. For $n=1$ through $8$,

$$
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{3^n}{2^n}\right\rfloor\right)_{n=1}^8=(1,2,3,5,7,11,17,25).
$$

The exact sum of these eight terms is $71$. A direct subset-sum enumeration gives no missing integer in $0,1,\ldots,71$. The same result follows from the elementary greedy lemma: if a finite increasing list begins with $1$ and each next term is at most one plus the sum of all previous terms, its distinct subset sums cover the entire interval from $0$ to the total sum. Here the successive checks are

$$
2\le2,\quad 3\le4,\quad 5\le7,\quad 7\le12,\quad 11\le19,\quad 17\le30,\quad 25\le47.
$$

This is a reproducible finite calculation: the eight floors are the exact quotients $3^n$ divided by $2^n$, the displayed inequalities hold in integer arithmetic, and the resulting covered interval is exactly $[0,71]$. It does not establish completeness of the infinite sequence. Later terms might create gaps, and a finite prefix can never prove a statement about all sufficiently large integers.

## What is known

The primary paper read for this audit is R. L. Graham, “On a conjecture of Erdős in additive number theory,” *Acta Arithmetica* 10 (1964), 63–70, DOI [10.4064/aa-10-1-63-70](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-10-1-63-70). The journal record is [here](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/acta-arithmetica/all/10/1/95427/on-a-conjecture-of-erdos-in-additive-number-theory), and the scanned paper is [here](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/64_02_erdos.pdf). Its introduction on p. 63 defines “complete” and the stronger “entirely complete,” meaning that every positive integer is representable.

Graham’s Theorem 2 on p. 65 proves that $0<t<1$ and $1<\alpha\le5^{1/3}$ imply entire completeness. Theorem 3 on the same page proves, for $0<t<1$ and $1<\alpha<2$, that completeness is equivalent to entire completeness. Theorem 4 on p. 67 reduces the remaining cases to three local patterns, and Theorems 5–6 on pp. 68–69 give an explicit union of the corresponding regions where the sequence is not complete. Graham therefore settles the unit-square subproblem described in his paper, with the complete region having area approximately $0.85$; this is not a solution of the full $(0,\infty)^2$ question.

The maintained page cites P. Erdős and R. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), p. 57, as the original catalogue source, and Graham’s 1964 paper as the primary result behind the remarks. The page says that Graham found arbitrarily many disjoint completeness intervals in the unit-square regime and repeats the conjectural-looking range $1<\alpha<(1+\sqrt5)/2$. Those are source-backed historical statements, not a claim that the complete region is the whole square.

## What remains

The most useful bounded successor is a source-to-formal normalization audit of Graham’s unit-square result. Its input should be the exact p. 63–69 paper, the pinned Lean file, and the live statement. Its target should be a short ledger matching the original $n=1$ sequence to the formal $n=0$ range, defining exactly what `IsAddComplete` means, and listing the hypotheses and conclusion of Graham’s Theorem 6 without broadening its unit-square scope.
