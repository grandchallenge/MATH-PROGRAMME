**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #519, **Turán's pure power-sum problem**, is **PROVED**. Its central assertion is that, whenever a list of complex numbers includes the number one, at least one of its first $n$ power sums has magnitude bounded away from zero by an **absolute constant**, independent of $n$. Atkinson proved the assertion with $c=1/6$ in 1961; Biró subsequently improved the lower bound beyond $1/2$. Determining the optimal constant is a related, harder optimization problem.

## The question in plain language

Take an arbitrary list of $n$ complex numbers

$$
z_1=1,\quad z_2,\ldots,z_n\in\mathbb C.
$$

The numbers need not be real, nonnegative, distinct, nor inside the unit circle. For each positive integer $k$, form a **power sum**:

$$
S_k=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_j^k.
$$

Turán asked whether there is a single universal $c>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{\max_{1\le k\le n}|S_k|>c}
$$

for **every** $n$ and every such list.

Why is this surprising? Complex numbers can cancel. You can arrange $S_1$ to vanish, and even several early power sums to vanish simultaneously. Yet the assertion says that among the **first $n$** sums, at least one always has magnitude bounded away from zero by a fixed number, no matter how large $n$ becomes.

The normalization $z_1=1$ matters. Without any normalization, all $z_j$ could be arbitrarily small, making every power sum arbitrarily small. In Biró's equivalent extremal formulation, the condition is

$$
\max_{1\le j\le n}|z_j|=1.
$$

After reordering a maximizer first and multiplying all numbers by a common unit complex phase, one obtains $z_1=1$; the absolute magnitudes $|S_k|$ are unchanged under that phase rotation. The two normalizations therefore give the same extremal constant.

## A small example

For $n=2$, choose $z_1=1$ and $z_2=-1$. Then

$$
S_1=1-1=0,\qquad S_2=1+1=2.
$$

Checking only the first sum would falsely suggest complete cancellation. The second sum detects the structure missed by the first.

A more dramatic example is the list of **all $n$‑th roots of unity**:

$$
z_j=e^{2\pi i(j-1)/n},\qquad 1\le j\le n.
$$

This list includes $z_1=1$. The geometric-series identity gives

$$
S_k=
\begin{cases}
0,&1\le k<n,\\
n,&k=n.
\end{cases}
$$

Hence **all of the first $n-1$ power sums vanish**, but the $n$‑th equals $n$. This illustrates why including the final exponent $k=n$ is indispensable.

An exact algebraic method avoids numerical roots of unity entirely: work in the ring $\mathbb Z[X]/(X^n-1)$ and sum the monomials $X^{jk}$ for $j=0,\ldots,n-1$. The coefficient pattern proves the cancellations without floating-point approximations.

## What is known

### Atkinson's affirmative answer

Erdős described the problem on printed [p.251 of *Some unsolved problems* (1961)](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=31), Section IV.11.1. That original page was visually inspected. Erdős reports earlier $1/n$‑type estimates of Turán, Erdős, de Bruijn and Uchiyama, then notes that **F. V. Atkinson** had just proved the existence of an absolute constant, with $c=1/6$.

Atkinson's original paper is *On sums of powers of complex numbers*, *Acta Mathematica Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae* **12** (1961), 185–188. Erdős cites it on the same printed page. The complete original Atkinson proof was not retrieved during this audit, so its constant is attributed to the published result rather than reconstructed.

### Biró's improvements and the optimal constant

András Biró, *On a problem of Turán concerning sums of powers of complex numbers*, *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* **65** (1994), 209–216, [DOI 10.1007/BF01875148](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01875148), strengthened the bound to

$$
\max_{1\le k\le n}|S_k|>\frac12.
$$

His later paper, *An improved estimate in a power sum problem of Turán*, *Indagationes Mathematicae* (N.S.) **11** (2000), 343–358, [primary publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0019357700800038), proves that some **effectively computable** universal $q>1/2$ works for every $n$. Its abstract does not supply an explicit numerical value.

For precision, define the extremal quantity

$$
R_n=\inf_{\substack{z_1,\ldots,z_n\in\mathbb C\\ \max_j|z_j|=1}}
\max_{1\le k\le n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_j^k\right|.
$$

By compactness, this infimum is attained. Atkinson and Biró give uniform **lower bounds** on $R_n$.

A distinct paper, Biró's *An upper estimate in Turán's pure power sum problem*, *Indagationes Mathematicae* (N.S.) **11** (2000), 499–508, [DOI 10.1016/S0019-3577(00)80018-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0019-3577(00)80018-X), instead proves

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}R_n<1.
$$

Its constructions yield $R_n<5/6$ for sufficiently large $n$. It also reports Harcos's computer-assisted upper estimate below approximately $0.69368$. These are **construction-side upper bounds on the optimum**, not guarantees for every configuration. The numerical computation was not independently replayed by GCL.

The existence statement is therefore proved, but the optimal constant remains a separate problem. The maintained [Erdős #519 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/519), edited February 2026, labels the existence statement **PROVED (LEAN)**.

### Newton–Girard as an elementary interface

A useful structural argument explains why the first $n$ sums cannot **all** vanish. Write the monic polynomial with the chosen numbers as roots:

$$
P(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-z_j)
=t^n-e_1t^{n-1}+e_2t^{n-2}-\cdots+(-1)^n e_n.
$$

The $e_k$ are elementary symmetric functions with $e_0=1$. The Newton–Girard identities say

$$
ke_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e_{k-j}S_j
\qquad(1\le k\le n).
$$

If $S_1=\cdots=S_n=0$, these identities force $e_1=\cdots=e_n=0$. Hence $P(t)=t^n$ and all roots are zero, contradicting $z_1=1$.

This shows that at least one power sum is **nonzero for each tuple**. It does **not** establish a bound away from zero uniform over all $n$. That quantitative stability is the hard part of Atkinson's original theorem.
