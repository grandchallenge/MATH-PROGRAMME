## The question in plain language

Fix $r\ge3$. An integer $m>1$ is **$r$‐powerful** when every prime $p\mid m$ occurs to exponent at least $r$. Erdős asked two distinct questions: are there infinitely many integers *not* expressible as a sum of at most $r$ positive $r$‐powerful numbers, and does the set of representable integers have natural density zero? Both questions are open in the [maintained source catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/940).

For clarity, write

$$
A_r=\{m\ge1: m=x_1+\cdots+x_j,\ 1\le j\le r,\ x_i\ \text{r-powerful}\}.
$$

The density question is whether $|A_r\cap[1,X]|/X\to0$. Density zero would imply the first assertion, but infinitely many exceptions alone do not imply density zero.

## A small example

*An example and a counting trap*

For $r=3$, both $8=2^3$ and $27=3^3$ are 3-powerful. Their sum $35$ is representable with two summands. The examples show that sums exist, but do not determine the frequency of such sums.

Every perfect $r$‐th power is $r$‐powerful, so the set of sums of at most $r$ perfect $r$‐th powers lies inside $A_r$. That inclusion points in the wrong direction for proving an upper bound on the size of $A_r$. One cannot deduce density zero for $A_r$ from an upper bound on sums of perfect powers alone.

## What is known

The catalogue reports that Erdős initially claimed a simple argument established infinitely many nonrepresentable integers, but Schinzel identified a mistake. This historical claim is therefore **not** an accepted proof.

For $r=2$, Baker and Brüdern [BaBr94] proved the density-zero assertion for sums of at most **two** 2-powerful numbers. This is compatible with Heath-Brown's theorem [He88] that all sufficiently large integers are sums of at most **three** 2-powerful numbers, treated in [Problem #941](https://www.erdosproblems.com/941). The change in the allowed number of summands is mathematically decisive.

For $r=3$, the catalogue notes that even the density-zero question for sums of at most three **cubes** is unresolved. A theorem for cubes, if obtained, would still require extra reasoning to handle all 3-powerful numbers. The related [Problem #1107](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1107) asks about $r+1$ summands and should not be substituted for this $r$‐summand question.

## Why the problem is difficult

The count of individual $r$‐powerful integers below $X$ is sparse, but combining up to $r$ summands creates many additive combinations. The resulting sums collide, and naïve counts of tuples may be too large to establish density zero. A proof must exploit genuine arithmetic restrictions or cancellation, not silently assume all sums are distinct or that a heuristic volume estimate is a rigorous upper bound.

## GCL's current state

This article records the two open claims, the historical correction, and the established $r=2$ comparison. It does not revive the discredited counting argument. A bounded finite experiment could enumerate powerful numbers by exact prime valuations and use dynamic programming to compute $A_r\cap[1,X]$; such computations **cannot** settle a limit as $X\to\infty$.
