## The question in plain language

An Egyptian fraction writes a proper fraction as a sum of distinct reciprocals. For integers $1\leq a<b$, let $D(a,b)$ be the smallest possible value of the largest denominator in such a representation, and then set

$$
D(b)=\max_{1\leq a<b}D(a,b).
$$

The maximum is over every numerator, not just numerators coprime to $b$. The question attached to Erdős Problem 305 asks whether there is an absolute constant and a function tending to zero such that, for all sufficiently large $b$,

$$
D(b)\leq Cb(\log b)^{1+\delta(b)}.
$$

This is the precise meaning of saying that $D(b)$ is at most a constant multiple of $b(\log b)^{1+o(1)}$. It is an asymptotic question about how far one must search for denominators, uniformly over all fractions with denominator $b$; it does not ask for a bounded number of summands.

The original issue is [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1575](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1575). Its frozen catalogue packet identifies the entry at `teorth/erdosproblems@536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89`, blob `4e60263a80af6207aca494d2e1388e44fa372889`, and records the catalogue status as `proved` on 2025-08-31.

## A small example

Take $a=2$ and $b=3$. The distinct-denominator identity

$$
\frac{2}{3}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6}
$$

shows that $D(2,3)\leq 6$. To check that the bound is exact, enumerate every subset of the possible denominators $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ with rational arithmetic. The reproducible check was:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations
target = Fraction(2, 3)
hits = [s for r in range(6) for s in combinations(range(1, 6), r)
        if sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in s), Fraction(0)) == target]
print(hits)
print(Fraction(1, 2) + Fraction(1, 6))
```

Its exact output was `[]` and `2/3`. Therefore no representation has largest denominator at most $5$, and $D(2,3)=6$. This is a finite check of one small value, not evidence for the asymptotic bound. In particular, checking many denominators cannot establish a statement quantified over every sufficiently large $b$.

## What is known

The maintained live page [Erdős Problem 305](https://www.erdosproblems.com/305) gives the same definition and says `PROVED`. It cites Erdős and Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), p. 38, as the original source. Bleicher and Erdős, “Denominators of Egyptian fractions,” *Journal of Number Theory* 8 (1976), 157–168, established the earlier upper bound that is a constant multiple of $b(\log b)^2$; their primary scan is available [here](https://combinatorica.hu/~p_erdos/1976-10.pdf). For prime $b$, they also supplied a lower bound of order $b\log b$.

Yokota’s “On a problem of Bleicher and Erdős,” *Journal of Number Theory* 30 (1988), 198–207, DOI [10.1016/0022-314X(88)90017-0](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-314X(88)90017-0), improved the upper bound to a factor of $(\log\log b)^4(\log\log\log b)^2$ beyond $b\log b$.

The most direct primary evidence checked here is Yang P. Liu and Mehtaab Sawhney, “On further questions regarding unit fractions,” [arXiv:2404.07113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07113), version 1 submitted 10 April 2024. Their Theorem 1.5, PDF p. 3, states that for every $1\leq a<b$ there are distinct integers $1<n_1<\cdots<n_k$ with

$$
n_k\leq b(\log b)(\log\log b)^3(\log\log\log b)^{O(1)}.
$$

The proof is on PDF p. 14. Since the displayed extra factors are $(\log b)^{o(1)}$, this implies the original target. The paper explicitly identifies a sharper factor $(\log\log b)^{1+o(1)}$ as an open direction; that refinement is not needed to settle Problem 305 as stated. The downloaded primary PDF has SHA-256 `29f6ea8f73bb17a9084bec115af4b3d8015d9b6d5e3c803a3caa8f79a287d91b`.

## What remains
