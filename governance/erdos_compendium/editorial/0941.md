## The question in plain language

An integer \(m>1\) is **powerful** if each prime factor occurs with exponent at least two. Erdős and Ivić asked whether every sufficiently large positive integer can be represented as the sum of **at most three** powerful positive integers. The answer is **yes**: Heath-Brown proved it in 1988. [Erdős Problem #941](https://www.erdosproblems.com/941).

## An example

The integer \(25=5^2\) is powerful, and \(33=8+25=2^3+5^2\) is a sum of two powerful numbers. The integer \(52=4+16+32\) is a sum of three powerful numbers: \(4=2^2\), \(16=2^4\), and \(32=2^5\). These are exact elementary witnesses. They do not themselves prove the **all sufficiently large integers** theorem.

The terminology matters: a powerful integer is not necessarily a square. For example \(8=2^3\) and \(72=2^3\cdot3^2\) are powerful but not squares. Limiting summands to squares would change the question substantially.

## A useful exact representation

Every positive powerful integer can be written \(a^2b^3\) with \(b\) squarefree. If a prime occurs with exponent \(e\ge2\), choose the exponent of \(b\) to be \(0\) when \(e\) is even and \(1\) when \(e\) is odd. The remaining even exponent determines the power in \(a^2\). Conversely every \(a^2b^3\) is powerful. This gives a sound exact finite-generation method for bounded experiments.

In particular, every square is powerful, but three squares alone cannot represent all large integers: Legendre's three-square theorem excludes integers of the form \(4^u(8v+7)\). Heath-Brown's theorem makes essential use of the wider class of powerful numbers; the result cannot be attributed to the three-square theorem.

## What is known

Heath-Brown [He88] established the affirmative answer. The original question appears in Erdős [Er76d] and the 1986 Oberwolfach problems list [Ob1], where it was attributed to Erdős and Ivić. The current catalogue labels #941 **PROVED** and explicitly distinguishes the unsolved higher-\(r\) version [Problem #1107](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1107) and the different question [#940](https://www.erdosproblems.com/940), which allows only \(r\) summands of \(r\)-powerful numbers for \(r\ge3\).

The exact threshold above which all integers admit such representations is **not supplied** by this manuscript. Without extracting the effective constants of the published proof, one must not claim a numerical cutoff.

## Why the theorem is significant

The positive integers representable by at most two powerful summands have density zero, as recorded in #940 for the Baker–Brüdern result. Yet allowing **three** powerful summands covers every sufficiently large integer. This illustrates a sharp qualitative change in additive coverage when the number of summands increases by one. Density zero for two summands does not conflict with eventual full coverage for three.

## GCL's current state

The affirmative theorem is a literature result, not an original proof. The \(a^2b^3\) characterization is elementary and justified above. No independent reconstruction of Heath-Brown's analytic proof, Lean formalization, exact effective bound, or complete numerical coverage certificate is claimed.
