## The question in plain language

A set of positive integers has **positive natural density** when it occupies a nonvanishing fraction of the positive integers in the limit. Erdős asked whether such a set must contain *three distinct* numbers $a,b,c$ satisfying

$$
\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=c.
$$

Here $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ is the *least common multiple*: the smallest positive integer divisible by both $a$ and $b$. For instance,

$$
\operatorname{lcm}(6,10)=30.
$$

The three numbers $6,10,30$ are distinct and form the requested configuration. The density assumption is essential: finite or sufficiently sparse sets may avoid it.

The phrase **three distinct** is especially important. If $a\mid b$, then $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=b$; even an infinite divisibility chain $2,4,8,16,\ldots$ does **not**, by itself, produce a witness with the least common multiple distinct from both inputs.

The answer was published **yes** by Erdős, Sárközy and Szemerédi in **1966**, using Kleitman's **1966** combinatorial lemma. Kleitman's often-cited 1971 union-free paper is a subsequent strengthening, not the first resolving publication. The current [Erdős #487 catalogue entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/487) reports **PROVED (LEAN)**. That reports the state of published mathematics and external formalization, not an independent GCL certification.

## A small example

The bridge from set theory to least common multiples is exact. Pick distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ and a positive integer $q$ coprime to all of them. Associate every subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}$ with

$$
d(S)=q\prod_{i\in S}p_i.
$$

Then the prime-factor rule for the least common multiple gives

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(d(S),d(T))=d(S\cup T).}
$$

The mapping $S\mapsto d(S)$ is injective, so **three distinct subsets** give **three distinct integers**. Thus an exact union triple

$$
S\cup T=U,\qquad S,T,U\text{ pairwise distinct},
$$

becomes the desired numerical configuration. With primes $2,3,5$ and $q=1$, choose $S=\{2,3\}$, $T=\{2,5\}$ and $U=\{2,3,5\}$. Their products are $6,10,30$.

Conversely, a finite family of subsets with no three-distinct union triple gives, under this correspondence, a finite set with no three-distinct least-common-multiple triple. For example, the family of all subsets of a fixed cardinality is union-free in this sense: the union of two *different* same-size subsets is strictly larger than either, so cannot lie at the same level.

**Important limitation:** this finite correspondence is not, by itself, a proof about *arbitrary positive-density sets of integers*. Passing from ordinary density to sufficiently rich multiplicative Boolean cubes is a substantive transference step. It must not be omitted from the published argument.

## What is known

### Erdős's original question

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p. 236, the final paragraph of Problem I.26, Erdős refers to the theorem of Davenport and Erdős that a positive-density sequence contains an infinite divisibility chain

$$
a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots,\qquad a_i\mid a_{i+1},
$$

then asks whether one can find **three distinct** elements whose least common multiple is the third. The original printed page was visually inspected. The earlier chain result is not itself a solution, because the lcm of two comparable members is one of its inputs. The 1936/1937 Davenport–Erdős original proof was not replayed here.

### Direct resolving paper (1966)

P. Erdős, A. Sárközy and E. Szemerédi, *On the solvability of the equations [a_i,a_j]=a_r and (a'_i,a'_j)=a'_r in sequences of positive density*, *J. Math. Anal. Appl.* **15** (1966), 60–64, [primary scan](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1966-18.pdf). Original printed pp.60–62, including Theorem 1 and its proof, were directly inspected.

Theorem 1 proves **infinitely many distinct LCM triples** provided, for infinitely many large $x$, the harmonic sum over the set exceeds a suitable constant times

$$
\frac{\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}.
$$

Positive **lower natural density** suffices because its harmonic sum grows proportionally to $\log x$. In the original proof, each $a_i$ is expressed as a square times a squarefree integer, $a_i=r_i^2b_i$. A fixed square part retains enough reciprocal weight. Divisor counting then produces a squarefree integer with many selected divisors, whose distinct prime supports form a sufficiently large set family. Applying Kleitman's combinatorial lemma gives the requisite union triple and hence the numerical LCM triple. The 1966 authors explicitly cite D. Kleitman, *On a combinatorial problem of Erdős*, *Proc. Amer. Math. Soc.* **17** (1966), 139–141, reference [6]. The underlying Kleitman proof and the constants throughout the derived density bound have not been independently replayed.

### Union-free set families and the solution

A family $\mathcal F\subseteq 2^{[k]}$ is *union-free* if no **three pairwise distinct** $S,T,U\in\mathcal F$ satisfy $S\cup T=U$.

Erdős Problem #447 asks whether every such family satisfies

$$
\frac{|\mathcal F|}{2^k}\longrightarrow0\qquad(k\to\infty).
$$

The maintained [#447 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/447) attributes a positive solution, including the stronger asymptotic estimate

$$
|\mathcal F|\le(1+o(1))\binom{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor},
$$

to Daniel J. Kleitman, *Collections of subsets containing no two sets and their union*, in *Combinatorics* (proceedings of the Los Angeles meeting), American Mathematical Society (1971), pp. 153–155. The original proceedings table of contents confirms the paper and page location, but the full three-page proof has **not** been independently retrieved or checked.

Kleitman's later primary paper, *Extremal properties of collections of subsets containing no two sets and their union*, *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A* **20** (1976), 390–392, DOI 10.1016/0097-3165(76)90037-6, describes improved bounds and Lubell–Yamamoto-type inequalities. Its publisher metadata and abstract were inspected, **not** the complete paper.

The first direct 1966 proof gives the global density-to-prime-support transference in its squarefree decomposition and divisor estimates. These published steps were inspected, but the imported Kleitman 1966 lemma remains a separate unreplayed dependency.
