## The question in plain language

For n>=600, a graph on 2n+1 vertices and at least n²+n+1 edges has a SIMPLE path of exactly three edges whose two endpoints have the same degree IN THE ORIGINAL GRAPH. A 3-edge walk with repeated vertices is not enough; endpoints are not required to be adjacent.

## A small example

In K_{n,n+1}, any 3-edge path begins in one bipartition class and ends in the other; endpoint degrees n+1 versus n, so it cannot have equal-degree endpoints. For n=2, K2,3 has five vertices and six edges; its 3-paths have endpoint degrees 3 and 2. This finite structural witness verifies sharpness logic but not n>=600 positive theorem.

## What is known

Maintained #816 and independently read original published Kaizhe Chen–Jie Ma, *A problem of Erdős and Hajnal on paths with equal-degree endpoints*, JCTB 179 (July 2026), 1–18, DOI 10.1016/j.jctb.2026.01.006, Theorem 2: for n>=600, the unique graph on 2n+1 vertices with at least n²+n edges lacking such a path is K_{n,n+1}. It has exactly n²+n edges and is the sharp exception. The original published theorem statement is independently identified, not full proof kernel-replayed.

The sharp theorem identifies the entire exception class: when $n\geq600$, among graphs on $2n+1$ vertices with at least $n^2+n$ edges, the only graph without equal-degree endpoints of a simple three-edge path is $K_{n,n+1}$. The latter has exactly $n^2+n$ edges, and each three-edge path crosses between its two degree classes. Raising the edge count by one removes the exception.

The published theorem can be separated into its threshold and exception:

$$
|V(G)|=2n+1,\quad e(G)\geq n^2+n,\quad n\geq600.
$$

If no simple three-edge path has equal-degree endpoints, then $G$ must be $K_{n,n+1}$. Conversely, in $K_{n,n+1}$ any path of odd length crosses from one part to the other, where the degrees differ. Therefore one additional edge above $n^2+n$ rules out the sole exception.
