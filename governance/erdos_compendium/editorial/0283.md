## The question in plain language

Choose a polynomial $p$ with integer coefficients and positive leading coefficient, and is not forced to have a common divisor larger than $1$ on all positive inputs. The question asks whether every sufficiently large integer $m$ can be made in two ways at once: as a sum of distinct positive denominators whose reciprocals add to $1$, and as the sum of the corresponding values of $p$. In symbols, does

$$
\forall p\;\Bigl[\bigl(\mathrm{lc}(p)>0\bigr)\land\neg\exists d\ge 2\;\forall n\ge 1\;(d\mid p(n))\Bigr]\Rightarrow
\exists M\;\forall m\ge M\;\exists k\ge 1\;\exists 1\le n_1<\cdots<n_k:
\sum_{i=1}^k\frac1{n_i}=1\ \land\ \sum_{i=1}^k p(n_i)=m.
$$

The quantifiers matter. “Sufficiently large” means one threshold $M$ works for every later integer, not merely infinitely many $m$. The denominators are distinct because of the strict inequalities, and the same $n_i$ must satisfy both equations. The divisor condition is a local obstruction: if one $d>1$ divides every available value $p(n)$, then sums of those values cannot cover every large integer.

The exact public statement is [Erdős Problem #283](https://www.erdosproblems.com/283). The original GCL assignment is [issue #1553](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1553), and the source audit’s authenticated [RESULT/1 comment](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1553#issuecomment-6135376048) is the evidence reviewed here.

## A small example

Take the square polynomial $p(n)=n^2$ and denominators $2,4,6,12$. The reciprocal calculation is exact:

$$
\frac12+\frac14+\frac16+\frac1{12}=\frac6{12}+\frac3{12}+\frac2{12}+\frac1{12}=1.
$$

The corresponding value is

$$
2^2+4^2+6^2+12^2=4+16+36+144=200.
$$

Thus $200$ has the required representation for this particular polynomial. The following calculation uses rational arithmetic, so it does not rely on floating-point rounding:

```python
from fractions import Fraction

A = [2, 4, 6, 12]
assert sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in A), Fraction(0)) == 1
square_sum = sum(n * n for n in A)
assert square_sum == 200
print("reciprocal sum = 1; square sum =", square_sum)
```

Its output is `reciprocal sum = 1; square sum = 200`. This is a checked finite witness, not evidence that all large integers work. It also illustrates why the two requirements cannot be checked independently: changing one denominator can preserve the reciprocal identity while changing the polynomial sum.

## What is known

The fully general polynomial statement should not be described as a verified published theorem on the evidence presently available. Several important special cases are genuine.

R. L. Graham’s “A theorem on partitions,” *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society* 3 (1963), 435–441, proves that every integer exceeding $77$ is a sum of distinct positive integers with reciprocal sum $1$; this is Theorem 1 on p. 435, with DOI [10.1017/S1446788700039045](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788700039045). It also proves the reciprocal-partition generalization for a positive rational target and a lower bound on the parts. This is the case $p(n)=n$, not the arbitrary-polynomial problem.

For $p(n)=n^2$, Max A. Alekseyev’s “On partitions into squares of distinct integers whose reciprocals sum to 1,” published in *The Mathematics of Various Entertaining Subjects*, vol. 3 (2019), pp. 213–221, proves that $8542$ is the largest exception; hence every $m>8542$ works. The accessible [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05928) records the theorem and publication data, with the published chapter linked by DOI [10.2307/j.ctvd58spj.18](https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctvd58spj.18).

Cassels’s 1960 paper, “On the representation of integers as the sums of distinct summands taken from a fixed set,” *Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum* 21, pp. 111–124, has an accessible [repository record and scan](https://acta.bibl.u-szeged.hu/13906/). Its Theorem I begins on p. 111 and its proof runs through pp. 112–122. The source audit found a completeness theorem for sums of distinct elements of a set, but did not find in that paper the reciprocal-sum-preserving polynomial theorem attributed to it by the catalogue page. That is a source-fidelity exception, not a reason to discard Cassels’s actual theorem.

Wouter van Doorn’s [arXiv:2502.02200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02200), v2 (23 July 2025), Theorems 1–3, gives bounds for partitions with prescribed reciprocal sum and lower-bounded parts. It supports the linear reciprocal-partition background, but the claimed polynomial ranges such as $p(n)=n+b$ for $1\le b\le5000$ and $p(n)=n^2+b$ for $1\le b\le800$ were not independently verified against a primary proof here. Burr’s attribution in the formal catalogue likewise remains without an identified primary paper in this audit.

The public page also cites an original source as `[ErGr80, p. 32]`. That book/page was not available in the retrieved source packet, so the original attribution and any claim resting only on that page remain unchecked. “Unavailable” here means unavailable to this audit, not disproved.
