## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $k$, consider all ways to write one as a sum of exactly $k$ distinct unit fractions:

$$
X_k=\left\{(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^k:x_1<\cdots<x_k,\ \sum_{i=1}^k\frac1{x_i}=1\right\}.
$$

When $X_k$ is nonempty, define $f(k)$ to be the largest possible smallest denominator:

$$
f(k)=\max\left\{x_1:(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\in X_k\right\}.
$$

The question is whether

$$
f(k)\sim\frac{k}{e-1}.
$$

The quantifier matters. This means that for every positive $\varepsilon$, there should be a $K$ such that for every $k\ge K$, the set $X_k$ is nonempty and

$$
\left|\frac{f(k)}{k/(e-1)}-1\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

It is an assertion about all sufficiently large $k$, not merely a statement about a subsequence. The elementary splitting identity below shows that $X_k$ is nonempty for every $k\ge3$, so the domain issue can be settled separately. The difficult part is making the smallest denominator as large as the asymptotic predicts.

## A small example

The identity

$$
\frac1n=\frac1{n+1}+\frac1{n(n+1)}
$$

preserves the reciprocal sum and increases the number of terms by one. If it is applied to the current largest denominator, the two new denominators are larger than every denominator already present, so strict ordering and distinctness are preserved. Starting from

$$
1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac17+\frac1{42},
$$

we can repeatedly split an offending denominator until every denominator is greater than $5$. The following calculation uses exact rational arithmetic, not floating-point approximations.

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from collections import Counter

a = [2, 3, 7, 42]
N = 5
while True:
    c = Counter(a)
    bad = [n for n in a if n <= N or c[n] > 1]
    if not bad:
        break
    n = min(bad)
    a.remove(n)
    a += [n + 1, n * (n + 1)]

a.sort()
print(a)
print(len(a), a[0], a[-1], len(a) == len(set(a)))
print(sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in a), Fraction(0)))
```

The output is a 24-term increasing list beginning with $6$ and ending with $3263442$; the checks return `True` for distinctness and `1` for the exact sum. This is a finite padding illustration. It does not estimate $f(k)$ and does not prove the asymptotic theorem.

## What is known

There is an immediate upper bound. If the smallest denominator is $u$, then the reciprocal sum is at most the sum obtained from the $k$ consecutive denominators $u,u+1,\ldots,u+k-1$. Comparing that harmonic sum with an integral gives

$$
1\le \log\left(1+\frac{k}{u}\right)+o(1),
$$

and hence $u\le(1+o(1))k/(e-1)$. Thus the conjectural constant is forced from above.

The published primary source is Ernest S. Croot III, “On unit fractions with denominators in short intervals,” *Acta Arithmetica* 99 (2001), 99–114, DOI [10.4064/aa99-2-1](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa99-2-1). On printed p. 99 Croot defines the same $X_k$ and asks the maximum $x_1$ question. His Main Theorem on printed p. 100 states that, for a fixed positive rational $r$ and every $N>1$, there are distinct integers

$$
N<x_1<\cdots<x_k\le\left(e^r+O_r\left(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\right)\right)N
$$

whose reciprocal sum is $r$. Taking $r=1$ gives the relevant short-interval construction. The proof of that theorem reaches its conclusion on printed pp. 104–105 in the accessible [journal PDF](https://www.impan.pl/shop/en/publication/transaction/download/product/83061). This is a published external proof claim; inspecting the theorem and proof location is not the same as independently rechecking every argument, and it is not GCL certification.

Croot’s own introduction says that the two Erdős–Graham questions are solved “for infinitely many $k$.” That wording is the central source exception. His theorem is quantified over the lower cutoff $N$, whereas Problem 284 asks for a value for every sufficiently large cardinality $k$. A proposed bridge is to choose $N$ as a function of $k$, ensure Croot’s representation has at most $k$ terms, and then use largest-denominator splitting to reach exactly $k$ terms without changing the smallest denominator. The published statement, as checked here, does not by itself display the required cardinality bound or the resulting argument for every sufficiently large $k$.

## What remains

The bounded mathematical-documentary task is to prove or refute the bridge. One must state a function $N(k)$, extract from Croot a representation with cardinality at most $k$, and show that repeated splitting of its largest denominator reaches exactly $k$ terms while retaining a smallest denominator of $(1-o(1))k/(e-1)$. The error term $O(\log\log N/\log N)$ must be carried through an explicit $\varepsilon$–$K$ argument. Eventual nonemptiness is easier: split the largest term of $1=1/2+1/3+1/6$ repeatedly.

If Croot’s theorem cannot provide the needed cardinality control, the correct result is an unresolved semantic bridge, not a silent upgrade from “infinitely many” to “all large.” A source-faithful follow-up should stop at that blocker and should not describe the catalogue label as a proof of the stronger quantifier.
