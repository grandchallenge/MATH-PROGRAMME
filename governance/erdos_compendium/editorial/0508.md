## The question in plain language

Colour **every point** in the Euclidean plane. Two points separated by exactly one unit must receive different colours. What is the smallest number of colours needed?

The mathematical object is the infinite **unit-distance graph** $G_{\mathbb R^2}$, whose vertices are every point of the plane and whose edges join pairs at Euclidean distance exactly one. Its chromatic number is

$$
\boxed{\chi(\mathbb R^2)
=\min\{k:\exists c:\mathbb R^2\to\{1,\ldots,k\},
\ \|x-y\|=1\Rightarrow c(x)\ne c(y)\}.}
$$

The implication must hold for **all real points**. Colours need not define measurable, Borel or geometrically nice regions: arbitrary pointwise assignments are allowed. Proving a colouring impossible only for measurable colour classes would be a weaker theorem than the unrestricted one.

Erdős's 1961 version asks specifically whether the plane can be partitioned into **four** such independent sets. Modern mathematical work asks for the exact $\chi$, not only a yes-or-no answer about four colours.

## A small example

Three vertices of a unit equilateral triangle are pairwise separated by exactly one. They must therefore receive **three distinct colours**. This proves immediately that

$$
\chi(\mathbb R^2)\ge3.
$$

A square with side one has four unit-length **sides** but its diagonals have length $\sqrt2$. Its induced unit-distance graph is a **four-cycle**, so its four vertices can be coloured with **two** colours alternating around the square. This does not mean that two colours suffice for the entire plane: finite examples provide **lower-bound obstructions**, never an upper colouring of the infinite plane.

The crucial general principle is that for any **finite** set of planar points $V$, the induced graph $G_V$ has edges precisely where pairwise distance is one, and any valid plane colouring restricts to a proper colouring of $G_V$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\chi(\mathbb R^2)\ge\chi(G_V).}
$$

This reduction is exact but one-sided. Finding a finite graph that needs five colours proves the plane needs at least five. It does **not** tell us whether six or seven colours are enough globally.

## What is known

### Erdős, de Grey, and Isbell

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.246, Problem III.7, [the original page](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=26) was visually inspected. Erdős says a three-colour partition is impossible and asks about **four**.

The original lower bound was gradually improved by finite unit-distance graphs. The **Moser spindle** proves at least four colours are needed. Aubrey D. N. J. de Grey, *The chromatic number of the plane is at least 5* (2018), [arXiv:1804.02385](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.02385), constructed a finite unit-distance graph with **1581 vertices** that is not four-colourable. Therefore $\chi(\mathbb R^2)\ge5$.

The upper bound $\chi(\mathbb R^2)\le7$ follows from a periodic plane colouring based on carefully sized **hexagonal cells**, credited to John R. Isbell. Interior and boundary cases must be handled consistently so that same-colour points never lie exactly one unit apart. Combining these results gives the long-standing bound

$$
5\le\chi(\mathbb R^2)\le7.
$$

The maintained [Erdős #508 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/508), last edited January 2026, still reports this classical range and classifies the exact problem **OPEN**.
