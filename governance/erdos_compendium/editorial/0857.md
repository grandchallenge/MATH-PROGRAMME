## The question in plain language

Imagine a collection of subsets of the same finite universe. A *sunflower* is a group of sets whose pairwise intersections are all identical. The common intersection is the *core*; the portions outside the core are disjoint *petals*. For example, the three sets $\{1,2\}$, $\{1,3\}$ and $\{1,4\}$ form a sunflower with core $\{1\}$.

For integers $n$ and $k$, define $m(n,k)$ as the smallest number of **distinct** subsets of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ that forces a sunflower consisting of $k$ members. [Erdős Problem #857](https://www.erdosproblems.com/857) asks for estimates, or ideally an asymptotic formula, for $m(n,k)$ when $k$ is fixed and $n$ grows.

The subsets need not have equal sizes. This is often called the *weak* or *non-uniform* sunflower problem; it is distinct from the stronger classical question about uniform-size families.

## A small example

A universe with two elements has four subsets: $\varnothing$, $\{1\}$, $\{2\}$ and $\{1,2\}$. The three sets $\varnothing$, $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ form a sunflower with empty core, but the three sets $\varnothing$, $\{1\}$ and $\{1,2\}$ do not: the intersection of the last two is $\{1\}$, while the other intersections are empty.

Exhaustive enumeration of every family of subsets gives the following exact small cases for three-petal sunflowers.

| Universe size $n$ | Largest family without a 3-sunflower | $m(n,3)$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 2 | 3 | 4 |
| 3 | 5 | 6 |
| 4 | 8 | 9 |

The value $m(1,3)=3$ is vacuous: the universe has only two distinct subsets. The [finite-check script](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/blob/editorial/ec-bulk-006-200/governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_856_857.py) checks all triples in all candidate families for $n\le4$. These finite cases are not evidence of a sharp exponential growth rate.

## What is known

In his [1970 paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1970-21.pdf), printed pages 127–128, Erdős phrased the problem in terms of sets with the same pairwise **union**. This is equivalent to the present intersection formulation. Indeed, if $U$ is the universe, De Morgan's law gives

$$
(U\setminus A)\cup(U\setminus B)=U\setminus(A\cap B).
$$

Taking complements is a bijection on subsets of $U$, so it converts families with a common pairwise intersection into families with a common pairwise union, preserving their size. The change of language does not change $m(n,k)$.

Eric Naslund and Will Sawin proved a substantial bound for three-sunflower-free families in [*Upper bounds for sunflower-free sets* (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1017/fms.2017.12). If $F$ contains no three-sunflower, their theorem implies

$$
|F|\le3n\sum_{j\le n/3}\binom nj
\le\left(\frac{3}{2^{2/3}}\right)^{n(1+o(1))}.
$$

Thus $m(n,3)$ is at most one more than this upper bound. The published theorem gives an exponential upper estimate, but does not settle the exact asymptotic formula sought by Erdős.

## What remains

A useful bounded formal task is to prove that taking complements preserves the sunflower threshold. A second step would establish the finite identity

$$
m(n,k)=1+\max\{|F|:F\subseteq\mathcal P([n])\text{ contains no }k\text{-sunflower}\}.
$$

This identity depends on the family being a set of distinct subsets and on the usual convention that a forcing threshold above the size of the whole power set is permitted. The finite values in the table provide exact tests for any formalization, including the vacuous case $n=1$. None of this supplies a sharp asymptotic formula.
