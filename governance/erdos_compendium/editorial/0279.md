## The question in plain language

Give each prime number $p$ one residue class: choose an integer $r_p$ with $0\le r_p<p$, and then consider all numbers congruent to $r_p$ modulo $p$. Every member of that class can be written as $r_p+tp$. The question asks whether the classes can be chosen so that, for every integer $k\ge 3$, every sufficiently large integer $n$ has at least one representation with $t\ge k$.

In symbols, the proposed choice must satisfy

$$
\forall k\ge 3\;\exists (r_p)_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}\;\exists N\;\forall n\ge N\;\exists p,t:
$$

$$
p\ \mathrm{prime},\qquad 0\le r_p<p,\qquad t\ge k,\qquad n=r_p+tp.
$$

The threshold $N$ may depend on $k$, and the residue assignment may also depend on $k$. What cannot vary with $n$ is the assignment once $k$ has been fixed: each prime gets one permanent class. The inequality $t\ge k$ is important. It says that the representation must occur at least $k$ steps along the progression, so necessarily $n\ge kp$. This is stronger than merely asking whether every large integer belongs to one of the chosen residue classes.

The intended answer is currently unknown. “All sufficiently large” means all integers beyond one fixed threshold, not merely a large proportion, not infinitely many integers, and not a different threshold chosen separately for each finite experiment.

## A small example

Here is a finite check that makes the quantifiers concrete. Take $k=3$, use the primes at most $31$, and try the simple assignment $r_p=p-1$. For this assignment, a number $n$ is covered by $p$ exactly when

$$
n\equiv p-1\pmod p\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3p\le n.
$$

For $n=59$, the witnesses include

$$
59=1+29\cdot2=2+19\cdot3=4+11\cdot5.
$$

Thus $p=2,3,5$ all work, with $t=29,19,11$ respectively. For $n=60$, an eligible prime would have to satisfy $p\le 60/3=20$ and divide $61$, because $60\equiv-1\pmod p$. No such prime exists, so $60$ is not covered by this assignment.

An independent enumeration of $30\le n\le100$ found $55$ covered values and these $16$ uncovered values:

$$
30,36,40,42,46,52,58,60,66,70,72,78,82,88,96,100.
$$

This is a valid finite certificate against this particular assignment on this particular interval. It is not evidence that every possible assignment fails, and it is not evidence that the Erdős question is false. Conversely, a finite assignment that covers a long interval would not establish eventual coverage. The distinction between a finite window and one assignment covering the entire tail is the central logical hazard in this problem.

## What is known

The original source is Erdős and Graham’s *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, Monographies de l’Enseignement Mathématique 28 (Geneva, 1980), printed p. 29. In the paragraph that introduces this problem, they explain that if a sequence of moduli satisfies $\sum_i1/n_i=\infty$, then almost all integers can be represented as $a_i+tn_i$ with $t\ge2$. They immediately distinguish this density-one statement from coverage of all sufficiently large integers. They then ask whether, for some fixed $k$, all but finitely many integers can be represented with $t\ge k$, and note that the prime-modulus case already seems difficult for $k=3$.

That passage supplies the historical formulation and a useful comparison, not a solution. “Almost all” permits an exceptional set of density zero; problem #279 asks for no exceptions after a finite point. Those are very different conclusions.

There is related sieve technology, but it does not close this question. For example, Ford, Green, Konyagin, and Tao prove in Theorem 1 on p. 936 of their 2016 *Annals of Mathematics* paper that long intervals of consecutive composite numbers exist, using a random construction of residue classes to cover a suitable finite set of primes. Their construction is designed for finite prime-gap intervals. It does not provide one fixed residue class for every prime that covers every sufficiently large integer with the lower-bound condition $t\ge k$.

The authenticated source return identifies the strongest safe finite interface as follows. For fixed $k,N,M$, let $F(k,N,M)$ say that residues can be chosen for the primes $p\le M/k$ so that every $N\le n\le M$ has a witness:

$$
F(k,N,M):\quad\exists(r_p)_{p\le M/k}\;\forall N\le n\le M\;\exists p\le n/k:
$$

$$
p\ \mathrm{prime},\qquad n\equiv r_p\pmod p,\qquad 0\le r_p<p.
$$

A single fixed $N$ for which $F(k,N,M)$ is satisfiable for every $M\ge N$ would, by compactness, give the global tail statement. Solutions whose thresholds or residue choices drift with $M$ would not be enough.

## What remains

The first useful successor task is finite and falsifiable: fix $k=3$, $N=30$, and $M=100$, specify every residue $r_p$ for $p\le M/3$, and emit either a witness $(n,p,t)$ for every target or the first uncovered target together with all eligible primes. Every witness must satisfy primality, $0\le r_p<p$, $t\ge3$, and $n=r_p+tp$. Independent recomputation should reproduce the result.

That task is only a test of an assignment. The substantive missing step is uniform permanence. A proof would need to show that, as new scales and new primes are introduced, the remaining uncovered integers are small enough for the available fresh residue classes, while all previously covered integers retain a valid witness. The bookkeeping must work for every scale, not just for a chosen list of intervals. A computation can locate patterns or refute a proposed assignment; it cannot certify termination of this infinite process.

The clean stopping rule is therefore conservative: stop at the prescribed finite bound, or at the first uncovered target, invalid residue, failed maturity inequality, or unsupported source hypothesis. Do not promote finite success to a global theorem.
