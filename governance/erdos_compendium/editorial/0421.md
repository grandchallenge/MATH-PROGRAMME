## The question in plain language

Choose a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers $1\leq d_1<d_2<\cdots$. For any indices $u\leq v$, multiply the consecutive entries from $d_u$ through $d_v$. Erdős Problem 421 asks whether the sequence can be chosen so that **every such consecutive-block product is different**, while its selected integers have **natural density one**. If $D$ is the selected set, the second requirement is

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|D\cap[1,x]|}{x}=1.
$$

This permits deleting an asymptotically negligible proportion of the positive integers but not a fixed positive fraction. The two demands are in tension: removing many numbers makes products easier to distinguish, whereas density one allows very few omissions. The requirement concerns all pairs of nonempty index intervals, not merely consecutive intervals of a given length. A finite prefix cannot establish density one because the latter is an asymptotic property.

## A small example

The sequence $(2,3,6)$ fails: the product of its first two terms equals $2\cdot3=6$, the product of its final one-term block. Thus index ranges $[1,2]$ and $[3,3]$ collide despite having different lengths. This is exactly the kind of equality the conjectured construction would have to avoid.

By contrast, $(2,3,5,7,11)$ has fifteen nonempty consecutive blocks and all fifteen products are different. This was verified by exact multiplication of every interval. Indeed, if we take *all primes* as our infinite sequence, unique prime factorization makes the consecutive-block products distinct: each product determines the precise prime factors that occurred and hence the block. But prime numbers have natural density zero, so this does not answer the actual question.

If every selected entry is at least $2$, a strict extension of one block multiplies its product by at least $2$ and cannot preserve it. In that setting two blocks that are properly nested cannot collide; the interesting finite collisions must involve nonnested intervals. This elementary reduction helps with exhaustive searches but is much weaker than a proof that dense infinite sequences exist.

## What is known

The original statement appears in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **printed page 84** of the [scanned book](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). The original source asks the exact density-one consecutive-product question and gives no full construction there.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #421](https://www.erdosproblems.com/421) page, observed in the source inquiry on 13 October 2026, labels the problem **OPEN**. It reports Selfridge's construction of sequences of density greater than $1/e-\varepsilon$ for any prescribed positive $\varepsilon$. This is meaningful positive-density progress, but its numerical threshold is strictly below one. It does not provide the required density-one set. Related Problem #786 discusses the construction and adjacent ideas.

The maintained page also indicates that a solution has been **claimed in user comments**. Comments are not automatically adjudicated mathematical proofs. The page retains OPEN status, and this bounded source inquiry has not located an independently verified published density-one construction. This is a material evidence boundary: a reported solution claim should trigger critical examination, not automatic promotion to a theorem.
