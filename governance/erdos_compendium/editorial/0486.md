## The question in plain language

Imagine indefinitely many modular exclusions. At each modulus $n$ in an arbitrary set $A$ of positive integers, choose forbidden remainders $X_n\subseteq\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. An integer $m$ is allowed if it avoids each exclusion whose modulus is smaller than $m$:

$$
B_{>}=\{m\ge1:\ \forall n\in A,\quad m>n\Longrightarrow [m]_n\notin X_n\}.
$$

Erdős asked whether this set always has a *logarithmic density*:

$$
\delta_{\log}(B_>)=
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log x}
\sum_{\substack{m<x\\m\in B_>}}\frac1m.
$$

Unlike natural density, which counts integers equally, logarithmic density assigns integer $m$ the weight $1/m$. The maintained [Erdős Problems catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/486) lists this problem as **OPEN**.

**Source-fidelity warning.** Erdős's primary 1961 Problem I.26.1 imposes a forbidden congruence when $m\ge n$, whereas his 1980 printed retelling and the current catalogue say $m>n$. These define potentially different sets at infinitely many equality-boundary integers $m=n$. No equivalence between the universal statements has been established here. The elementary results below hold under either convention.

## A small example

Take $A=\{2,3\}$, $X_2=\{0\}$ and $X_3=\{1\}$. For all $m>3$ the allowed integers must be odd and must not equal $1$ modulo $3$. The only surviving residues modulo $6$ are $3$ and $5$, giving natural and logarithmic density

$$
\delta(B_>)=\frac26=\frac13.
$$

In the modern strict-threshold convention $2$ itself is allowed, since the modulus-two exclusion has not yet activated. In the original non-strict convention $2$ is excluded. The finite difference is immaterial here, but need not be finite for an infinite sequence of moduli.

As an infinite example, take $A=\{2,4,8,16,\ldots\}$ and $X_n=\{0\}$ for all $n\in A$. Every even $m>2$ is excluded by the modulus-two rule; every odd $m$ survives. Thus $B_>$ consists of the odd integers together with $2$, and has density $1/2$. Here the tail sum of excluded residue weights converges:

$$
\sum_{n\in A}\frac{|X_n|}{n}=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{2^k}=1.
$$

## What is known

### The classical special case

Erdős stated the problem in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed pp. 235–236, I.26.1–I.26.2, using $m\ge n$. He repeated it in *A survey of problems in combinatorial number theory* (1980), printed p. 114, using $m>n$. Both primary pages have been examined directly.

H. Davenport and P. Erdős proved that the logarithmic density exists for the **zero-residue case** $X_n=\{0\}$, for arbitrary moduli. Their 1936/1937 theorem was given an elementary presentation in 1951. The set of multiples itself need not have natural density, by Besicovitch's 1934 construction. The introduction of the 1951 primary article was checked, but the complete proof was not independently replayed. The single forbidden residue generalization is independently listed as open in [Erdős Problem #25](https://www.erdosproblems.com/25).

### A sufficient condition with a short proof

Put $c_n=|X_n|$. If

$$
\sum_{n\in A}\frac{c_n}{n}<\infty,
$$

then the allowed set $B_>$ has a **natural density**, hence also a logarithmic density. The same conclusion holds under $m\ge n$.

For a cutoff $N$ retain only $n\in A$ with $n\le N$, obtaining $B_N$. Apart from finitely many integers at or below $N$, membership is periodic with period

$$
L_N=\mathrm{lcm}\{n\in A:n\le N\},
$$

where the lcm of the empty set is $1$. Thus $B_N$ has an exact natural density $d_N$, equal to the fraction of permitted residues modulo $L_N$. Increasing $N$ only removes allowed integers, so $d_N$ decreases to some $d\ge0$.

For any forbidden residue modulo $n$, among integers $m\le x$ with $m>n$ there are at most $\lfloor x/n\rfloor$ belonging to that residue class. By the union bound, for the full set $B$ we obtain

$$
0\le |B_N\cap[1,x]|-|B\cap[1,x]|
\le xR_N,\qquad
R_N=\sum_{\substack{n\in A\\n>N}}\frac{c_n}{n}.
$$

Here the infinite sum is legitimate because only finitely many moduli can activate below $x$. Consequently,

$$
d_N-R_N\le\underline d(B)\le\overline d(B)\le d_N.
$$

As $N\to\infty$, summability implies $R_N\to0$, and both bounding quantities tend to $d$. Therefore $\underline d(B)=\overline d(B)=d$. The same residue-count bound works at the non-strict threshold $m\ge n$.

This is an **elementary sufficient condition**, not a solution to the full open problem. It gives no control when $\sum c_n/n$ diverges; notably, the Davenport–Erdős zero-residue theorem can handle certain divergent tails.
