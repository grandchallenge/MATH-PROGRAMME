## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 362 asks how often a finite set can repeat a subset sum. Let $A$ be a finite set of $N$ distinct natural numbers, let $t$ be a target, and count the subsets $S$ of $A$ whose elements add to $t$. There are $2^N$ subsets altogether, but the question asks for a uniform upper bound on the largest fibre of the sum map: how many different subsets can land on the same target?

The first assertion predicts the scale $2^N/N^{3/2}$, with a constant independent of both $A$ and $t$. The second assertion asks for the same kind of estimate after restricting to subsets of one prescribed size $l$, with the stronger scale $2^N/N^2$ and a constant independent of $l$ and $t$. These are concentration questions: the more spread out the available numbers are, the fewer subsets should share a sum, while an unusually structured set can create a large central fibre.

The original issue for this audit is [MATH-PROGRAMME #1632](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1632), assignment `ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0362-S01`.

## A small example

Take $A={1,2,3,4}$ and $t=5$. Exact enumeration gives

$$
\{S\subseteq A:\sum_{n\in S}n=5\}=\{\{1,4\},\{2,3\}\}.
$$

Thus the unrestricted fibre has size $2$. The two-element fibre is the same two sets, also of size $2$. A direct finite check, using only integer addition and enumeration, produced the list `[(1, 4), (2, 3)]` in both cases. Here $2^N/N^{3/2}=16/8=2$, while $2^N/N^2=16/16=1$; the example therefore illustrates why each theorem needs an unspecified absolute constant. It does not establish an asymptotic bound, uniformity in $A$, or the second assertion for every $l$ and $t$. It is a sanity check on the definitions and on the distinction between the two fibres, not a proof of the Erdős question.

## What is known

The primary paper by András Sárközy and Endre Szemerédi, [Über ein Problem von Erdős und Moser](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/acta-arithmetica/all/11/2/95839/uber-ein-problem-von-erdos-und-moser), Acta Arithmetica 11 (1965), 205–208, is available as the journal’s scanned three-page article. On page 205 it defines the number of representations of a real target by zero-one coefficients for ordered positive real numbers. It records the earlier Erdős–Moser estimate with an extra factor of $(\log N)^{3/2}$ and states their improvement: for every $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$, the maximum fibre is less than $(1+\varepsilon)8/\sqrt{\pi}$ times $2^N/N^{3/2}$. This is a direct primary resolution of the first question, stronger in its real-number formulation than the natural-number special case.

The maintained problem page attributes the fixed-cardinality assertion to Gábor Halász’s more general multidimensional result, [Estimates for the concentration function of combinatorial number theory and probability](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02018403), Periodica Mathematica Hungarica 8 (1977), 197–211. The bibliographic record and the problem page are accessible, but the primary Halász article itself was not readable within this timebox. The attribution and the claimed order are therefore reported as source evidence, not as an independently checked page-level reading here. Richard Stanley’s [1980 paper](https://math.mit.edu/~rstan/pubs/pubfiles/42.pdf), pages 168–184, is a further primary source for the extremal statement that the largest fibre is attained by the centred interval of consecutive integers (with the appropriate parity convention).

## What remains

The first classical proof is source-locked enough for a bounded documentary article: the scan, journal metadata, page location, and exact downloaded-paper SHA-256 `5e54210d944202a1903fa2d99ed8ec27be67a8f858f59ff95521dae6dd9260` are recorded in this audit. The remaining work is to read and compare Halász’s primary theorem, including its hypotheses and the precise dependence of constants on the fixed cardinality parameter. A successor formal review should also compare the current Lean quantifiers with the primary statement, decide whether allowing zero is harmless, and inspect the linked external Lean development at [commit `8822f7ddef30fadbd92e1c6ab4ed897af356af5e`](https://github.com/plby/lean-proofs/tree/8822f7ddef30fadbd92e1c6ab4ed897af356af5e) without treating that pointer as replay or certification.

The phrase “for any fixed” is also a quantifier issue, not merely an informal qualifier. The first constant is uniform in the set and target; the second must be tested for independence from the chosen cardinality. A future comparison should preserve that order of quantifiers and should not replace an absolute constant by one that secretly depends on $l$, $t$, or the particular set.
