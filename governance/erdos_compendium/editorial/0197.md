## The question in plain language

Can the **positive integers** be partitioned into **two disjoint subsets** $A$ and $B$ so that *each* subset admits an enumeration avoiding all monotone three-term arithmetic progressions? Each enumeration may have its own order. A monotone triple means three numbers $a,a+d,a+2d$ with $d\ne0$ that appear in increasing or decreasing numerical order within the chosen enumeration.

This is a simultaneous partition-and-ordering problem. It is not asking for one permutation of the whole of $\mathbb N$, which is known to force a monotone triple.

## A small example

The ordering $2,1,3$ of the set $\{1,2,3\}$ avoids a **monotone** three-term progression, even though the set contains $1,2,3$ as an arithmetic progression. In a partition problem, a simple way to avoid false conclusions is first to specify the two disjoint sets and then list the *independent orders* of the elements in each part.

Finite experiments are easy to satisfy and can be misleading. For every finite interval there are permutations avoiding monotone three-term progressions, but they need not form compatible initial segments of infinite permutations.

## What is known

The two-set partition conjecture remains **OPEN**. The original Erdős–Graham formulation and [maintained #197](https://www.erdosproblems.com/197) state that **three** subsets suffice. Hence the issue is the exact number of parts: whether three can be improved to two. Davis, Entringer, Graham and Simmons (1977) showed that the **whole** positive integers cannot be permuted to avoid monotone three-term arithmetic progressions.

Sarosh Adenwalla's [*Avoiding monotone arithmetic progressions in permutations of integers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04451), published in *Discrete Mathematics* (2024), studies related permutation and density restrictions, and explicitly identifies this partition question as unresolved. His constructions for avoiding **four**- or **five**-term progressions do not resolve the two-part avoidance of **three**-term progressions.

A permutation of $\mathbb Z$ or the rationals need not restrict to a partition of $\mathbb N$ with the exact two-set condition. No full construction or obstruction for two sets is established by the sources inspected.

## What remains

A bounded successor uses constraint programming to partition $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ into two parts and find an ordering of each avoiding monotone three-term progressions. Acceptance: a certificate includes the partition, two exact permutations and an exhaustive triple test; every element appears exactly once. Compare with a three-part fixture. Stop at finite $N$; even success for large finite instances does not certify an infinite partition.
