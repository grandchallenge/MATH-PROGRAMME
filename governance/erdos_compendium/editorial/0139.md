## The question in plain language

For fixed $k\ge3$, let $r_k(N)$ be the largest possible size of a subset of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ containing no nontrivial $k$ term arithmetic progression. A nontrivial progression has a **nonzero** common difference. Erdős and Turán asked whether

$$
r_k(N)=o(N),\quad\text{equivalently}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{r_k(N)}N=0.
$$

The limit is for each fixed $k$, not one claim about a growing progression length.

## A small example

The three integers $1,2,3$ form a nontrivial arithmetic progression, so they cannot all belong to a set counted by $r_3(3)$. The set $\{1,3,4\}$ contains no nontrivial three-term progression and has size three when considered inside $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Finite examples show the definition, not the asymptotic density limit.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Endre Szemerédi's 1975 theorem proves that a subset of the positive integers with positive upper density contains arithmetic progressions of every prescribed finite length. Taking its contrapositive yields the stated limit. Quantitative refinements have different scopes: Kelley–Meka and subsequent work give strong bounds for $k=3$; Green–Tao treat $k=4$; Leng–Sah–Sawhney address $k\ge5$. These advances must not be conflated with an exact asymptotic formula for $r_k(N)$, which remains open in Erdős #142.

## What remains

Write a finite exact solver for $r_3(N)$, $N\le16$. Acceptance: test every subset or provide a transparent integer-programming certificate, prohibit zero common difference, and compare computed values with independently enumerated witnesses. Do not claim that these finite values establish the density-zero theorem or the still-open problem of exact asymptotics.
