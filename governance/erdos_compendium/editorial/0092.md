## The question in plain language

Given a finite set $A$ of $n$ distinct points in the Euclidean plane, each point $x$ may have many other points at the same distance from it. That distance may **depend on $x$**.

Define $f(n)$ as the largest integer for which there is an $n$ point configuration where **every** point $x$ has at least $f(n)$ neighbours on some circle centered at $x$. Erdős #92 conjectured

$$
f(n)\le n^{o(1)}.
$$

The stronger formulation asked for an exponent of order $1/\log\log n$. This is not an ordinary unit-distance count: the permitted radii can vary with the point.

## A small example

For the four vertices of a unit square, each vertex has two neighbours at distance one and one at distance $\sqrt2$. Thus this particular configuration gives $f(4)\ge2$. A complete classification of all four-point configurations is not necessary for that lower bound.

The local requirement that *every vertex* have many equidistant neighbours is stronger than merely having many such pairs on average, unless one passes to a dense subconfiguration.

## What is known

**DISPROVED.** The May 2026 construction disproving the planar unit-distance conjecture in [Erdős #90](https://www.erdosproblems.com/90) implies this result. The [original OpenAI publication](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/) links a mathematical proof of planar point sets with at least $n^{1+\delta}$ unit-distance pairs for infinitely many $n$ and some absolute $\delta>0$.

Here is the exact elementary reduction. On such a point set, make the unit-distance graph $G$; its average degree is at least $2n^\delta$. Every graph with average degree $d$ contains a nonempty subgraph with minimum degree at least $d/2$: repeatedly delete any vertex whose degree is below $d/2$, and note that deleting them all would remove fewer than the total $dn/2$ original edges. Therefore a subset $A'$ of at most $n$ points has minimum unit-distance degree at least $n^\delta$. With $m=|A'|$, we obtain $f(m)\ge n^\delta\ge m^\delta$. These $m$ tend to infinity, so this contradicts a universal subpolynomial upper bound.

Unlike #90, the source for #92 does **not** require a different radius at every vertex; the counterexample uses the same unit radius throughout.
