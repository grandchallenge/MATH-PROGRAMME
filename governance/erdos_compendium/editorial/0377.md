## The question in plain language

Let $B_n = \binom{2n}{n}$ be the central binomial coefficient. For each positive integer $n$, form the sum

$$
f(n)=\sum_{\substack{p\leq n\\p\ \mathrm{prime}\\p\nmid B_n}}\frac{1}{p}.
$$

The question is whether there is one absolute constant $C>0$ such that $f(n)\leq C$ for every $n$. The primes dividing $B_n$ are deliberately omitted: the question asks whether the reciprocal mass of the primes up to $n$ that escape the central binomial coefficient stays bounded as $n$ grows. This is the exact scope of [original issue #1647](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1647), not a question about the size of $B_n$ itself or about its largest prime factor.

The quantifiers matter. The same constant must work for all positive integers, with no exceptional set and no “sufficiently large” qualification. A statement that $f(n)=O(\log\log n)$, or that $f(n)$ is close to a constant for almost all $n$, is informative but does not settle the uniform boundedness question.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check at $n=10$:

$$
\binom{20}{10}=184756.
$$

The primes at most $10$ are $2,3,5,7$. Direct integer divisibility gives $2\mid184756$, while $3$, $5$, and $7$ do not divide it. Therefore

$$
f(10)=\frac13+\frac15+\frac17=\frac{71}{105}.
$$

This was verified with integer arithmetic by computing `comb(20, 10)`, testing each of the four primes by remainder, and summing with exact rational fractions; the checks returned `binom(2*10,10) = 184756`, the excluded set `[2]`, the included set `[3, 5, 7]`, `f(10) = 71/105`, and `exact checks: PASS`. The example makes the definition concrete and is reproducible without floating-point decisions. It proves nothing about a bound valid for every $n$: a finite table can neither certify an infinite quantifier nor exclude a later value of $f(n)$.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős, R. L. Graham, I. Z. Ruzsa, and E. G. Straus, “On the Prime Factors of $\binom{2n}{n}$,” *Mathematics of Computation* 29 (1975), 83–92, [the scanned paper](https://renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1975-27.pdf). Its introduction on printed page 83 explicitly says that the authors could not decide whether this function is unbounded. That is the historical statement of the open problem, not evidence that the requested constant exists.

The same paper proves substantial average information. Its Theorem 2 (printed page 86) gives

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x} f(n)=\gamma_0,
\qquad
\gamma_0=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\log k}{2^k},
$$

and Theorem 3 (printed page 87) gives the corresponding second-moment limit with value $\gamma_0^2$. Their corollary then yields $f(n)=\gamma_0+o(1)$ for almost all integers, in the density sense. These are external published results, but their “almost all” quantifier leaves room for exceptional integers.

Later on printed page 90, in the discussion surrounding equation (7), the authors record an upper bound of the form $f(n)\leq c\log\log n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, with some $c<1$. This improves the trivial Mertens-scale comparison but is still not a constant bound. The live problem page also records that a positive answer would force the reciprocal mass of the primes dividing $B_n$ to be $(1-o(1))\log\log n$; that implication is useful context, not a proof of either direction.

## What remains

The smallest missing mathematical ingredient is a uniform argument controlling the exceptional integers left open by the average theorems. A bounded successor task should first fix an input cutoff $N$ and compute every $f(n)$ for $1\leq n\leq N$ exactly. It can avoid enormous binomial integers by using the valuation identity

$$
v_p\!\left(\binom{2n}{n}\right)=\sum_{j\geq1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{p^j}\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor\right),
$$

so that $p$ is omitted precisely when this valuation is positive. A verifier should receive $N$, the prime list, each valuation or carry certificate, and the exact rational maximum and its witnesses. Acceptance is a replay that every listed prime and divisibility decision is correct; falsification is any missed prime, incorrect valuation, or new $n\leq N$ exceeding the reported maximum. Stop at the declared cutoff or resource limit. Even a successful finite certificate is evidence for a range, not a proof of the global conjecture.
