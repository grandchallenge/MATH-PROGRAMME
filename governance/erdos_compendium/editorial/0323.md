## The question in plain language

For integers $k$ and $m$, let $f_{k,m}(x)$ count the integers not exceeding $x$ that can be written as a sum of $m$ nonnegative powers of degree $k$. The main question asks whether, for every $k\geq 1$ and every $\epsilon>0$, the case of $k$ summands satisfies

$$
f_{k,k}(x) \gg_\epsilon x^{1-\epsilon}.
$$

In words, does allowing exactly $k$ powers of degree $k$ produce almost as many represented integers as the interval up to $x$, apart from an arbitrarily small power loss? The companion question asks whether, whenever $1\leq m<k$, one has

$$
f_{k,m}(x) \gg x^{m/k}
$$

for all sufficiently large $x$. Here the implied constants may depend on the fixed parameters, and the functions count distinct represented integers, not representations with multiplicity. Zero is allowed as a summand, so the natural-number convention includes zero among the represented values.

The original [Erdős Problem 323 issue](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1593) asks for source locking and a bounded research design. It does not authorize a claim that either open estimate has been proved.

## A small example

Take $k=m=3$ and $x=100$. The available cubes are $0,1,8,27,64$. Exact enumeration of all triples, retaining sums at most $100$, gives

$$
\{0,1,2,3,8,9,10,16,17,24,27,28,29,35,36,43,54,55,62,64,65,66,72,73,80,81,91,92,99\}.
$$

Thus $f_{3,3}(100)=29$. This is reproducible with integer arithmetic:

```python
from itertools import product
p = [a**3 for a in range(5)]
direct = sorted({sum(t) for t in product(p, repeat=3) if sum(t) <= 100})
pairs = {a + b for a in p for b in p if a + b <= 100}
recombined = sorted({s + c for s in pairs for c in p if s + c <= 100})
assert direct == recombined
assert len(direct) == 29
print(direct)
```

The second construction independently forms pair sums before adjoining the third cube, so the assertions check both the count and the set identity. This finite calculation illustrates collisions between representations and the meaning of “distinct integers.” It proves no asymptotic lower bound: one value of $x$ cannot establish behavior as $x$ tends to infinity, and the case $k=3$ does not settle any other exponent or number of summands.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, Monographies de l’Enseignement Mathématique 28 (1980), section 5, printed p. 45. The scan states the definition of $f_{k,l}(x)$, gives the two lower-bound questions, says the first inequality appears unattackable by the methods then available, and records Landau’s result

$$
f_{2,2}(x)\sim \frac{cx}{\sqrt{\log x}}
$$

for some $c>0$. This is an external historical statement in the source, not a new proof supplied here. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/323) repeats that $k=2$ result and says that for $k>2$ it is not known whether $f_{k,k}(x)=o(x)$. Its visible discussion includes recent numerical reports for $k=m=3$; those posts explicitly provide numerical evidence rather than a proof and are not used as theorem evidence.

The pinned catalogue record at [teorth/erdosproblems commit 5364087](https://github.com/teorth/erdosproblems/blob/536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89/data/problems.yaml) identifies number theory and powers, no prize, informal status open with last update 2025-08-31, formal status unformalized, and statement-formalized metadata yes (last update 2026-03-15). ## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a fixed-parameter source-and-lemma audit for $(k,m)=(3,3)$, followed by a narrowly stated finite certificate. Its input should be the primary definition, the exact formal definition, and an independently reproduced table of $f_{3,3}(10^r)$ over a declared finite range. Its target should be either a proved elementary bound valid on that range, or a rigorously verified obstruction to a proposed stronger finite claim. Acceptance should require exact integer code, a second implementation or independently checkable certificate, and explicit agreement on whether zero is counted. Falsification is any mismatching value, omitted summand, or claimed asymptotic inference from finite data. Stop when the declared range and cross-check are complete; do not infer positive density or solve the open problem.
