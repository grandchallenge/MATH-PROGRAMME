## The question in plain language

List the primes as $p_1=2<p_2=3<p_3=5<\cdots$. For a fixed integer $k\ge2$, let

$$
P_k=p_1p_2\cdots p_k.
$$

[Erdős Problem #891](https://www.erdosproblems.com/891) asks whether, for every sufficiently large starting integer $n$, the $P_k$ consecutive numbers

$$
n,n+1,\ldots,n+P_k-1
$$

must contain at least one integer with **more than $k$ distinct prime factors**. This is stronger than merely requiring $k$ prime factors, and multiplicities of repeated powers do not count.

The smallest unresolved instance is $k=2$: must every sufficiently late block of **six** consecutive integers contain a number with at least **three** distinct prime factors?

## A small example

The six numbers

$$
4372,4373,4374,4375,4376,4377
$$

have distinct prime supports $\{2,1093\}$, $\{4373\}$, $\{2,3\}$, $\{5,7\}$, $\{2,547\}$, and $\{3,1459\}$ respectively. Every number has at most two distinct prime factors. Thus the asserted six-number property does **not** hold for all starting integers; the conjecture explicitly permits finitely many exceptions.

An independent exact sieve scanned all $1\le n\le100000$ for $k=2,3,4$. Among this finite set of starting points, the last failing starts were **4,372** for length 6, **8,615** for length 30, and **50,841** for length 210. A finite last observed failure is **not** proof of a last failure globally.

## What is known

Erdős and Selfridge, [*Some problems on the prime factors of consecutive integers*](https://combinatorica.hu/~p_erdos/1967-21.pdf), *Illinois Journal of Mathematics* **11** (1967), pp.428–430, cite A. Schinzel's consequence of Pólya's theorem on unbounded gaps between integers with only small prime factors. For fixed $k$, Schinzel's argument forces an integer with more than $k$ distinct prime factors in every sufficiently late interval of length

$$
P'_k=p_1\cdots p_{k-1}p_{k+1},
$$

which is **longer** than the proposed $P_k$. Erdős and Selfridge say that even the case $k=2$ could not be improved from Schinzel's interval length to the conjectured six.

The original source for Schinzel's result is [*Unsolved Problem 31*, *Elemente der Mathematik* **14** (1959), pp.82–83](https://www.erdosproblems.com/891), which reports a ten-integer result for three distinct prime factors in the case $k=2$. The maintained catalogue keeps #891 **OPEN**.

An additional source distinction is conditional: the [modern discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/891) explains that, assuming **Dickson's prime-tuples conjecture**, reducing the interval length from $P_k$ to $P_k-1$ would fail infinitely often. This does not disprove the original $P_k$ conjecture, nor does the conditional argument hold unconditionally.

## GCL's current state

There is a short exact prime-factor-count lemma underlying the conditional sharpness claim. No positive integer **strictly smaller than** $P_k$ can have $k$ distinct prime factors, because the smallest product of $k$ distinct primes is exactly $P_k$. Hence every $m<P_k$ has at most $k-1$ distinct prime factors.

Let $L$ be a multiple of all positive integers below $P_k$. For each $1\le m<P_k$, the number

$$
Lq+m=m\left(\frac Lm q+1\right)
$$

has at most $k$ distinct prime divisors **if** the displayed linear factor is prime. Dickson's conjecture can be applied only after checking admissibility of the complete family of linear forms. Under that conditional simultaneous-primality hypothesis, the first $P_k-1$ members of the block starting at $Lq+1$ have at most $k$ prime factors each. This explains why the claimed interval length is expected to be sharp, but it is not an unconditional theorem about the conjectured $P_k$ windows.

The reproducible program governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_891.py computes the number of **distinct** prime factors of all numbers in each tested interval using a smallest-prime-factor sieve. It compares the actual maximum with $k+1$ for $k=2,3,4$, records failing starts and initial witnesses, and checks the elementary primorial lemma for small values through $k=5$. The scan is exact on its declared finite range only.

## What remains

A bounded follow-up is a **source locked finite interval and primorial certificate**. Inputs are integer $k\ge2$, the first $k+1$ primes, exact distinct-prime-factor function, and a finite start interval. Prove that every positive $m<P_k$ has fewer than $k$ distinct prime factors, formalize the precise length $P_k$ predicate, and replay the finite scan without heuristics.

Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or a pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem with literal axiom report, independently checked sample factorizations for the interval starting at 4,372, and matching failure counts for all three tested lengths. A composite incorrectly treated as prime, a repeated prime factor counted more than once, or an incorrect half-open endpoint invalidates the finite certificate.

To resolve the actual open problem, one must prove an **eventual uniform** interval statement for every fixed $k$, starting with six consecutive numbers. The finite check and conditional Dickson sharpness observation are not substitutes for that asymptotic proof.
