## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #336 studies how far two notions of “additive basis” can separate. An infinite set $A$ of nonnegative integers is a basis of order $r$ if every sufficiently large integer is a sum of at most $r$ members of $A$, with repetition allowed, and $r$ is the least such number. Its exact order is the least $k$ for which every sufficiently large integer is a sum of exactly $k$ members of $A$. The distinction matters because adding a fixed number of summands is not automatically possible when the set has no suitable residue structure.

Let $h(r)$ be the largest finite exact order attained by a basis of order $r$. The problem asks for the value of

$$
\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{h(r)}{r^2}.
$$

The word “sufficiently” is an eventual quantifier: finitely many small exceptions do not matter. The original Erdős–Graham paper writes the corresponding extremal function as $g(r)$ and works with nonnegative integers; the maintained page phrases the set as $A\subseteq\mathbb N$. That ambient-set convention should be retained explicitly in any future formal statement, rather than silently treating the two wordings as identical.

## A small example

The maintained entry gives the lacunary set

$$
A=\bigcup_{j\geq 0}(2^{2j},2^{2j+1}],
$$

as an example whose ordinary order is $2$ while its exact order is $3$. This is an infinite asymptotic claim, not something a finite prefix can prove. Nevertheless, its first two nonempty blocks already make the mechanism visible. Taking $A_0=\{2,5,6,7,8\}$, exact enumeration gives every integer from $10$ through $16$ as a sum of two members and also as a sum of three members:

$$
\begin{aligned}
10&=5+5=2+2+6, & 11&=5+6=2+2+7,\\
12&=5+7=2+2+8, & 13&=5+8=2+5+6,\\
14&=6+8=2+5+7, & 15&=7+8=2+5+8,\\
16&=8+8=5+5+6.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same finite set has sorted consecutive gaps $1,2,1$, whose gcd is $1$. This is a reproducible exact-arithmetic sanity check of the local pattern and of the gap calculation. It does not establish that all sufficiently large integers have either representation, nor does it determine $h(2)$ or the limiting constant. The asymptotic construction and its proof require the infinite set and the source theorem.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, “On bases with an exact order,” *Acta Arithmetica* 37 (1980), 201–207. Its Theorem 1, on p. 202, proves that a basis $A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots\}$ has an exact order if and only if

$$
\gcd\{a_{i+1}-a_i:i\geq 1\}=1.
$$

The hypothesis is about all consecutive gaps of the infinite sorted set, not merely a finite sample. Their Theorem 2, on pp. 203–206, bounds the extremal function at quadratic scale, with asymptotic constants $1/4$ and $5/4$. In the concluding remarks on p. 206 they ask whether the limit exists and, if it does, what its value is. Thus this primary paper is evidence for the definition, the exact-order criterion, and the original bounds; it is not a solution of the limit problem.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #336 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/336), last edited there on 28 October 2025, reports later bounds $1/3\leq\lim h(r)/r^2\leq 1/2$, attributing the lower bound to Georges Grekos and the upper bound to John C. M. Nash, and reports $h(2)=4$, $h(3)=7$, and $10\leq h(4)\leq 11$. Those are useful literature pointers. In this bounded audit I directly read Erdős–Graham, but did not independently inspect the Grekos, Nash, or Plagne proofs; those page-level attributions remain reported external evidence here, not a new verification.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-and-interface task: freeze the exact natural-number convention, define ordinary and exact order with explicit eventual quantifiers and repetition, and reconcile the notation $h(r)$ with Erdős–Graham’s $g(r)$. Then obtain and inspect the cited Grekos, Nash, and Plagne primary documents, recording theorem or page locations for each later bound. A bounded computational companion can enumerate finite prefixes of candidate bases, verify gap gcds, and test representation intervals, but must label every result finite and non-asymptotic.

The central mathematical remainder is still the limit question: either prove existence and identify the constant, or produce a rigorously sourced obstruction to the asserted limit. Neither the finite example, the catalogue label, the live page, nor a future formal statement would by itself supply that proof.
