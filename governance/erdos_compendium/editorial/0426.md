## The question in plain language

A graph can contain another graph as a subgraph in many different ways. Erdős Problem 426 studies the unusually restrictive situation in which a particular graph type occurs **exactly once**. The subgraphs in this problem need not be induced: extra edges of the larger graph may be omitted when selecting a copy of the smaller one.

For a graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, let $U(G)$ count the different **isomorphism types** that occur exactly once as subgraphs of $G$, and let $M(n)$ be the largest possible $U(G)$ among all $n$-vertex graphs. The problem asked whether this maximum could be a positive constant fraction of the approximate total number of nonisomorphic graphs on $n$ vertices:

$$
\frac{2^{\binom n2}}{n!}.
$$

This normalization arises because there are $2^{\binom n2}$ labelled simple graphs, and almost all large graphs have no nontrivial automorphisms, so dividing by $n!$ approximates the number of isomorphism classes. The question is whether one host graph can contain a constant proportion of these types **uniquely**, not merely contain them at all.

## A small example

Take the triangle $K_3$, the complete graph on three vertices. It contains a subgraph isomorphic to the single-edge graph $K_2$ in three ways, one for each edge. Therefore $K_2$ is **not** a unique subgraph type of $K_3$. The triangle $K_3$ itself occurs only once as a subgraph, namely using all its vertices and all three edges, so it is a unique type.

This example distinguishes a mathematical occurrence from an isomorphism type. There is one isomorphism type called $K_2$, but the host has three copies. The number of types occurring once must not count the three copies as three unique types. Likewise, choosing only induced subgraphs changes the counting problem: a three-vertex path is not an induced subgraph of $K_3$, but can be obtained as a noninduced spanning subgraph by omitting one edge.

These small statements can be checked by listing the three vertices and their possible edge subsets. They illustrate the definition but say nothing about the asymptotic count for large $n$.

## What is known

The problem originated with Erdős and Entringer, *On the number of unique subgraphs of a graph* (1972), and was discussed by Erdős in *Problems and results in graph theory and combinatorial analysis*, Proceedings of the Fifth British Combinatorial Conference (1976), pp. 169–192. Successive constructions by Entringer and Erdős, Harary and Schwenk, and Brouwer produced many unique subgraph types but did not reach a constant fraction of the full graph-isomorphism scale.

The final negative solution is Domagoj Bradač and Micha Christoph, [*Unique subgraphs are rare*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16233), first submitted in October 2024. They prove that

$$
M(n)=o\left(\frac{2^{\binom n2}}{n!}\right).
$$

In words, even the best $n$-vertex host graph contains a proportion of uniquely occurring graph types that tends to zero. This answers the original question **no**. The maintained [Erdős Problems #426](https://www.erdosproblems.com/426) entry marks the result **DISPROVED (LEAN)** and reports a quantitative refinement of order $\log\log\log n/\log\log n$ for the normalized maximum. Such a refinement changes the rate of decay, not the basic negative answer.

Erdős had offered $100 for a construction achieving the desired positive fraction and $25 for a disproof. This historical context is not a claim that GCL earned or administers an award. The published mathematical resolution is attributed to the named researchers and must remain separate from any GCL formal verification or editorial disposition.
