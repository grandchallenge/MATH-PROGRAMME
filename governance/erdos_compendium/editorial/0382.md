## The question in plain language

Take a consecutive block of positive integers from $u$ through $v$, and multiply the block. Let $p$ be the largest prime that divides this product. The block is “bad” when $p$ occurs at least twice in its prime factorization, equivalently when $p^2$ divides the product. Erdős Problem 382 asks two questions about the length $v-u$ of such a block:

$$
\text{Must }v-u=v^{o(1)}\text{ as }v\to\infty?\qquad\text{Can }v-u\text{ be arbitrarily large?}
$$

Here $v^{o(1)}$ means that, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently large relevant endpoints satisfy $v-u\leq v^\varepsilon$. The natural normalization used by the current formal statement is $1\leq u\leq v$ and $v\geq2$. Writing $P(u,v)=\prod_{m=u}^{v}m$, the hypothesis is that the largest prime divisor $P^+(P(u,v))$ satisfies

$$
\bigl(P^+(P(u,v))\bigr)^2\mid P(u,v).
$$

The first question is an asymptotic upper bound on every bad interval. The second is an existence statement: for every $k$, is there a bad interval with $v-u\geq k$? These are different quantifier patterns; evidence for one does not settle the other.

## A small example

The smallest useful worked witness in the formal source is $u=48$, $v=50$. Exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
P(48,50)=48\cdot49\cdot50=117600=2^5\cdot3\cdot5^2\cdot7^2.
$$

Thus the largest prime divisor is $7$, its exponent is $2$, and $7^2$ divides the product. The interval has width $v-u=2$. This calculation was checked directly by factoring the integer $117600$ and testing the remainder modulo $49$; the remainder is $0$.

This is a finite certificate that the hypothesis is nonempty, not evidence for either asymptotic answer. A finite search can find witnesses or disprove a proposed small bound, but cannot prove a statement quantified over all sufficiently large $v$ or over every $k$. The maintained discussion also records larger numerical examples supplied by Stijn Cambie, including a width of $13$; those reports are useful leads, but the present article treats the exact $(48,50)$ calculation as the verified finite example.

## What is known

The accessible primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), whose scan is linked below. On printed p. 73 they define an integer as “bad” when it belongs to an interval $u\leq n\leq v$ for which the largest prime factor of $\prod_{i=0}^{v-u}(u+i)$ occurs with exponent greater than $1$. They then say that results of K. Ramachandra imply that the interval length is at most $v^{1/2-\varepsilon}$ for some positive constant $\varepsilon$. The same page says it is expected to be bounded by $v^\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, and that it can be arbitrarily large, but states that the latter assertion had not been proved.

This primary page is stronger than the maintained page’s current summary, which reports the weaker-looking bound $v-u\leq v^{1/2+o(1)}$. The discrepancy is preserved as a source wording issue: it does not change the fact that both the $v^{o(1)}$ assertion and the arbitrary-width assertion are presented as open. Ramachandra’s cited paper is K. Ramachandra, “A Note on Numbers with a Large Prime Factor,” *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* (2) 1 (1969), 303–306, DOI [10.1112/jlms/s2-1.1.303](https://doi.org/10.1112/jlms/s2-1.1.303). Its bibliographic record was verified, but the publisher PDF returned HTTP 403 in this audit, so Ramachandra’s proof and exact constants were not independently read.

The maintained page also explains a conditional route through prime gaps: a sufficiently strong short-interval prime statement would force a bad interval to be short. That is a conditional reduction or heuristic, not an unconditional solution. Likewise, the page’s positive-probability discussion near squares of primes is a heuristic for large widths, not a proof of the second question.
