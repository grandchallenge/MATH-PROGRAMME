## The question in plain language

Fix an integer $n\ge 1$. Start with $a_0=0$ and $a_1=n$. Having chosen $a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_k$, choose $a_{k+1}$ to be the least integer strictly larger than $a_k$ for which the enlarged set contains no three-term arithmetic progression. Thus the normalized sequence is

$$
a_{k+1}=\min\{m\in\mathbb Z:m>a_k,\ \{a_0,\ldots,a_k,m\}\text{ contains no }x<y<z\text{ with }x+z=2y\}.
$$

The question asks for an explicit description of $a_k$ and for its growth as $k$ tends to infinity. The quantifiers matter: the construction is a separate sequence for each fixed initial value $n$, and the choice at every stage is the least admissible integer after the current maximum. It is not a choice from all positive integers, and it is not a question about merely finding some infinite progression-free set.

There is a material normalization exception. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry for 271](https://www.erdosproblems.com/271) says “least positive integer” but omits $m>a_k$. Literally using that wording after $0,4$ would choose $1$, because the set containing $0$, $1$, and $4$ has no three-term progression; that contradicts the required ordering. The original printed statement and Odlyzko–Stanley’s formulation both include the increasing condition, so this article uses the increasing version and preserves the live-page conflict rather than silently repairing it.

## A small example

For $n=4$, the beginning is

$$
A(4)=[0,4,5,7,11,12,16,23,26,31,33,37,38,44,49,56].
$$

The finite rule is easy to check exactly. Once $m$ is larger than every selected term, adding $m$ creates a progression precisely when two earlier terms $x<y$ satisfy $x+m=2y$. After $0,4,5,7$ have been selected, $8$ is rejected because $0+8=2\cdot4$, $9$ is rejected because $5+9=2\cdot7$, and $10$ is rejected in two ways, including $4+10=2\cdot7$. Hence $11$ is the next term.

The following integer-only calculation independently reproduces the displayed prefix; Python’s integers perform the equalities without rounding or probabilistic tests.

```python
from itertools import combinations

def greedy(n, count):
    A = [0, n]
    while len(A) < count:
        m = A[-1] + 1
        while any(x + m == 2*y for x, y in combinations(A, 2)):
            m += 1
        A.append(m)
    return A

expected = [0, 4, 5, 7, 11, 12, 16, 23,
            26, 31, 33, 37, 38, 44, 49, 56]
assert greedy(4, 16) == expected
print(greedy(4, 16))
```

This is a finite verification of the recurrence, not evidence for a proposed asymptotic law. The same prefix appears in the inspected Odlyzko–Stanley paper, p. 1, and in the original Erdős–Graham discussion.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham’s 1980 source places the problem on printed p. 22 and records the increasing greedy construction, including the special behavior for $n=1$ and the difficulty of the $n=4$ case. Odlyzko and Stanley, pp. 1–2, give exact ternary membership descriptions for $n=3^r$ and $n=2\cdot3^r$. For $n=3^r$, their Theorem 1 characterizes membership by restrictions on ternary digits; Theorem 2 gives the analogous characterization for $2\cdot3^r$. Their regular-case calculation yields the exponent $\log 3/\log 2$ in the corresponding growth comparison.

For other initial values, Odlyzko–Stanley describe the behavior as irregular and use a probabilistic heuristic. On p. 3 they motivate a scale of the form $a_k$ comparable to $k^2/\log k$ for irregular cases. That is an empirical model, not a theorem. The maintained entry reports a related dichotomy and reports data for $A(4)$, but the entry’s comments are not a substitute for a primary proof; the source audit specifically records the Lindhurst thesis as unavailable for direct inspection.

A proved general bound comes from Richard A. Moy, “On the Growth of the Counting Function of Stanley Sequences.” If $A$ is any finite 3-free subset of the nonnegative integers and $S(A,x)$ counts its greedy Stanley sequence up to $x$, Theorem 1.1, p. 1, states that for every $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
S(A,x)\ge (\sqrt{2}-\varepsilon)\sqrt{x}
$$

for all sufficiently large $x$. Applying this to $\{0,n\}$ gives a quadratic-order upper bound on the inverse sequence: since $a_k\le x$ exactly when $S(A,x)\ge k+1$, one obtains $a_k=O_n(k^2)$. This does not prove the explicit bound displayed on the maintained page, and it does not select between the regular and irregular asymptotic pictures.

## What remains

The first unresolved obligation is semantic, not computational: the source packet must continue to distinguish the increasing recurrence from the live page’s incomplete wording. Any future proof or counterexample must state its indexing convention, its initial set, and the exact meaning of “least.” A result for a non-increasing greedy rule would not settle this problem.

The second obligation is mathematical. The explicit quadratic bound reported by the maintained entry and by unreviewed comments is

$$
a_k\le \frac{(k-1)(k+2)}{2}+n\qquad(k\ge 0).
$$

It needs a primary proof with the zero-based conversion checked line by line. Moy’s Theorem 1.1 supplies the asymptotic counting lower bound, but the source audit correctly treats this stronger explicit formula as a proposed target rather than an accepted theorem. Lindhurst’s numerical evidence also remains unavailable for direct verification.

The third obligation is status review. The exact Formal Conjectures absence means there is no protected theorem statement to replay.
