## The question in plain language

Can a set of points avoid every isosceles triangle without producing an enormously large variety of distances? If $n$ distinct points lie in the plane and **every three points have three different pairwise distances**, Erdős Problem [#657](https://www.erdosproblems.com/657) asks whether the complete set must determine *more than a constant times $n$* different distances, by a factor that increases without bound as $n$ grows.

Write

$$
D(X)=\bigl|\{\lVert x-y\rVert:x,y\in X,\ x\ne y\}\bigr|,
\qquad
F(n)=\min_{\substack{X\subset\mathbb R^2,\ |X|=n\\
\text{every triple is scalene}}}D(X).
$$

The conjecture is that $F(n)/n\to\infty$. Equivalently, **for every** fixed $M>0$, every sufficiently large admissible planar configuration must have at least $Mn$ distinct distances. Constructing just *one* family with many distances would not answer that universal question.

The maintained catalogue still lists the two-dimensional question as **OPEN**.

## A small example

Consider the four corners of a nonsquare rectangle,

$$
X=\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,1),(2,1)\}.
$$

Any triangle formed by three of these corners has side lengths $1$, $2$ and $\sqrt5$. It is therefore scalene, as required. The entire set has only these **three** distinct distances, even though it contains six unordered pairs of points:

$$
D(X)=3=|X|-1.
$$

Why is that possible? Every repeated distance belongs to **two disjoint edges**: the two vertical sides, the two horizontal sides or the two diagonals. Reusing a length at two edges meeting at a vertex would instead create an isosceles triangle.

A similar three-point fixture is any scalene triangle, which has three distances. Such examples illustrate what the geometry permits at small $n$, but they do not produce arbitrarily large planar configurations with $D(X)=O(n)$.

## What is known

**A useful exact linear bound.** Regard the $n$ points as vertices of the complete graph $K_n$, and label each edge by its geometric length. The absence of isosceles triangles means that edges with the same label cannot meet at a vertex. Thus every collection of equal-length edges forms a *matching*: a collection of disjoint pairs.

A matching in $K_n$ has at most $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ edges. Since $K_n$ has $\binom n2$ edges and each of the $D(X)$ distances accounts for at most one matching,

$$
\binom n2\leq D(X)\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor.
$$

Consequently,

$$
D(X)\geq
\begin{cases}
n,&n\ \text{odd},\\
n-1,&n\ \text{even}.
\end{cases}
$$

The rectangle realizes the even $n$ equality at $n=4$. This elementary graph argument also appears in the maintained [#657 public discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/657). It is **not** a new GCL theorem. Crucially, an $n-1$ lower bound is still only linear; it supplies no factor tending to infinity.

**Why a one-dimensional analogy matters.** For points on the real line, take any ordered triple $a<b<c$. It makes an isosceles triangle in the distance sense exactly when

$$
b-a=c-b,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad a+c=2b.
$$

Thus line configurations without equal incident distances are the familiar **three-term arithmetic-progression-free sets**. This links the one-dimensional problem to additive combinatorics.

Adrian Dumitrescu studied related midpoint-free and $\lambda$ free point sets in [*On distinct distances and $\lambda$ free point sets*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disc.2007.11.046), *Discrete Mathematics* **308** (2008), 6533–6538. The paper's publisher abstract explicitly addresses one-dimensional estimates and related geometry. The maintained Erdős entry summarizes subsequent progress on that one-dimensional question, including results using Bloom–Sisask and Kelley–Meka bounds for three-term progressions. **None of those cited one-dimensional inequalities is claimed here as a theorem for arbitrary planar sets.**

Erdős originally discussed the problem with Davies (in [Er73]) and later mentioned investigations with Füredi, Ruzsa and Pach (in [Er97e]), as reported by the maintained catalogue. Those original historical passages were not independently inspected in this audit.

**Dimension matters.** There are constructions in Euclidean spaces of dimension increasing with $n$ that avoid isosceles triples with relatively few distances. Their existence does not disprove an assertion about the *fixed two-dimensional plane*. A proof or counterexample must keep the ambient dimension exactly two.

The claim boundary is important. We have not discovered a new planar lower bound, and the conjecture remains open on the evidence inspected. This is a substantive explanatory **unaccepted draft**, not a mathematical certificate, protected publication or editorial admission.

## What remains

To improve the known elementary argument, one must show that disjoint pairs of edges of the *same* length cannot be arranged as freely in the Euclidean plane as the matching bound allows in an abstract complete graph. In other words, the missing ingredient concerns the **geometric compatibility between different matching color classes**, not the elementary fact that each class is a matching.

A bounded research experiment could enumerate small configurations using **exact squared distances**, counting how often disjoint edge pairs may reuse a length without any vertex seeing equal lengths. It would need an explicit stopping rule and a rigorously stated geometric lemma before promoting numerical observations into mathematics. In particular, checking all configurations up to a fixed size is not a proof of superlinear growth.
