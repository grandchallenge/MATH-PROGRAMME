## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem #413 studies when adding a small arithmetic function to every smaller integer still stays below a chosen integer. Write $ω(m)$ for the number of distinct prime divisors of $m$. An integer $n$ is an $ω$-barrier when

$$
m+ω(m) ≤ n
$$

for every integer $m<n$. The first question asks whether infinitely many such barriers exist. The second asks for a constant $ε>0$ for which infinitely many $n$ satisfy the weaker inequality $m+εω(m)≤n$ for every $m<n$. These are global questions: a candidate $n$ must dominate every earlier $m$, not merely most of them.

The same language also compares nearby functions. $Ω(m)$ counts prime factors with multiplicity, while the exponent-product function $d_0(m)$ is defined by $d_0(m)=∏_i a_i$ when $m=∏_i p_i^{a_i}$. A barrier for $d_0$ is therefore a related but different object from a barrier for $ω$.

The original issue is [MATH-PROGRAMME #1683](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1683). Its source packet fixes the catalogue and formal-source identities used here; this article does not turn a source audit into a mathematical certification.

## A small example

The number $5$ is an $ω$-barrier. For $m=1,2,3,4$, the values of $m+ω(m)$ are $1,3,4,5$, respectively, so their maximum is exactly $5$. By contrast, $7$ is not an $ω$-barrier: $6=2· 3$ has $ω(6)=2$, and $6+ω(6)=8>7$.

I also reran an exact integer sieve, using trial division by the smallest prime factor for every integer through $99999$. For each $n$, I maintained the maximum of $m+Ω(m)$ over $1≤m<n$ and tested whether it was at most $n$. The resulting list had last element $99840$; the direct checks were

$$
99840=2^9·3·5·13,    Ω(99840)=12,    max_{m<99840}(m+Ω(m))=99840,
$$

and $99841$ fails because $99840+Ω(99840)=99852>99841$. Thus the finite computation reproduces the reported largest $Ω$-barrier below $10^5$. It says nothing about infinitely many $Ω$-barriers, still less about infinitely many $ω$-barriers. It is a check of a finite range, not a proof of an asymptotic statement.

## What is known

In *Some unconventional problems in number theory*, Acta Mathematica Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae 33 (1979), 71–80, Erdős defines a barrier by the same universal inequality and states Theorem 1 on printed page 71: for $d_0(n)=∏_i a_i$, there are infinitely many barriers, and the set of such integers has positive density. His proof outline on printed pages 79–80 uses an averaging argument over multiples of a product of small primes. At the end he additionally says that the density of $d_0$-barriers exists, with a stronger family of density assertions. This is an external theorem about $d_0$, not a solution of the $ω$ question.

The same paper says that infinitely many $ω$-barriers seemed difficult and that even the existence of some fixed positive coefficient in front of $ω$ was not proved there. It records the computational fact, attributed to Selfridge, that $99840$ is the largest $Ω$-barrier below $10^5$.

A modern primary source changes the second question. Lau, *On the Number of Prime Factors of Consecutive Integers*, arXiv:2604.15042 (2026), Theorem 1.3, proves that there is a constant $C>0$ and infinitely many positive integers $N$ such that $ω(N-k)≤Ω(N-k)≤C\log k$ for every integer $1<k<N$. Setting $n=N-1$ and $ε=1/C$, and using $\log k≤k-1$, gives $m+εω(m)≤n$ for the corresponding earlier $m=N-k$. This establishes the weak, positive-$ε$ question, subject to the ordinary source interpretation of the theorem; it does not establish infinitely many exact $ω$-barriers.

## What remains

The principal mathematical remainder is the exact first question: decide whether there are infinitely many positive $n$ satisfying $m+\omega(m)\le n$ for all $m<n$. A bounded successor should first normalize the domain convention at $m=0$, then reproduce Lau's implication for the weak part and separately investigate exact $ω$-barriers. A useful finite phase can enumerate barrier candidates and record the first violating $m$ for each failure, but it must stop at a declared bound and cannot be presented as evidence of infinitude.

The formal file also needs semantic review: its real-valued definition quantifies over natural $m<n$, whereas the printed source uses arithmetic-function notation and does not spell out the zero convention in the theorem sentence. The variants for $d_0$, $Ω$, and the finite maximum must remain distinct. Any stronger formal claim requires a proof source and specialist mathematical review; a `sorry` theorem is only a statement interface.
