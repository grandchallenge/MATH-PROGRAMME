## The question in plain language

Start with a fixed positive integer $a$ and a prime $p$. Choose any finite strictly increasing list of integers beginning with $a$, and add their factorials:

$$
a_1!+a_2!+\cdots+a_n!,\qquad a=a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_n.
$$

The first question asks whether the powers of $p$ dividing such sums have a finite ceiling. If a ceiling exists, call it $f(a,p)$ and ask how it depends on $a$ and $p$. The second question asks for one prime $p$ and one infinite increasing sequence $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ for which the exponent of the highest power of $p$ dividing each successive partial sum tends to infinity. These are valuation questions: they concern exact divisibility, not the size of the factorial sum as an ordinary integer.

The quantifiers matter. The list length $n$ is free and is not the exponent of $p$; the starting point $a$ is fixed while later factorial indices may be arbitrarily large. The source also asks for distinct factorials, so repeated indices are not allowed.

## A small example

Take $a=1$ and $p=5$, with the increasing list $(1,4)$. Exact arithmetic gives

$$
1!+4!=1+24=25=5^2,
$$

and $5^3=125$ does not divide $25$. Thus this particular sum has valuation at the prime $5$ exactly $2$, and, if $f(1,5)$ is finite, it proves the lower bound $f(1,5)\geq 2$.

This calculation is reproducible without floating-point arithmetic: evaluate the two factorials as integers, add them, and divide by $5$ repeatedly until the quotient is no longer divisible by $5$. It does not prove that $f(1,5)$ is finite, determine its value, or construct an infinite sequence whose partial-sum valuations diverge. It is only one finite witness.

## What is known

The primary source located for this audit is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, Monographies de l’Enseignement Mathématique 28 (1980), available as a scan at [the author-hosted PDF](https://fanchung.ucsd.edu/ron/papers/80_11_number_theory.pdf). On printed page 79 it asks whether there is a bound depending on the first index and the prime for the exponent in a sum of distinct factorials, and then asks about an infinite increasing sequence with unbounded successive valuations. The same page reports that S. Lin showed the largest power of $2$ occurring in a sum of distinct factorials containing $2$ is $2^{254}$.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #404 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/404) presents the normalized form, records Lin as giving $f(2,2)\leq254$, and marks the problem open. Its discussion includes later comments with computational witnesses and conjectural observations, including very large finite lower bounds for some odd-prime cases and a suggested finite subset-sum extension lemma. Those comments are useful leads, not independently checked theorems for this article. In particular, the public discussion's stronger wording $f(2,2)=254$ is reported here as an external claim, not as GCL verification.

## What remains

The smallest useful next step is a source-and-certificate check for one fixed finite transition suggested in the discussion. For $p=5$, test the normalized factorials from $5!$ through $9!$ modulo $5$: after dividing each by $5^{\nu_5(5!)}=5$, their residues are $4,4,3,4,1$. Their subset sums cover every residue class modulo $5$. A bounded task can verify this table and the exact normalization, then check that an already supplied witness below $5$ extends to the corresponding divisibility level below $10$.

Acceptance is an exact-arithmetic residue table, an explicit increasing witness, and a short proof of the finite extension implication. Falsification is any missing residue, incorrect valuation, repeated index, or failure of the claimed divisibility. The task stops after this single transition; it must not be presented as evidence of an infinite construction. A separate, more ambitious successor would need a pinned Lin memorandum or a pinned computational certificate before treating the $f(2,2)$ report as established.
