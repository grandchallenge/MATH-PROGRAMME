## The question in plain language

For positive integers $n\ge1$ and $k\ge1$, say a run

$$
m+1,m+2,\ldots,m+k,\qquad 1\le m\le n,
$$

is **$k$‐rough in the required sense** if every one of its $k$ entries has **at least one prime divisor strictly greater than $k$**. Let $K(n)$ be the largest $k$ for which such a run exists with starting point $m\le n$. Erdős asked for the order of growth of $K(n)$, particularly whether

$$
\log K(n)\le(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}.
$$

This is **OPEN**. The condition is not that every prime factor of every entry exceed $k$, and $m\le n$ bounds the *start*, not necessarily the final integer $m+k$. [Maintained Erdős #962](https://www.erdosproblems.com/962).

## A small example

*An exact small example*

Take $m=4$ and $k=2$. The required integers are $5$ and $6$. The prime $5>2$ divides $5$, and the prime $3>2$ divides $6$. Thus the run qualifies and $K(n)\ge2$ for every $n\ge4$.

But the same start does not give $k=3$: although $5$ and $7$ have prime factors exceeding $3$, the middle integer $6=2\cdot3$ has **none greater than $3$**. A brute-force verifier that merely checks each integer is composite, or that one of its prime factors is $\ge k$, would misread the definition. The strict inequality $p>k$ is essential.

## What is known

*Established lower bounds and the original conjecture*

Erdős [Er65] noted the elementary lower estimate

$$
\log K(n)\ge(\log n)^{1/2-o(1)}.
$$

In [Er76e, p.273] he gave the stronger scale

$$
\log K(n)\gg\sqrt{\log n\,\log\log n}.
$$

Quanyu Tang's December 2025 note, as recorded in the maintained catalogue, sharpens the explicit leading constant:

$$
\log K(n)\ge\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}-o(1)\right)
          \sqrt{\log n\,\log\log n}.
$$

The last statement is source-attributed to a publicly discussed note; full independent proof audit remains pending. The two bounds are compatible because the implicit constant in the earlier $\gg$ assertion is unspecified. Erdős's proposed *upper* behavior, approximately $\exp((\log n)^{1/2+o(1)})$, is still much smaller than any fixed positive power of $n$. [Original-source catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/962); [dated discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/962).

The catalogue also reports Erdős's older upper estimate

$$
K(n)\le n^{1/2}\exp(-(\log n)^c)
$$

for some absolute $c>0$ and sufficiently large $n$, and Tao's independently described simple estimate $K(n)\le(1+o(1))\sqrt n$. These historical facts do **not** represent the strongest published fixed-power upper estimate now obtainable.

## A useful published upper bound via Grimm's function

Laishram and M. Ram Murty studied a *different* quantity $g(m)$: the largest $j$ such that there exist **pairwise distinct primes** $q_1,\ldots,q_j$ with

$$
q_i\mid(m+i),\qquad 1\le i\le j.
$$

They proved that there is a constant $\alpha$ with $0.45<\alpha<0.46$ such that

$$
g(m)<m^\alpha
$$

for all sufficiently large $m$. This is their Theorem 1(i), *Grimm's conjecture and smooth numbers*, **Michigan Mathematical Journal 61** (2012), pp.151–160, DOI [10.1307/mmj/1331222852](https://doi.org/10.1307/mmj/1331222852). [Author manuscript](https://www.isid.ac.in/~shanta/PAPERS/GCnSN.pdf).

Every block eligible for #962 automatically has **distinct representative prime divisors**. Indeed, for each $i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$, choose $q_i>k$ dividing $m+i$. If the same prime $q$ divided both $m+i$ and $m+j$ for $i\ne j$, then $q\mid(j-i)$. But

$$
0<|j-i|<k<q,
$$

which is impossible. Thus all $q_i$ are distinct, so $k\le g(m)$.

Combining this elementary injection with Laishram–Murty gives, for all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
\boxed{K(n)< n^{0.46}.}
$$

The finitely many small starting points $m$ can be absorbed into the eventual bound, because each has a finite $g(m)$; for large $m\le n$, use $K(n)\le g(m)<m^\alpha\le n^\alpha<n^{0.46}$. This is a **rigorous corollary conditional only on the cited published theorem**, not an unconditional proof independently developed by GCL. It improves the historical fixed-power exponent $1/2$, yet falls far short of the conjecture: it yields $\log K(n)\le 0.46\log n$, not $(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}$.

A July 2026 research report also records this reduction; GCL does **not** assert priority for the observation. [Independent report](https://www.erdosproblemaday.com/report/962).

## Why it is difficult

The threshold $k$ appears on **both sides** of the definition: the interval length is $k$, while every entry must have a prime factor exceeding exactly that length. Classical results on a single large prime divisor or average distribution of smooth numbers do not directly bound the longest such interval among all starts up to $n$.

The Grimm reduction exploits a special combinatorial advantage: prime divisors larger than the interval length cannot repeat across two different entries. It converts the problem into a distinct-prime assignment, which is more structured but remains much too weak for the conjectured logarithmic exponent.
