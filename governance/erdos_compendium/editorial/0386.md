## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 386 asks whether a nontrivial binomial coefficient can repeatedly be made from one uninterrupted run of primes. The live statement takes integers satisfying $2\leq k\leq n-2$ and asks whether $\binom{n}{k}$ can be the product of consecutive primes infinitely often. In concrete terms, this means that there should be a fixed $k$ and infinitely many $n$ for which

$$
\binom{n}{k}=p_a p_{a+1}\cdots p_{b-1}
$$

for some consecutive primes $p_a,p_{a+1},\ldots,p_{b-1}$. The phrase “infinitely often” is the central difficulty: finding many examples, or checking every case below a large cutoff, does not settle it.

The reading with a fixed parameter $k$ matters. The pinned formal file encodes an existential choice of $k$ followed by “frequently” many $n$ tending to infinity. It also records separate variants asking for every fixed $k$ and for $k=2$. Those are useful comparisons, but they are not automatically equivalent to the main question. By symmetry, $\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}$, so one can often inspect $k\leq n/2$ when doing a finite search.

There is also a difference between a product of consecutive primes and a number whose prime divisors merely form a consecutive set: the former requires each prime in the interval exactly once. A correct audit must therefore check both squarefreeness and the absence of gaps. This is why an equality of large integers alone is not enough to certify the intended property.

## A small example

The page’s example is exact:

$$
\binom{21}{2}=\frac{21\cdot20}{2}=210=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7.
$$

The four factors are consecutive primes. The following standard-library calculation was run as an exact integer check:

```python
from math import comb, prod

assert comb(21, 2) == 210
assert prod([2, 3, 5, 7]) == 210
assert comb(21, 2) == prod([2, 3, 5, 7])
```

Other displayed examples also verify directly: $\binom{7}{3}=35=5\cdot7$, $\binom{10}{4}=210=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$, $\binom{14}{4}=1001=7\cdot11\cdot13$, and $\binom{15}{6}=5005=5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13$. These calculations establish examples, not an infinite family. For $k=2$, the elementary identity

$$
8\binom{n}{2}+1=(2n-1)^2
$$

is also an exact way to test a proposed value of $n$; it does not prove that only finitely many such values exist.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 74. The scanned page gives the consecutive-prime-product question, the example $\binom{21}{2}$, and the authors’ assessment that proving finiteness for $k=2$ seemed hopeless. It then presents as a probability or conjectural expectation the stronger belief that the phenomenon should not occur infinitely often in the interior range $3\leq k\leq n-3$. This is historical mathematical context, not a proof.

The maintained Erdős Problems page currently labels #386 OPEN and says that a finite computation cannot resolve it. It records the four examples supplied by Desmond Weisenberg and lists $n=4,6,15,21,715$ as the known values in the $k=2$ case, with a link to OEIS A280992. The page also warns that its open status reflects its owner’s current belief and that unlisted literature may exist. Thus the safest status statement is that the problem remains open in the maintained source, while a literature search should not be mistaken for proof that no partial result exists.

## What remains

The smallest source-sensitive task is to freeze the quantifiers before doing a large computation: fixed $k$ versus a $k$ allowed to vary with $n$, and “consecutive primes” versus an arbitrary finite prime interval. A bounded successor can then take an explicit cutoff $N$, enumerate all $n\leq N$ and $2\leq k\leq n/2$, and return every exact equality with its prime interval. Acceptance is an independently recomputed complete list for that declared region; falsification is one missed equality, nonprime factor, or nonconsecutive interval. The task stops at $N$ and makes no claim beyond it.

For the unresolved theorem, the missing step is not another example but a uniform argument controlling all sufficiently large $n$ and the permitted $k$. Any proposed reduction must state its hypotheses and prove that it preserves the product-of-consecutive-primes condition. A finite certificate can support the documentary record and test formal definitions, but it cannot decide the infinite question.
