## The question in plain language

Every composite integer has a smallest prime divisor. For example, the smallest prime dividing $209=11\cdot19$ is $11$.

Let $p(m)$ denote the smallest prime divisor of a composite integer $m$. If $m$ is just larger than $n$, consider how much of the gap $m-n$ can be absorbed by $p(m)$:

$$
n<m<n+p(m).
$$

Erdős Problem #463 asks whether, **for every sufficiently large integer $n$**, there is a composite integer $m$ substantially farther to the right than $n$ that still satisfies this inequality. “Substantially farther” must grow without bound as $n$ increases.

A precise way to state this is to ask whether a function $f(n)$ exists with $f(n)\to\infty$ and

$$
n+f(n)<m<n+p(m)
$$

for some composite $m$ at every sufficiently large $n$.

The emphasis is on **every sufficiently large $n$**, not just infinitely many specially chosen $n$. Examples with arbitrarily large gaps along one subsequence do not settle the question.

## A small example

Take $n=200$ and the composite integer

$$
m=209=11\cdot19.
$$

Its smallest prime factor is $p(m)=11$, so

$$
200+9=209<200+11=211.
$$

Thus the gap $m-n=9$ is allowed. By contrast, at $n=100$ a short exhaustive check finds no composite integer with $100<m<100+p(m)$.

For a reproducible way to compare such examples, define the **reach**

$$
R(n)=\max\left(
\{m-n:\ m\text{ composite},\ n<m<n+p(m)\}\cup\{0\}
\right).
$$

The added zero handles the case where no qualifying composite exists. Exact integer enumeration gives:

| $n$ | $R(n)$ | A qualifying example |
|---:|---:|---|
| $10$ | $0$ | None |
| $20$ | $1$ | $m=21$, with $p(m)=3$ |
| $23$ | $2$ | $m=25$, with $p(m)=5$ |
| $100$ | $0$ | None |
| $200$ | $9$ | $m=209$, with $p(m)=11$ |

The table shows that the reach varies irregularly. It does not establish whether $R(n)$ ultimately tends to infinity.

Crucially, the exact search for $R(n)$ is finite. For a composite $m$, its smallest prime factor satisfies $p(m)\leq\sqrt m$. Writing $s=m-n$, eligibility requires

$$
s<p(m)\leq\sqrt{n+s},
$$

so $s^2-s-n<0$ and therefore

$$
0<s<\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4n}}2.
$$

Only $O(\sqrt n)$ candidate shifts must be checked for a fixed $n$.

## What is known

The original formulation appears in Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 92. It asks, for each fixed constant $c>0$, whether every sufficiently large $n$ admits a composite $m$ with

$$
m>n+c,\qquad m-p(m)<n.
$$

This is **equivalent** to the later formulation involving a function $f(n)\to\infty$, rather than a different conjecture. The elementary proof is useful because it makes the order of quantifiers explicit.

For any fixed $c$, the original property says exactly that $R(n)>c$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Requiring this for **every** fixed $c$ is the definition of

$$
R(n)\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

If $R(n)\to\infty$, choose $f(n)=\lfloor R(n)/2\rfloor$. The function $f$ also tends to infinity; every sufficiently large $n$ has a composite maximizing the reach with $m-n=R(n)>f(n)$. Conversely, if a diverging $f$ gives such a witness for all sufficiently large $n$, then $R(n)>f(n)$ there, forcing $R(n)\to\infty$.

There is an easy construction showing that reach becomes arbitrarily large **on a subsequence**. For each prime $q$, take

$$
n=q^2-q+1,\qquad m=q^2.
$$

The smallest prime factor of $m$ is $q$, and

$$
m-p(m)=q^2-q<n,\qquad
m-n=q-1.
$$

It follows that

$$
R(q^2-q+1)\geq q-1.
$$

As $q$ ranges through larger primes, the right-hand side tends to infinity. Indeed, together with the elementary upper bound above, this shows that $R(n)$ can be nearly $\sqrt n$ along these special inputs.

**But a large limsup does not imply a diverging limit.** The unresolved issue is ruling out infinitely many later values of $n$ with small reach. The prime-square examples do not address that requirement.

The maintained Erdős Problems catalogue labels #463 **OPEN**. A related question is attributed to a 1992 article by Erdős concerning a minimum of $m-p(m)$ over $m>n$. That later paper was not independently inspected here. There is an important domain subtlety: if the minimum includes **prime** values $m$, then $p(m)=m$, and the minimum is trivially zero because arbitrarily large primes exist. A nontrivial formulation must restrict the domain or define a different quantity. No uninspected version of the 1992 statement has been adopted as an established fact.

## What remains

The first documentary task is to inspect the original 1992 *Eureka* article and pin its exact definition of the related minimum, especially whether prime candidates are excluded. This prevents accidental identification of an interesting composite-number question with a trivial zero-valued minimum.

The next bounded mathematical task is to prove a **uniform** lower bound for $R(n)$ over every sufficiently large $n$, even one that grows extremely slowly. Alternatively, a rigorous infinite sequence of inputs with bounded reach would refute the conjecture. Either outcome would have to go beyond the successful prime-square subsequence and finite enumerations.

An independently replayable computational fixture can evaluate $R(n)$ using trial division and the explicit finite upper bound on $m-n$. Finite checks can falsify claims about specific ranges, but they do not determine an eventual-for-all assertion by themselves.
