## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, the ratio $\phi(n)/n$ measures the fraction of numbers up to $n$ that are coprime to it. For each $c$ between zero and one, let $f(c)$ be the natural density of integers satisfying

$$
\frac{\phi(n)}{n}<c.
$$

Schoenberg proved that this density exists. [Erdős Problem #50](https://www.erdosproblems.com/50) asks whether **there is no point** at which the derivative $f'(c)$ exists and is strictly positive. The question remains **open**.

## A small example

Every positive $n$ has $\phi(n)>0$, so $f(0)=0$. For every $n>1$, $\phi(n)<n$, so $f(1)=1$. Increasing the threshold $c$ only adds eligible integers, hence $f$ is nondecreasing.

For example, $\phi(6)/6=1/3$ and $\phi(2)/2=1/2$. These arithmetic facts do not determine derivatives of the limiting distribution function.

## What is known

The maintained registry credits Schoenberg with the distribution's existence and Erdős with proving the function is **purely singular**. A continuous purely singular distribution function has derivative zero almost everywhere. But *almost everywhere* allows an exceptional set of measure zero; it does **not** imply no point has an existing positive derivative. That distinction is the mathematical heart of #50.

The historical primary reference is Schoenberg, *On asymptotic distributions of arithmetical functions*, *Transactions of the AMS* **39** (1936), 315–330. The full source proof and the exact original pointwise derivative argument were not independently reconstructed here.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1319](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1319#issuecomment-6133365340) compares the density and purely singular definitions with frozen Formal Conjectures, whose research proof bodies are unfilled. The formal open question uses derivatives *within* the interval $[0,1]$, raising a boundary convention for endpoints that needs careful source fidelity. No positive derivative or global negation has been established.

## What remains

Prove that every existing derivative of $f$ is nonpositive or zero, or construct a point of finite positive derivative. For a monotone function the former amounts to proving zero at every differentiability point; the known almost-everywhere statement is insufficient.
