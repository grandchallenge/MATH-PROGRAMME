## The question in plain language

How many different distances must appear between the vertices of a three-dimensional convex polyhedron? Unlike an arbitrary collection of points, the vertices are all extreme points of one solid: none lies inside the convex hull of the others.

The [maintained Erdős Problem #660](https://www.erdosproblems.com/660) asks whether every such $n$ vertex polyhedron determines at least

$$
(1-o(1))\frac n2
$$

distinct pairwise Euclidean distances, as $n$ becomes large. The phrase $1-o(1)$ means that the coefficient may fall slightly below one-half, but the relative shortfall must tend to zero.

There is a **historical wording ambiguity**: an earlier rendering of Erdős's question concerned the *existence* of configurations with fewer distances, whereas the currently maintained problem asks for a *universal lower bound*. The current, mathematically interesting question remains **OPEN**.

## A small example

Put six equally spaced points on the unit circle in the plane $z=0$, and add one vertex above its centre:

$$
v_j=(\cos(2\pi j/6),\sin(2\pi j/6),0),
\quad j=0,\ldots,5,
\qquad a=(0,0,\sqrt3).
$$

These seven points are the vertices of a full three-dimensional convex pyramid, with a regular hexagonal base.

The base determines the three positive chord lengths

$$
1,\qquad\sqrt3,\qquad2.
$$

Every distance from the apex to a base vertex equals

$$
\sqrt{1+(\sqrt3)^2}=2.
$$

The apex adds **no new distance**. The seven vertices therefore determine precisely three positive distances,

$$
D(P)=3=\frac{7-1}{2}.
$$

This does not disprove the asymptotic conjecture: it shows how close to $n/2$ a legitimate three-dimensional convex polyhedron can get.

## What is known

The preceding geometry generalizes to every sufficiently large vertex count. Take a regular $m$ gon of radius one in the plane $z=0$. Its positive chord lengths are

$$
L_k=2\sin\left(\frac{\pi k}{m}\right),
\qquad1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac m2\right\rfloor.
$$

They are strictly increasing over the indicated range, so the base has exactly $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ distances. Its longest chord $L=L_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}$ exceeds $1$ for $m\geq3$.

Add an apex of height

$$
h=\sqrt{L^2-1}
$$

directly above the centre. All base-to-apex distances become exactly $L$, one of the distances already present among the base vertices. Every base point is a genuine extreme vertex of the resulting pyramid, and the apex is outside the base plane. Thus for $n=m+1$,

$$
D(P)=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
$$

Consequently the minimum number of distinct distances among all $n$ vertex convex polyhedra is **at most** $\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$. This is an elementary construction, also discussed in the public source comments. It proves that no universal bound with a leading constant **larger than** $1/2$ could be correct. But an *upper bound on the minimum* is not the requested *lower bound for every polyhedron*.

**The planar analogue is a theorem.** In the plane, the vertices of a convex polygon determine at least $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ distances, a result attributed to Elte Altman's 1963 paper [*On a problem of P. Erdős*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/93), *American Mathematical Monthly* **70**, 148–157. It is not legitimate simply to apply that proof to arbitrary vertices in $\mathbb R^3$: those vertices need not all lie in one plane, and a planar polygon theorem does not automatically give an appropriate large planar subset of a three-dimensional convex polyhedron.

The maintained #660 entry also reports that Erdős once attributed a linear three-dimensional distance bound to Altman without supplying a corresponding reference. This is an **unverified historical attribution**, not an inspected primary proof.

**An important quantifier distinction.** The original wording discussed on the [public #660 thread](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/660) could be read as asking whether there *exists* a polyhedron with unusually few distances for each large $n$. The current version asks whether **every** polyhedron has nearly $n/2$ distances. The regular-pyramid family addresses an existence upper bound and does not settle the universal lower-bound question.

## GCL's current state

The frozen catalogue and currently maintained problem both report **OPEN**. The [authenticated source return on original issue #1931](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1931#issuecomment-6144471179) reproduces the exact regular-pyramid construction, verifies its full-dimensional convexity and records the historical quantifier discrepancy as a documentary blocker.

The precise original [Er97e, p.531] passage remains to be inspected in the primary publication rather than only its later transcription. The same limitation applies to the original Altman paper's full proof and the reported later attribution in [Er75f].

This is an **unaccepted editorial manuscript**. It makes no claim to have resolved the open three-dimensional lower bound, constructed a novel theorem, completed a protected production merge or verified a deployed mathematical page.

## What remains

The immediate documentary task is to obtain and compare the relevant 1997 Erdős page, Altman's 1963 theorem and Erdős's later attribution. The result should preserve the exact quantifier structure of each historical version, while clearly naming the current mathematical conjecture.

The substantive problem is then to establish a uniform lower bound for the number of different distances determined by every three-dimensional convex polyhedron. A bounded preliminary approach is to investigate whether the vertex set contains an appropriately large subset whose geometry permits a planar or near-planar counting lemma. But claiming that every convex polyhedron has many vertices on one plane would itself require proof and is generally not an available premise.

A satisfactory advance must give a proved estimate with explicit assumptions, an honest comparison with existing general three-dimensional point-set results, and either a new geometric constraint or a certified counterexample. Numerical experiments on small pyramids cannot settle an asymptotic universal statement.

The editorial production path still requires critical source checking, consolidated protected publication, public browser MathJax inspection and exact-hash V2 admission.
