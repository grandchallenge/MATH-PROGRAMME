## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 437 starts with strictly increasing positive integers $1\leq a_1<\cdots<a_t\leq x$ and studies their cumulative products $P_j=a_1a_2\cdots a_j$. How many of these $P_j$ can be perfect squares? More specifically, is it possible, for any fixed $\varepsilon>0$, to choose a sequence having more than $x^{1-\varepsilon}$ square partial products when $x$ is sufficiently large? The restriction $a_j\leq x$ applies to every **factor**, not to the potentially enormous running products. Distinctness and increasing order rule out a trivial choice of repeated small square factors.

The key algebra is parity of prime valuations. An integer $M$ is a square exactly when $v_p(M)$ is even for every prime $p$. Each new factor adds its prime-exponent parity vector to the running parity state over $\mathbf F_2$. Thus the problem seeks many returns to the zero parity vector along a walk whose steps come from a strictly increasing sequence of distinct integers. This description explains why a collection of smooth numbers can be useful: shared small prime factors make cancellation of parities possible, without forcing each chosen number itself to be square.

## A small example

A small exact example is the increasing sequence $(1,2,8,9)$. Its partial products are $1,2,16,144$. Three are squares, since $1=1^2$, $16=4^2$ and $144=12^2$. The factor $8$ is not square, yet $2\cdot8$ is square because the exponent of $2$ changes from one to four. The final factor $9$ is square and therefore preserves the square status. All calculations are integer exact and demonstrate the parity-vector mechanism directly.

The example does **not** establish the asymptotic question. A four-term prefix gives only three square running products, whereas the Erdős question asks for arbitrarily large families with almost linearly many square products in the scale of the largest allowed *factor*. An independent finite fixture can compare an integer-square-root test for every $P_j$ against its factor-parity calculation. Agreement catches prime-factorization mistakes and incorrect square tests. The empty product should not be counted as a chosen prefix.

## What is known

The historical source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), which remarked that $o(x)$ square partial products is expected and not entirely trivial to establish. The maintained [Erdős Problems #437](https://www.erdosproblems.com/437) page reports a **positive solution** following the work of Bui, Pratt and Zaharescu (2024), highlighted by Terence Tao.

Write $L(x)$ for the largest number of square partial products obtainable using factors up to $x$, and let $u(x)=\sqrt{\log x\log\log x}$. The reported estimates are
$$
x\exp(-(\sqrt2+o(1))u(x))\leq L(x)\leq
x\exp(-(1/\sqrt2+o(1))u(x)).
$$
Since $u(x)=o(\log x)$, both sides have the form $x^{1-o(1)}$, so the lower bound indeed exceeds $x^{1-\varepsilon}$ eventually for every fixed positive $\varepsilon$. The constants in the two bounds differ; it would be unjustified to claim a single exact asymptotic from these inequalities.

The related [Problem #841](https://www.erdosproblems.com/841) concerns another square-prefix question and is not automatically covered by this theorem. Any analysis must distinguish the asymptotic maximum from the count of individually square terms in the selected sequence.

## What remains

A useful bounded follow-up is to build two independent counters for square partial products for $x\leq30$. One multiplies integers and tests integer square roots. The other updates a prime-exponent parity vector, counting a square exactly when all parity bits vanish. Exhaustive maximization can be attempted only for a transparently stated small input range; the number of increasing factor sequences grows exponentially. The resulting finite maxima should be recorded with maximizing sequences and their exact partial products.

The source-review half of the task should read the Bui–Pratt–Zaharescu theorem and Tao's derivation, including all $o(1)$ terms and the definition of $L(x)$. The task succeeds when numerical fixtures agree and both constants appear on the correct sides of the bounds. It fails if a finite search is called an asymptotic proof, if repeated factors are accidentally allowed, or if the two different constants are replaced by an invented precise limit. Stop at this bounded source and arithmetic audit.
