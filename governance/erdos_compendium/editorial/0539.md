**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #539 asks how few distinct ratios $a/\gcd(a,b)$ an $n$‑element set of positive integers can generate. The exact asymptotic order remains **OPEN**, though a July 2026 ProofCouncil preprint derives the sharp *power exponent* one-half, using a higher-dimensional construction. Its accompanying Lean development proves the exponent conclusion, but the finer subexponential upper bound is established only in the paper's informal proof.

## The question in plain language

Let $A$ contain $n$ distinct **positive integers**. For each **ordered** pair $a,b\in A$, form the integer quotient

$$
\frac{a}{\gcd(a,b)}.
$$

Collect the distinct results in

$$
Q(A)=\left\{\frac{a}{\gcd(a,b)}:a,b\in A\right\}.
$$

The diagonal choices $a=b$ all contribute $1$; reversing a pair may produce a different quotient. Define

$$
\boxed{h(n)=\min_{\substack{A\subseteq\mathbb Z_{>0}\\|A|=n}}|Q(A)|.}
$$

How does $h(n)$ grow with $n$? It counts the minimum unavoidable diversity of quotients after cancelling the common factors of every ordered pair.

The problem is connected to divisibility but is **not** about ordinary differences of integers. Its surprising structure emerges when every integer is replaced by the vector of the exponents in its prime factorization.

## A small example

Choose $A=\{6,10,15\}$. Computing the quotient for every ordered pair gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
a\backslash b &6&10&15\\ \hline
6&1&3&2\\
10&5&1&2\\
15&5&3&1
\end{array}
$$

and therefore

$$
Q(A)=\{1,2,3,5\},\qquad |Q(A)|=4.
$$

Now replace an integer $a$ by its vector of prime exponents. Using primes $2,3,5$, the elements above become

$$
v(6)=(1,1,0),\quad
v(10)=(1,0,1),\quad
v(15)=(0,1,1).
$$

For any $a,b$, prime factorization gives

$$
v_p\left(\frac a{\gcd(a,b)}\right)
=\max\{v_p(a)-v_p(b),0\}.
$$

Thus the quotient is represented by the **coordinatewise positive part** of the difference between exponent vectors. This is an exact identity, not an approximation.

For a finite set $F\subseteq\mathbb Z^d$, define

$$
D(F)=\{(x-y)^+:x,y\in F\},
\quad
(x-y)^+_i=\max\{x_i-y_i,0\}.
$$

Then for the exponent vectors of a set $A$,

$$
\boxed{|Q(A)|=|D(v(A))|.}
$$

Conversely, any finite integer-vector configuration can be translated into the nonnegative orthant and encoded using distinct primes. Therefore the minimum over **all finite dimensions** of $|D(F)|$ for $|F|=n$ equals $h(n)$. This is the key bridge to additive combinatorics.

## What is known

### Erdős and Szemerédi's original problem

The [1973 original Chapter 12, printed pp.124–125](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=9) was inspected directly. After a related conjecture of R. L. Graham, Erdős defines $h(n)$ as the guaranteed number of distinct quotients and credits joint work with Szemerédi giving a square-root lower estimate and a power-saving upper estimate,

$$
n^{1/2}\ll h(n)\ll n^{1-c}
$$

for some absolute $c>0$. He specifically asks whether one can determine

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log h(n)}{\log n}.
$$

This limit is a **subquestion** about the power exponent. Knowing it does not determine the full asymptotic order of $h(n)$.

Andrew Granville and Friedrich Roesler, *The Set of Differences of a Given Set*, *American Mathematical Monthly* **106** (1999), 338–344, [DOI 10.2307/2589556](https://doi.org/10.2307/2589556), explained the prime-exponent translation and proved the classical bounds

$$
\boxed{c\sqrt n\le h(n)\le Cn^{2/3}.}
$$

They credit the upper-bound construction to Freiman and Lev. The original authors' [publication description](https://dms.umontreal.ca/~andrew/1999.php) was checked. The full seven-page journal proof was not independently reconstructed in this tranche.

### An elementary lower-bound argument

Let $F\subseteq\mathbb Z^d$ contain $n$ points and let $q=|D(F)|$. Because

$$
x-y=(x-y)^+-(y-x)^+,
$$

we have $F-F\subseteq D(F)-D(F)$.

Any set of $q$ distinct vectors has at most $q(q-1)+1$ distinct ordinary differences: there are at most $q(q-1)$ ordered unequal pairs plus the zero difference. On the other hand, any set of $n$ distinct integer vectors has at least $2n-1$ distinct ordinary differences. To see this, apply an integer linear functional injective on $F$; the image has $n$ distinct integers whose difference set includes $n-1$ positive and $n-1$ negative differences, as well as zero.

Consequently,

$$
2n-1\le q(q-1)+1,
$$

or

$$
\boxed{h(n)\ge\frac{1+\sqrt{8n-7}}2.}
$$

This explicit improvement on the constant in the square-root lower bound appears as Proposition A.4 in the July 2026 ProofCouncil paper. Its short finite combinatorial argument can be checked independently of the more complicated upper-bound construction.

### The 2026 power-exponent result

J. Schmitt, T. Gehrunger, J. Dekoninck, G. Bérczi, U. Kreitner, L. Price and D. Holmes, *ProofCouncil: An LLM Agent for Solving Open Mathematical Problems*, [arXiv:2607.09474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.09474), submitted July 10, 2026, give a self-contained upper-bound construction in Appendix A.

Theorem A.1, on printed p.11, states

$$
\boxed{
\frac{1+\sqrt{8n-7}}2
\le h(n)\le
\sqrt n\,\exp(C\sqrt{\log n})
}
$$

for some absolute constant $C>0$ and $n\ge2$. Because the correction $\exp(C\sqrt{\log n})$ is smaller than every positive power of $n$, it follows that

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log h(n)}{\log n}=\frac12.}
$$

The complete original **25-page preprint** was retrieved. Its theorem, the vector-equivalence proof, the base example, the separated-suspension lemma and the discussion of the Lean proof's limitations were examined. This is a **2026 preprint**, not a GCL independently reviewed or peer-reviewed proof.

The upper-bound method starts with an *antidiagonal strip* in $\mathbb Z^2$. For a positive integer $W$, it considers

$$
B_W=\{(i,j):0\le i,j\le W^2,\
W^2\le i+j\le W^2+W-1\}.
$$

Its key estimates are

$$
|B_W|\ge\tfrac12W^3,\quad
|D(B_W)|\le3W^2,\quad
|B_W-B_W|\le6W^3.
$$

Then a **separated suspension** creates from $F\subseteq\mathbb Z^d$ a set of points

$$
S_K(F)=\{(j,x+jM\mathbf1,y-jM\mathbf1):
0\le j<K,\ x,y\in F\},
$$

where $M$ is larger than all relevant coordinate differences. If $m=|F|$, $q=|D(F)|$ and $t=|F-F|$, the proof shows

$$
|S_K(F)|=Km^2,\qquad
|D(S_K(F))|\le q^2+2(K-1)t.
$$

Iterating the suspension and optimizing its depth yields the $\sqrt n$ times subexponential upper estimate. The fixed-depth exponents are

$$
\alpha_s=
\frac{2^{s+1}}{2^{s+2}-1}
\longrightarrow\frac12.
$$

These inequalities give a concrete finite verification interface, but testing them on small strips does **not** verify the entire asymptotic iteration.
