## The question in plain language

A positive integer $N$ is $r$‐powerful if every prime $p\mid N$ satisfies $p^r\mid N$. Erdős asks, for $r\ge4$, whether the sum of $r-2$ coprime $r$‐powerful numbers can itself be $r$‐powerful, and whether there are at most finitely many such solutions. There is also a distinct question: are there infinitely many triples of coprime **3-powerful** numbers satisfying $a+b=c$? The maintained catalogue still marks the problem open. [Erdős Problem #939](https://www.erdosproblems.com/939).

## A small example

*Why coprimality needs precision*

"Pairwise coprime" means $\gcd(a_i,a_j)=1$ for all $i\ne j$. "Jointly coprime" means only $\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_s)=1$, a weaker requirement when $s\ge3$. For example $6,10,15$ are jointly coprime but not pairwise coprime. Source discussion of #939 explicitly notes this distinction for proposed numerical witnesses. A purported construction must state which interpretation it satisfies.

For a two-summand equation $a+b=c$, joint coprimality and pairwise coprimality are equivalent: a common prime dividing any two of $a,b,c$ divides the third as well. For three or more summands the equivalence fails. This makes the $r=4$ case structurally different from $r=5$.

## A helpful reduction and its limitation

If $N$ is $r$‐powerful, its prime factorization $N=\prod p^{e_p}$, with $e_p\ge r$, can be written as $N=A^r B$, where $B$ is $r$‐th-power-free and all of its prime exponents lie in $\{0,\ldots,r-1\}$. This representation is exact, but it does not turn the additive equation into a pure equation of $r$‐th powers: the coefficients $B$ vary with the solution. Applying Fermat's Last Theorem to the $r=4$ question as though every 4-powerful number were a fourth power is therefore invalid.

## What is known and how to read reported examples

The problem discussion records numerical candidate identities for $r=5$ with three 5-powerful summands. For example,

$$
2^5 17^6+7^{11}+2^5 3^6 7^8=3^7 13^7.
$$

The three summands share no prime common to **all three**, but the first and third share $2$, and the second and third share $7$. Thus this is an example under *joint* coprimality, not under pairwise coprimality. The arithmetic identity can be checked exactly; the interpretation is essential. [Catalogue discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/939).

The same discussion identifies a paper by Walsh (arXiv:2404.03970), but no broad unconditional answer to all the stated questions is established by the catalogue. Finite witnesses settle an existence subquestion for a specified interpretation and fixed $r$, but do not prove finiteness or infinitude of entire families.

## Why the problem is difficult

High prime multiplicities restrict each term strongly, while addition permits interactions among distinct prime supports. The total number of summands grows with $r$; pairwise-prime restrictions and jointly-prime restrictions have genuinely different arithmetic behavior. Algebraic constructions for one $r$ cannot simply be extrapolated to all $r$.

## GCL's current state

This article separates the existential, finiteness and infinitude questions and explicitly prevents a joint-coprimality witness from being misrepresented as pairwise coprime. The displayed $r=5$ identity is an attributed discussion example, not an independently certified global theorem. No unconditional general answer or formalized solution is claimed.
