## The question in plain language

Take a positive integer $n$ and look at the short block of integers immediately after it. The block length grows very slowly, as the natural logarithm of $n$:

$$
K=\lfloor\log n\rfloor,\qquad n+1,n+2,\ldots,n+K.
$$

Multiply those numbers and ask for the **smallest prime that does not divide the product**:

$$
q(n,K)=\min\left\{p\text{ prime}:p\nmid\prod_{i=1}^{K}(n+i)\right\}.
$$

The 1980 Erdős–Graham question asks whether there is a fixed $\varepsilon>0$ such that, for infinitely many $n$,

$$
q(n,\lfloor\log n\rfloor)>(2+\varepsilon)\log n.
$$

In ordinary terms: can every prime up to slightly more than twice $\log n$ be made to divide at least one member of a surprisingly short consecutive block, infinitely often? The answer to **this 1980 direction** is yes. There is a crucial source exception: Erdős's 1979 paper instead asks about an eventual **upper** bound $q(n,K)<(2+\varepsilon)\log n$. These are opposite quantifier/direction claims, not two spellings of one conjecture.

## A small example

For $n=32$, $K=\lfloor\log32\rfloor=3$, and the block is $33,34,35$. Their product is divisible by $2,3,5,7,11$ but not by $13$. Thus

$$
q(32,3)=13>2.1\log32.
$$

A second independently checked example is $n=113729$, $K=11$, and $q(n,K)=59$. Both are finite witnesses; neither proves that such blocks occur infinitely often.

For any prime $p\leq K$, one of $K$ consecutive integers is divisible by $p$. Therefore the challenge is to arrange divisibility by the larger primes between $K$ and about $2.1\log n$.

## What is known

The two original statements must be read separately. In *Some unconventional problems in number theory* (1979, printed p. 78), Erdős poses an eventual upper-bound question. In Erdős and Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980, printed p. 91), the inequality points the other way and is required infinitely often. The maintained catalogue labels #457 **proved (Lean)**, and an external Lean development states the 1980 direction. This catalogue label is not a GCL compilation result.

A source-audited elementary construction supplies a concrete positive answer with $\varepsilon=0.1$. Here is its mathematical mechanism, with the estimates exposed. Choose a large integer $m$, put

$$
A=\binom{2m}{m},\qquad Q=\{p\text{ prime}:2m<p\leq3m\},\qquad r=|Q|.
$$

Every prime $m<p\leq2m$ divides $A$. Every prime in $Q$ divides $\binom{3m}{m}$, hence

$$
r\log(2m)\leq\log\binom{3m}{m}\leq3m\log2,\qquad r=O(m/\log m).
$$

Simultaneous pigeonhole approximation gives an integer $1\leq k\leq6^r$ and integers $\ell_p$ for $p\in Q$ with

$$
|kA-\ell_p p|\leq p/6.
$$

Set $c=\lfloor3m/5\rfloor$ and $n=kA-c$. Since $\log A=2m\log2+O(\log m)$ and $\log k=O(m/\log m)$,

$$
\log n=(2\log2)m+O(m/\log m),\qquad m<\lfloor\log n\rfloor,\qquad2.1\log n<3m
$$

for sufficiently large $m$. The block $n+1,\ldots,n+K$ now catches every prime $p\leq3m$. If $p\leq m$, its length $K>m$ suffices. If $m<p\leq2m$, the block contains $n+c=kA$, which is divisible by $p$. If $2m<p\leq3m$, write $d_p=kA-\ell_p p$. Then the block position

$$
i_p=c-d_p
$$

lies between $1$ and $K$ for all sufficiently large $m$ because $|d_p|\leq p/6\leq m/2$ and $c\sim0.6m$. At that position $n+i_p=\ell_p p$. Thus every prime through $3m$ divides the block product, so

$$
q(n,K)>3m>2.1\log n.
$$

Finally $n\to\infty$ as $m\to\infty$, giving infinitely many examples. This reconstruction is an elementary proof of the stated $2.1$ consequence, **not** a GCL-certified replay of the external Lean file, and it does not automatically establish any sharper constant.
