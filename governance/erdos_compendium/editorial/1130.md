## The question in plain language

Problem #1129 minimizes the **largest** value of an interpolation amplification function. [Erdős Problem #1130](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1130) considers a different, somewhat reversed quantity: the **smallest of its local peaks**.

Let $x_1<\cdots<x_n$ be distinct interpolation nodes in $[-1,1]$, and write $x_0=-1$, $x_{n+1}=1$. Let $L(x)$ be the Lagrange Lebesgue function. Define

$$
\lambda_i=\max_{x\in[x_i,x_{i+1}]}L(x),\qquad
\Upsilon=\min_{0\le i\le n}\lambda_i.
$$

How large can this minimum local maximum become as we vary the nodes? In particular, is $\Upsilon=O(\log n)$ uniformly?

## A small example

For two interior nodes $x_1=-1/2$, $x_2=1/2$, the basis polynomials give the exact identity

$$
L(x)=\max(1,2|x|),\qquad -1\le x\le1.
$$

The peak values in the three intervals are $2,1,2$, so

$$
\Upsilon(-1/2,1/2)=1,\qquad
\Lambda(-1/2,1/2)=2.
$$

This illustrates that the minimum of the interval maxima is **not** the same quantity as the global maximum.

## What is known

Erdős earlier proved a uniform upper bound of order $\sqrt n$ and predicted a sharper logarithmic result.

The maintained catalogue credits **de Boor and Pinkus (1978)** with the extremal characterization and, through the #1129 results, the estimate

$$
\Upsilon\le\frac2\pi\log n+O(1).
$$

A structural maximizer has a form of equioscillation: the local peaks are equal.

There is a source-definition subtlety. If endpoint nodes are permitted, then an outer interval can collapse to a point. Its peak is then exactly one, and the minimum is forced down. A primary source check must determine how the 1978 theorem handles endpoint or degenerate intervals before claiming an unconditional equivalence of minimizers and maximizers.
