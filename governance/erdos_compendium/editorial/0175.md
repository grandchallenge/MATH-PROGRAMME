## The question in plain language

The *central binomial coefficient* counts ways to choose $n$ objects from $2n$:

$$
\binom{2n}{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2}.
$$

A positive integer is **squarefree** when no square of a prime divides it. Erdős #175 asked for a proof that $\binom{2n}{n}$ is **not squarefree for every $n\ge5$**. Its numerical range and the choice of the *central* coefficient are essential.

## A small example

For $n=4$, $\binom84=70=2\cdot5\cdot7$ is squarefree, so the threshold five cannot be replaced by four. For $n=5$, $\binom{10}5=252=2^2\cdot3^2\cdot7$, and for $n=8$, $\binom{16}8=12870$ is divisible by $3^2$. The power-of-two input $n=8$ illustrates why an argument relying only on $4\mid\binom{2n}{n}$ is insufficient.

## What is known

The original assertion is **PROVED**. Erdős and later authors noticed that $4\mid\binom{2n}{n}$ unless $n$ is a power of two. The remaining case was settled for all $n\ge5$ independently by Velammal (1995) and Granville–Ramaré (1996), while Sárközy (1985) had covered sufficiently large $n$.

The key elementary identity follows from Kummer's theorem: the 2-adic valuation of $\binom{2n}{n}$ equals the number of ones in the binary expansion of $n$. Hence every $n$ that is **not** a power of two contributes at least two factors of two. If $n$ is a power of two, the 2-adic valuation equals exactly one, and a proof must find the square of **an odd prime**.

A stronger, separate question asks about $f(n)$, the largest valuation of a single prime in $\binom{2n}{n}$. Sander proved $f(n)\to\infty$; Erdős–Kolesnik improved its unconditional lower bound to $f(n)\gg(\log n)^{1/4-o(1)}$. Whether $f(n)\gg\log n$ for **all** $n$ remains **OPEN**, although it holds for almost all $n$. The settled squarefree statement does not settle this logarithmic question.

## What remains

A bounded arithmetic task computes $\binom{2n}{n}$ and its prime valuations exactly for $1\le n\le500$, checks Kummer's binary-popcount identity, and finds an explicit squared prime for all $n\ge5$ in that range. Acceptance: $n=4$ is correctly identified as an exception, and the power-of-two cases cannot be certified merely using the factor two. Stop at finite fixtures, not a proof for all $n$.
