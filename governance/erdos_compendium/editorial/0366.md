## The question in plain language

Problem 366 asks whether two consecutive positive integers can have an unusually strong divisibility property in a specified order. A positive integer is called full of order $k$ when every prime appearing in its factorization appears to exponent at least $k$. Thus the question is whether there is a positive $n$ such that $n$ has order $2$ and $n+1$ has order $3$. In the website's original wording, this is written as: if a prime $p$ divides $n$, then $p^2$ divides $n$, and if $p$ divides $n+1$, then $p^3$ divides $n+1$.

The word “prime” matters. Read literally for every divisor $p$, the sentence would describe a different property; the conventional and formalized reading is for prime divisors. The quantifier is existential: one witness settles the question positively. A proof that no witness exists would settle it negatively, but the current record supplies no such theorem or finite cutoff. “Verifiable” is therefore a catalogue status for an existential problem, not a claim that a witness, decision procedure, or proof is already available.

## A small example

The direction of the two conditions is easy to confuse. Exact arithmetic verifies the documented reversed pair

$$
12167=23^3,\qquad 12168=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot13^2.
$$

Every prime exponent in the first factorization is at least $3$, so $12167$ has both order $3$ and order $2$. The exponents in the second factorization are $3,2,2$, so $12168$ has order $2$ but not order $3$. Consequently this is a genuine reversed-order pair, not a solution to Problem 366. The smaller pair $8=2^3$ and $9=3^2$ has the same reversed orientation.

These identities were checked with exact integer multiplication and prime factorization; no floating-point estimate is involved. They illustrate the definitions and why an example from the opposite order cannot be promoted to an answer. They establish nothing about whether an integer of order $2$ can be followed by one of order $3$.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/366), read on 2026-10-13, calls the problem “VERIFIABLE Open, but could be proved with a finite example.” It reports no known positive solution and says that, using [OEIS A060355](https://oeis.org/A060355), there are no other relevant examples below $10^{22}$. The latter is a reported finite-search boundary: because an integer of order $3$ is automatically of order $2$, any solution would occur among consecutive powerful pairs. It is not a proof that no larger pair exists.

The primary historical source is P. Erdős, “Problems and results on number theoretic properties of consecutive integers and related questions,” *Proceedings of the Fifth Manitoba Conference on Numerical Mathematics* (1976), pp. 25–44, especially p. 31. In the readable scan, Erdős defines the relevant sequence of powerful numbers, records Mahler's observation that Pell solutions to $x^2-8y^2=1$ give infinitely many consecutive powerful numbers, and discusses further questions about gaps and consecutive powerful numbers. The source page does not transparently state the modern mixed order two/order three sentence in the same notation. The live entry also cites Erdős and Graham (1980, p. 68), so the exact historical attribution should remain qualified rather than reconstructed from memory.

The live discussion contains an ABC-conditional finiteness argument and a later unverified claim of an enormous explicit bound. Those are reports attached to the website, not an externally certified theorem in this audit. An ABC assumption would be a conditional result, not a proof in the ordinary unconditional setting; neither report decides the existence question.
