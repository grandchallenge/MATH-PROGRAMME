## The question in plain language

How long must we search through binomial coefficients before finding one with **no small prime divisors**?

[Erdős Problem #1095](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1095) defines $g(k)$ as the least integer $n>k+1$ such that every prime factor of $\binom nk$ is **larger than $k$**.

The question is how rapidly $g(k)$ grows and whether its values fluctuate dramatically as $k$ increases.

## A small example

Exact integer enumeration gives the following initial values, testing every eligible smaller $n$ against all primes $p\le k$:

| $k$ | $g(k)$ |
|---:|---:|
| 2 | 6 |
| 3 | 7 |
| 4 | 7 |
| 5 | 23 |
| 6 | 62 |
| 7 | 143 |
| 8 | 44 |
| 9 | 159 |

The drop from $g(7)=143$ to $g(8)=44$ is real. This arithmetic function is not necessarily monotone, even at small inputs.

These values are exact finite calculations, not evidence for an unbounded limsup or a zero liminf of successive ratios.

## What is known

Ecklund, Erdős and Selfridge introduced the problem in 1974. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1095) reports a current lower bound of the form

$$
g(k)\gg\exp\!\big(c(\log k)^2\big)
$$

due to Konyagin, and an upper bound with exponential growth in $k$.

The original conjectures include eventual comparison with the least common multiple $L_k=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,k)$ and statements about extreme oscillation:

$$
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{g(k+1)}{g(k)}=\infty,
\qquad
\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{g(k+1)}{g(k)}=0.
$$

These are **separate open questions**. A later heuristic suggests that $\log g(k)$ should have scale $k/\log k$, but that is not an established asymptotic theorem.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2366](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2366#issuecomment-6166440552) reproduces $g(k)$ exactly for $2\le k\le9$, respecting the strict condition $n>k+1$.

The frozen formal-conjectures representation defines the same threshold through the least prime factor, but its conjectural theorem bodies contain placeholders and do not constitute certified proofs.

## What remains

Read the 1974 original and the later Konyagin, Granville–Ramaré and Sorenson–Sorenson–Webster sources. Check every lower-bound dependency, distinguish proved estimates from heuristics, and independently replay prime valuations in finite cases.
