## The question in plain language

Imagine coloring a fixed positive proportion of the points in an enormous square integer grid. Can the colored points avoid containing the four corners of a smaller square?

[Erdős Problem #658](https://www.erdosproblems.com/658) asks whether, for every fixed density $\delta>0$, there is a sufficiently large side length $N$ beyond which **every** $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}^2$ with at least $\delta N^2$ points contains four vertices of a square.

The answer is **yes**. Mathematics proves something stronger: one can insist that the square's sides be **parallel to the coordinate axes**. Jozsef Solymosi gave a quantitative proof in 2004; an earlier qualitative result follows from Furstenberg and Katznelson's density Hales–Jewett theorem.

The historical question attributed to Erdős may have left the square's orientation unspecified. Proving the axis-parallel version removes that ambiguity for the existence claim without resolving exactly what Erdős wrote.

## A small example

In the $3\times3$ integer grid, take

$$
A=\{(1,1),(1,3),(3,1),(3,3)\}.
$$

These four points form an axis-parallel square with side length $d=2$. More generally, the required four corners have the form

$$
(a,b),\qquad(a+d,b),\qquad
(a,b+d),\qquad(a+d,b+d),\qquad d\geq1.
$$

The four distances around the boundary are all $d$, and the two diagonals are both $d\sqrt2$.

The phrase **sufficiently large** is essential. In the $2\times2$ grid, choose just the three points

$$
A_0=\{(1,1),(2,1),(1,2)\}.
$$

This already has density $3/4$, but three points cannot contain all four distinct vertices of any square. This does not contradict the theorem: for fixed $\delta$, the asserted conclusion holds only once $N$ passes a threshold $N_0(\delta)$.

A thin diagonal or a single row can also be square-free, but its density tends to zero as the grid grows. Such examples do not refute a fixed-positive-density theorem.

## What is known

The logical form of the result is

$$
\forall\delta>0\quad\exists N_0(\delta)\quad
\forall N\geq N_0(\delta)\quad
\forall A\subseteq[N]^2,
$$

$$
|A|\geq\delta N^2
\ \Longrightarrow\
\exists a,b,d\in\mathbb N,\ d>0:
\{(a,b),(a+d,b),(a,b+d),(a+d,b+d)\}\subseteq A.
$$

Because every displayed collection forms a Euclidean square, this is strictly **stronger in its conclusion** than merely asserting the existence of a square in an unspecified orientation.

The 1991 article of Hillel Furstenberg and Yitzhak Katznelson, [*A density version of the Hales–Jewett theorem*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03041066), *Journal d'Analyse Mathématique* **57**, 64–119, supplies a broader combinatorial density theorem. Its relevant geometric corollary says that positive-density grids contain homothetic copies of finite rectangular patterns, including the four corners of a square. Here a *homothetic copy* of an axis-aligned pattern means every coordinate is rescaled by the same positive factor and translated.

Jozsef Solymosi's [*A Note on a Question of Erdős and Graham*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0963548303005959), *Combinatorics, Probability and Computing* **13** (2004), 263–267, gave a quantitative proof for exactly these four corners. The Cambridge University Press abstract explicitly states that every sufficiently dense grid contains

$$
\{(a,b),(a+d,b),(a,b+d),(a+d,b+d)\}.
$$

The word *quantitative* means that the proof supplies an effective (though very large) dependence on $\delta$, not that a modest finite grid necessarily contains a square at a given density. This draft does not assert a specific numerical value for $N_0(\delta)$.
