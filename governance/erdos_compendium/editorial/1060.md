## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $k$, let $\sigma(k)$ be the sum of its positive divisors. Define

$$
f(n)=\#\{k\ge1:k\,\sigma(k)=n\}.
$$

[Erdős Problem #1060](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1060) asks whether this number of representations can grow only very slowly:

$$
f(n)\le n^{o(1/\log\log n)}.
$$

A stronger question is whether $f(n)$ is bounded above by some fixed power of $\log n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. These are statements about arbitrarily large $n$, not about a finite list of computations.

## A small example

The value $n=336$ has two representations:

$$
12\,\sigma(12)=12\cdot28=336,
\qquad
14\,\sigma(14)=14\cdot24=336.
$$

To prove that there are no others, note that $\sigma(k)\ge k$, so $k\,\sigma(k)\ge k^2$. Hence any solution must satisfy $k\le\lfloor\sqrt{336}\rfloor=18$. Direct divisor-sum enumeration for all $1\le k\le18$ establishes $f(336)=2$.

We extended this exact finite test to all $n\le65{,}536$. Two divisor-sum algorithms agreed for every $1\le k\le256$. The largest multiplicity observed was $2$, reached at $336$, $5952$, $10080$, $27776$, $44352$, $60480$ and $61152$. This is a rigorous, reproducible finite-range result, not an asymptotic theorem.

## What is known

The [canonical Erdős catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1060) records the problem as **open**, referring to Richard Guy's *Unsolved Problems in Number Theory*, problem B11. The full original Erdős source is not identified in the checked packet, and the precise edition and page of Guy's discussion still require source-level review.

The [frozen formal-conjectures transcription](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/1060.lean) represents $f(n)$ by counting $k\le n$ satisfying $k\,\sigma(k)=n$; the restriction is harmless because $k\le\sqrt n$. Its two proposed upper bounds are recorded as open statements, not completed proofs.

## What remains

The mathematical problem is to prove one of the proposed upper bounds, or find integers violating it. A useful bounded successor is to verify the complete collision list to larger cutoffs using independent exact algorithms, beginning with the least $n>65{,}536$ such that $f(n)\ge3$, if one exists. Historical source work must locate the precise original Erdős question and verify Guy's formulation. Finite observations alone cannot establish the conjectured growth rate.
