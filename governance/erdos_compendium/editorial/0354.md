## The question in plain language

Take two positive real numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ whose ratio is irrational. From each one, form the rapidly growing integer sequence

$$
\lfloor \alpha\rfloor,\lfloor 2\alpha\rfloor,\lfloor 4\alpha\rfloor,\ldots
$$

and the analogous sequence for $\beta$. “Complete” means that every sufficiently large natural number can be made by adding distinct available terms. The two sequences are treated as a multiset: a value appearing once in each sequence gives two usable occurrences. Equivalently, one asks whether there is an $N$ such that every $n\geq N$ has the form

$$
n=\sum_{s\in S}\lfloor 2^s\alpha\rfloor+\sum_{t\in T}\lfloor 2^t\beta\rfloor
$$

for finite sets $S,T\subseteq\mathbb N$.

The main question is whether this always happens under the irrational-ratio hypothesis. A second question replaces the base $2$ by one fixed real $\gamma$ with $1<\gamma<2$, and asks whether some such $\gamma$ works for every admissible pair $\alpha,\beta$. The original issue-bound statement and source packet are [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1624](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1624).

## A small example

Let $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=\sqrt 2$. Their ratio is irrational, so this is an instance of the first question. The first five terms from the $\alpha$ sequence are $1,2,4,8,16$. For the $\beta$ sequence, exact integer arithmetic gives $1,2,5,11,22$.

There is no floating-point approximation hidden here. For $k_n=\lfloor 2^n\sqrt2\rfloor$, it is enough to check

$$
k_n^2\leq 2\cdot 4^n<(k_n+1)^2.
$$

For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the verified values are $k_n=1,2,5,11,22$. Sorting the ten available occurrences gives $1,1,2,2,4,5,8,11,16,22$. Each term is at most one plus the sum of its predecessors, so the usual interval argument shows that their subset sums contain every integer from $0$ through $72$, their total sum. An exact integer check records $73$ reachable sums and no gaps.

This is a useful sanity check for the definitions and for the multiset convention. It is not evidence of eventual completeness: a finite initial segment cannot control the infinitely many later terms, and one pair $(1,\sqrt2)$ cannot settle a universal assertion.

## What is known

The primary source read for this audit is N. Hegyvári, “On complete sequences,” *Annales Universitatis Scientiarum Budapestinensis*, 34 (1991), 7–10, available in the journal’s [1991 volume PDF](https://annalesm.elte.hu/annales34-1991/Annales_1991_T-XXXIV.pdf). On page 7, Hegyvári defines completeness through finite sums of distinct sequence terms and proves that, for each fixed $\alpha>0$, the set of $\beta$ for which the paired sequence is incomplete is measurable and has either measure zero or infinite measure. His Lemma 1 on pages 8–9 gives a concrete sufficient condition: an already represented interval can be extended when the two next sequence terms leave gaps smaller than the interval’s starting threshold. The proof also uses the scaling implication that incompleteness for $(\alpha,2\beta)$ implies incompleteness for $(\alpha,\beta)$.

The live problem page attributes additional results to Hegyvári’s 1989 paper: completeness when exactly one parameter is dyadic, and non-completeness for special power-of-two-related pairs. The 1991 paper’s introduction independently records the dyadic case and a stronger conjectural direction, but the 1989 article itself was not read in this timebox. The maintained page also reports that Jiang and Ma, *International Journal of Number Theory* 20 (2024), 915–933, and Fang and He, *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* 175 (2025), 532–542, extend non-completeness results for power-of-two-related parameters with $1<\alpha<2$. Those are primary bibliographic records, but their full texts were not used here to certify a theorem beyond the stated report.

Thus the irrational-ratio universal assertion remains a live question in the audited sources. Results for dyadic or power-of-two-related parameters do not decide it, because those pairs are generally outside the irrational-ratio hypothesis.

## What remains

A bounded successor should first reconcile the pinned and live formal statements, then read one primary paper in full and extract a theorem-level interface for a selected parameter family. A suitable target is Hegyvári’s interval-extension mechanism: for explicit $\alpha,\beta$, compute the first indices where the hypotheses of Lemma 1 hold, prove the required floor inequalities exactly, and produce a finite interval of represented integers. Acceptance would require a checked chain of integer inequalities and a precise statement of which later terms are controlled. Falsification would be either a failed inequality, a missing distinct-term condition, or a counterexample to the claimed interval. Stop after one family and one complete interval certificate; do not extrapolate it to all irrational ratios or to the $\gamma$ variant.

The larger gap is conceptual: no audited source here supplies a theorem settling the universal irrational-ratio case. Any future claim that it does must identify the exact theorem, hypotheses, page, and parameter translation. A Lean compilation or a proof by `sorry` would not fill that gap.
