## The question in plain language

Take the integers from $1$ through $N$, and choose as many as possible while avoiding three different chosen integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ satisfying

$$
1/a = 1/b + 1/c.
$$

Write the largest possible size as $f(N)$. Erdős Problem 302 asks for the asymptotic size of $f(N)$: does it have a limiting density, and in particular is $f(N) = (1/2+o(1))N$? “Distinct” matters. If $b=c$ were allowed, the elementary identity $1/n = 1/(2n)+1/(2n)$ would change the problem completely. The intended ambient set is the positive integers in the finite interval $[1,N]$, with no ordering imposed on the three variables beyond their being pairwise different.

The equation is also a divisibility condition. Multiplying by $abc$ gives $bc=a(b+c)$, so a triple is forbidden exactly when $b+c$ divides $bc$ and the resulting quotient is $a$. This reformulation is useful for finite searches, but it does not turn the asymptotic extremal question into a finite computation.

The historical source is Erdős and Graham’s *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), Unit Fractions chapter. The accessible scan places the relevant discussion on printed pages 38–39 and explicitly compares the question with the odd integers. The maintained page attributes the catalogue entry to that source as [ErGr80].

## A small example

Here is an exact finite check, performed with rational arithmetic rather than floating-point approximations. For every three-element subset of $[1,12]$, I tested $1/a=1/b+1/c$ by cross-multiplying; then I tested every one of the $2^{12}$ subsets for the absence of a forbidden triple. The only forbidden triples are

$$
\{2,3,6\},\quad \{3,4,12\},\quad \{4,6,12\}.
$$

The exhaustive result is $f(12)=10$. One attaining set is $\{1,2,3,4,5,7,8,9,10,11\}$: it omits $6$ and $12$, thereby hitting all three forbidden triples. For example, the first triple is checked exactly by $1/2=1/3+1/6$, and the last by $1/4=1/6+1/12$.

This illustrates the finite hypergraph viewpoint: integers are vertices and unit-fraction triples are hyperedges, and $f(N)$ is the independence number. It establishes only the stated value at $N=12$. It neither estimates the limiting density nor validates any claimed asymptotic constant. A finite search can miss configurations that first appear at much larger scales.

## What is known

The basic lower bound is $f(N)\geq (1/2+o(1))N$. Two simple constructions give it: all odd integers up to $N$, or all integers in the upper half of the interval. In either case the equation cannot occur among three selected values for elementary parity or size reasons.

The maintained problem page records a stronger construction due to Stijn Cambie: take the odd integers at most $N/4$ together with all integers in $[N/2,N]$. It gives $f(N)\geq (5/8+o(1))N$, so the proposed half-density asymptotic is false as a specific conjecture, even though the general request to estimate $f(N)$ remains open.

The same page records Wouter van Doorn’s upper bound $f(N)\leq (9/10+o(1))N$, attributed to a short note titled “Two-colouring and density lead to solutions to an equation in unit fractions.” The note is an external source report, not a GCL-certified result in this article. A newer external repository by Kenta Kitamura reports a stronger upper constant, approximately $0.8461739827964009$, with a large Lean development and finite certificates. Its README says the result has not yet received independent human review. This audit did not build or replay it, so the claim is recorded as unverified external evidence.

The related colouring problem is different: Brown and Rödl proved that every finite colouring contains a monochromatic solution. That result does not settle the density extremal problem, because a large triple-free set is not the same object as a colour class in a fixed finite colouring.
