## The question in plain language

List the primes in increasing order as $p_1=2,p_2=3,p_3=5,\ldots$. Suppose someone fixes a starting position $n$ and a positive integer modulus $d$. Erdős Problem 427 asks whether one can always choose a **nonempty consecutive block of primes beginning immediately after that position** whose sum is divisible by $d$. In symbols, is there always a $k\geq1$ such that

$$
d\mid p_{n+1}+p_{n+2}+\cdots+p_{n+k}?
$$

The starting position is fixed; one may change the length of the block, not skip primes or rearrange them. Divisibility says that the sum has remainder zero after division by $d$. The modulus cannot be zero, because the sum is strictly positive.

This simple-sounding question joins two different subjects. It is about the remainders of partial sums, but its proof depends on the remarkable existence of arbitrarily long **runs of consecutive primes in one specified reduced residue class**. The answer to Erdős's original question is affirmative, by an argument attributed to Cédric Pilatte using a theorem of D. K. L. Shiu.

## A small example

Take $n=2$ and $d=6$. We must start at the third prime, $p_3=5$. The first four primes after our starting position are $5,7,11,13$. Their sum is

$$
5+7+11+13=36=6\cdot6.
$$

Hence $k=4$ works. The calculation is exact integer arithmetic. It demonstrates what a valid witness looks like but says nothing about *every* starting point and modulus.

There is a useful elementary observation: if we add a prime that is congruent to $1$ modulo $d$, the partial sum's remainder increases by $1$. Imagine encountering a run of $d$ consecutive primes all congruent to $1$ modulo $d$. Whatever the residue of the sum immediately before that run, adding some number from $0$ through $d-1$ of those primes makes the result divisible by $d$. If the sum is already divisible at the beginning, stop there. The preceding sum must be nonempty; we arrange this by choosing the run sufficiently far after the original starting point.

For example, if the preceding sum has residue $r=4$ modulo $6$, adding two primes each congruent to $1$ modulo $6$ gives residue $4+2=0$ modulo $6$. The important part is not that a particular small run exists, but that arbitrarily late runs of the required length exist for **every** modulus. That last assertion is Shiu's theorem, not a consequence of this small calculation.

## What is known

The problem is recorded in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980). It now has a positive resolution from a deeper published prime-number theorem.

D. K. L. Shiu proved [*Strings of Congruent Primes*](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0024610799007863), *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* 61(2) (2000), pp. 359–373. Shiu showed that for any modulus $q$, any residue class $a$ coprime to $q$, and any fixed block length $\ell\geq1$, there are infinitely many blocks of $\ell$ **consecutive primes** all congruent to $a$ modulo $q$. The [publisher's abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jlms/article-abstract/61/2/359/870705) expressly states the existence of arbitrarily long strings. This is stronger than finding many isolated primes in the same residue class: no primes of other classes may intervene inside the selected block.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #427](https://www.erdosproblems.com/427) entry, last edited January 22, 2026, credits Cédric Pilatte with the deduction. Apply Shiu with $q=d$, $a=1$ and $\ell=d$. Choose a run

$$
p_m,p_{m+1},\ldots,p_{m+d-1}
$$

of consecutive primes, each congruent to $1$ modulo $d$, with $m>n+1$. This is possible because Shiu's result supplies infinitely many such runs.

Let $S=p_{n+1}+\cdots+p_{m-1}$. Choose $r$ in the range $1\leq r\leq d$ with $S\equiv r\pmod d$, taking $r=d$ when the remainder is zero. Put $t=d-r$, so $0\leq t<d$. The sum of the original prefix followed by precisely $t$ primes from the selected run then satisfies

$$
S+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}p_{m+j}
\equiv r+t
=r+(d-r)
\equiv0\pmod d.
$$

If $t=0$, the added sum is empty, but the **overall** original prefix still has positive length since $m>n+1$. Otherwise the final prime used is $p_{m+t-1}$. This small residue calculation proves the target statement **conditional on Shiu's published theorem**. No assumption about the distribution of arbitrary prime gaps is additionally needed.

## What remains

For mathematical exposition, the strongest useful bounded task is to formalize the **deduction from Shiu**, treating Shiu's result as an explicit premise, and separately document the premise's original journal source. Check the modulus $d=1$, the exclusion of $d=0$, the positivity of the stopping length, and the final index when the number $t$ of primes added from the Shiu run is zero.

An executable fixture can calculate prime prefixes for all $0\leq n\leq20$ and $1\leq d\leq20$, stopping at the first divisible nonempty sum and recording $k$ and the exact quotient. Such a bounded search is not a proof of the universal statement; it only checks the indexing and arithmetic. Acceptance requires correct witnesses plus a source-to-formal-quantifier comparison and a proof of the elementary conditional residue lemma. Falsification includes an off-by-one index, an inadmissible empty overall sum, or treating a pinned placeholder as a compiled theorem. Stop after that interface verification rather than claiming a new prime-number theorem.
