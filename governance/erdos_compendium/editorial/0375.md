## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 375 is Grimm's conjecture about a run of consecutive composite numbers. Given integers $n,k ≥ 1$, suppose every number from $n+1$ through $n+k$ is composite. The question asks whether one can assign to each position $i$ a prime $p_i$ so that $p_i ∣ n+i$, with no prime used twice. Thus the assignment is a system of distinct prime divisors for the whole block, not merely a statement that each number has some prime divisor.

The distinction matters. A composite number can have several prime divisors, but choices made for one position can collide with choices for another. The conjecture says that a collision-free choice always exists. The live Erdős Problems page calls the problem “FALSIFIABLE”: it is treated as open, while a single finite counterexample would disprove it. That label is not a claim of decidability, independence, or truth in a specified formal theory. No source read for this article reports an independence theorem. The natural mathematical reading is the ordinary arithmetic statement above, with the usual meanings of prime, divisibility, and consecutive integer.

The wording is preserved from the live page and from the primary Erdős–Selfridge source. In particular, the divisibility target is $n+i$, while the composite block is $n+1, …, n+k$.

## A small example

Take $n=48$ and $k=4$. The block is $49,50,51,52$, and an exact factor check gives:

```text
factor 49 50 51 52
49: 7 7
50: 2 5 5
51: 3 17
52: 2 2 13
49%7=0, 50%5=0, 51%17=0, 52%13=0
```

The witnesses $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)=(7,5,17,13)$ are distinct primes and divide the corresponding entries. The same calculation also makes compositeness visible: each entry has at least two prime factors, counting multiplicity. This is a reproducible finite check, not a proof of the universal statement. It tests one block only; even a very large bounded computation would leave all larger or unsearched blocks untouched.

The first two lengths are elementary. For $k=1$, a composite number has a prime divisor. For $k=2$, if the two consecutive composites shared their only possible prime witness, that prime would divide their difference $1$, which is impossible. Larger blocks require controlling many possible collisions.

## What is known

The primary historical trail begins with C. A. Grimm's 1969 paper, “A conjecture on consecutive composite numbers,” and the 1971 paper by Paul Erdős and J. L. Selfridge, “Some problems on the prime factors of consecutive integers II,” pages 13–21. The latter source states the distinct-prime-divisor conjecture and studies related functions measuring how long the required separation can be maintained. Erdős and Graham list the problem in their 1980 monograph, with the catalogue citation pointing to page 71.

The current live page reports several partial results, each weaker than the full universal conjecture. It records Grimm's range $k ≪ log n / loglog n$, an improvement attributed to Erdős and Selfridge, and the Ramachandra–Shorey–Tijdeman result $k ≪ (log n / loglog n)^3$. The cited paper is “On Grimm's problem relating to factorisation of a block of consecutive integers,” Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik 273 (1975), 109–124, DOI 10.1515/crll.1975.273. The same page reports that Laishram and Shorey verified the full assignment for all $k ≥ 1$ when $n ≤ 1.9 × 10¹⁰$; their paper is “Grimm's conjecture on consecutive integers,” International Journal of Number Theory 2 (2006), 207–211, DOI 10.1142/S1793042106000498.

These are bounded-range or conditional-length results, not a resolution of every $n$ and $k$. This article treats the displayed bounds as reported literature claims tied to their references; it does not certify the proofs or independently reproduce the $1.9 × 10¹⁰$ computation.
