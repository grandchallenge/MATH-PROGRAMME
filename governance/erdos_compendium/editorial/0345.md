## The question in plain language

For a set $A$ of positive integers, form every finite sum in which each member of $A$ is used at most once:

$$
P(A)=\left\{\sum_{a\in B}a:B\subseteq A\text{ finite}\right\}.
$$

The set is called complete when every sufficiently large natural number belongs to $P(A)$. Its threshold $T(A)$ is the least point from which this happens. Problem 345 applies this to powers with exponent $k$, $A_k=\{1^k,2^k,3^k,\ldots\}$. It asks whether infinitely many positive exponents $k$ satisfy

$$
T(A_k)>T(A_{k+1}).
$$

In ordinary language: does increasing the exponent sometimes make the last unavoidable gap in the distinct-power subset sums move downward, and does that happen infinitely often? The powers are positive, and the subset sum is finite and distinct. The source uses $n^k$ as a description of the sequence, so it is important not to read $n$ as one fixed base. The formal version restricts $k$ to $k>0$; this avoids the zeroth-power set, which is just $\{1\}$ and is not complete.

## A small example

The first-power set contains every positive integer, so the empty sum gives $0$ and every $m>0$ is already a singleton sum. Under the nonnegative convention, $T(A_1)=0$.

Here is an exact finite check at the next exponent. The only squares that can occur in a sum at most $129$ are $1^2,\ldots,11^2$. The following dynamic program uses integers only and updates subset sums once for each square:

```python
values = [n*n for n in range(1, 12)]
reachable = {0}
for value in values:
    reachable |= {s + value for s in tuple(reachable)}
print(128 in reachable)
print(129 in reachable)
print(4 + 9 + 16 + 36 + 64)
```

The verified output is:

```text
False
True
129
```

Thus $128$ has no representation by distinct positive squares, while

$$
129=2^2+3^2+4^2+6^2+8^2.
$$

Because no square larger than $11^2$ can enter a sum for $128$, the negative check is exhaustive for that particular integer. It does not show that every integer at least $129$ is representable, so it is not by itself a proof that $T(A_2)=128$, still less evidence for infinitely many exponent descents.

## What is known

The original source is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 55, cited by the maintained problem page as [ErGr80]. It records the question and the known values

$$
T(A_1)=0,\quad T(A_2)=128,\quad T(A_3)=12758,\quad T(A_4)=5134240,\quad T(A_5)=67898771.
$$

The sequence of positive powers with exponent $k$ is complete for every positive $k$. A relevant primary source is Doyon Kim, “On the Largest Integer that is not a Sum of Distinct Positive powers with exponent $n$,” *Journal of Integer Sequences* 20 (2017), Article 17.7.5, arXiv:1610.02439, especially its introduction and Theorem 1. Kim states the earlier completeness theorem and proves an explicit upper bound for the threshold as a function of $n$. His introduction also reports the exact threshold values through $n=6$, including $T(A_6)=11146309947$. These results guarantee that the thresholds used in Problem 345 exist and give upper bounds, but they do not compare $T(A_k)$ and $T(A_{k+1})$ infinitely often.

The live page also notes the suggestion that powers of two may be promising exponents because of their restricted residues modulo a related power of two. That is a heuristic direction recorded by the source, not a theorem establishing the requested inequality.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-faithful threshold comparison for a bounded set of exponents. Its input should be a positive bound $K$ and exact subset-sum data for $A_k$ and $A_{k+1}$ through a declared integer cutoff. Its target should be either an exact pair of threshold certificates for one chosen $k$, or a rigorously stated obstruction showing that the cutoff cannot certify a threshold because an untested larger generator may matter.

The method can use dynamic programming over the finitely many powers not exceeding the cutoff. Acceptance requires exact integer arithmetic, a reconstruction of every claimed representation, and an explicit missing-sum certificate for every claimed lower bound. Falsification is immediate if a reported missing value is represented, a representation reuses a power, or the proposed threshold is tested below the cutoff without controlling larger powers. Stop after the fixed exponent and cutoff range, at the first semantic mismatch, or when the source assumptions needed for an unbounded theorem become unavoidable. This would generate bounded evidence only; it would not promote the catalogue status or prove the infinite assertion.
