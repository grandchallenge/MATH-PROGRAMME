## The question in plain language

For a prime $p$ and positive integers $k,m\geq2$, let $r(k,m,p)$ be the smallest positive integer $r$ such that the consecutive integers $r,r+1,\ldots,r+m-1$ are all nonzero residues that are powers of degree $k$ modulo $p$. The Erdős–Graham question of Lehmer and Lehmer concerns the eventual limsup $\Lambda(k,m)=\limsup_{p\to\infty}r(k,m,p)$. Does every fixed $k$ admit a bounded first pair, that is, finite $\Lambda(k,2)$? Is the same true of three consecutive residues for all odd $k$? The two questions are separate. For an individual small prime, a suitable run may not exist at all; the limsup question concerns sufficiently large primes, with bounds depending on $k$ rather than a bound uniform in $k$.

## A small example

For quadratic residues modulo $11$, the nonzero squares are $\{1,3,4,5,9\}$. They contain the consecutive pair $3,4$ and also $4,5$; thus $r(2,2,11)=3$. By contrast, none of the two-element blocks $1,2$ or $2,3$ consists entirely of squares, so the least start is genuinely $3$. This exact finite computation can be verified by squaring $1$ through $10$ modulo $11$. It does not determine a limsup over all primes. A single prime with a small first run gives an upper bound at that prime only. One must take account of $k$ th-power residue classes and, when $\gcd(k,p-1)>1$, the size of the subgroup of nonzero powers of degree $k$.

## What is known

Lehmer and Lehmer originally raised these questions in 1962 and Erdős–Graham recorded them in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980). They noted $\Lambda(2,2)=9$. Later computations and proofs gave $\Lambda(3,2)=77$ (Dunton), $\Lambda(4,2)=1224$ (Bierstedt–Mills), $\Lambda(5,2)=7888$ and $\Lambda(6,2)=202124$ (Lehmer–Lehmer–Mills), and $\Lambda(7,2)=1649375$ (Brillhart–Lehmer–Lehmer). These are specific fixed-exponent theorems, not a universal formula in $k$. Hildebrand proved in 1991 that $\Lambda(k,2)<\infty$ for every $k\geq2$, resolving the first qualitative question. Graham proved $\Lambda(k,m)=\infty$ when $m\geq4$, and earlier work shows infinite limsup for triples when $k$ is even. The important surviving question is finite $\Lambda(k,3)$ for odd $k\geq5$, together with quantitative growth in $k$. The maintained [Problem #436](https://www.erdosproblems.com/436), last edited October 25, 2025, describes precisely this split between solved and open portions.

**An elementary proof of the first-pair bound.** For each prime $p\geq7$, the numbers $1$, $4$, and $9$ are nonzero quadratic residues. If $2$ is a quadratic residue, the pair $1,2$ works. Otherwise, if $5$ is a quadratic residue, the pair $4,5$ works. If both $2$ and $5$ are nonresidues, their product $10$ is a residue because the product of two nonsquare classes is a square class; hence $9,10$ works. Thus $r(2,2,p)\leq9$ for all primes $p\geq7$. The finitely many excluded small primes do not affect the limit superior.

The bound is attained at $p=43$: $9\equiv3^2$ and $10\equiv15^2\pmod{43}$, and no earlier adjacent pair of nonzero squares occurs. That *one-prime* example does not establish the infinite-prime lower bound required for $\Lambda(2,2)=9$, which is a result already attributed to Lehmer and Lehmer. Neither the argument nor the example settles the open odd-exponent triple-residue question.

A useful structural test is to let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p$. The nonzero powers of degree $k$ form a subgroup of the multiplicative group $\mathbb F_p^\times$ with index $\gcd(k,p-1)$. Thus one may test whether $r$ lies in this subgroup by reducing discrete-log exponents modulo the index, or, without computing discrete logarithms, by checking $r^{(p-1)/\gcd(k,p-1)}\equiv1\pmod p$. The latter criterion holds for any nonzero residue and is convenient for independent cross-validation. It also shows why a prime with $\gcd(k,p-1)=1$ is exceptional: every nonzero residue is already a power of degree $k$, so the first run is trivially available. The hard primes are those where $k$ shares factors with $p-1$.

Moreover, the assertion that $\Lambda(k,3)$ is infinite for even $k$ is qualitatively different from Graham's universal length-four obstruction. The surviving odd-exponent triple problem is not resolved by either result. An article that omitted these distinctions would misstate the current literature even if all its small numerical examples were correct.

## What remains

A bounded experiment should fix $2\leq k\leq7$ and enumerate sufficiently many primes, computing the exact subgroup of residues that are powers of degree $k$ and the least consecutive blocks of lengths two, three and four. Compare the first-run maxima observed with the historical exact values, stating that finitely many primes cannot prove a global limsup. Separately read Hildebrand's theorem with its quantifiers and its dependency on the fixed exponent, and prepare a critical source table of the odd $k$ triple cases. Acceptance requires an independent residue enumerator, exact small-prime certificates, and correct attribution of each proved and open regime. The test fails if zero residues are counted as nonzero, if a finite maximum is called a limsup, or if Hildebrand's pair theorem is silently promoted to triples. Stop at this bounded source-and-computation interface.
