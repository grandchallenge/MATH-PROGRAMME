## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $N$, look at every subset $A$ of the first $N$ positive integers and keep the ones whose distinct reciprocal sum is exactly $1$. Write their number as

$$
a_N=\#\left\{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}:\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n=1\right\}.
$$

The denominators are distinct because $A$ is a set, and the equality is an exact rational equality. The question is about the growth of the sequence $(a_N)$ as $N$ tends to infinity. It is not asking whether one particular finite search finds a representation of $1$.

The sharp asymptotic reported in the inspected literature is

$$
a_N=2^{(c_1+o(1))N},
\qquad c_1\approx0.91117,
\qquad N\to\infty.
$$

In this notation, $o(1)$ means a real error term depending on $N$ that tends to zero. Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently large $N$ satisfy

$$
2^{(c_1-\varepsilon)N}\leq a_N\leq2^{(c_1+\varepsilon)N}.
$$

Thus the literature status is proved or resolved in this asymptotic sense. It does not give an exact formula for every finite $N$, nor an explicit error bound in the displayed statement. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry for #297](https://www.erdosproblems.com/297) and the authenticated source audit identify Erdős and Graham’s 1980 book, p. 36, as the original reference. The audit did not access a scan of that page, so the historical pointer is retained without pretending that the original wording was independently reread here.

## A small example

At $N=6$, the two solutions are

$$
\{1\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\{2,3,6\},
$$

because

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac16=\frac{3+2+1}{6}=1.
$$

The following exhaustive check uses exact `Fraction` arithmetic. It examines all $2^6=64$ subsets, so it does not rely on decimal rounding.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

solutions = []
for mask in range(1 << 6):
    A = tuple(n for n in range(1, 7) if mask & (1 << (n - 1)))
    total = sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in A), Fraction(0))
    if total == 1:
        solutions.append(A)

print(solutions)
print(len(solutions))
```

The output is

```text
[(1,), (2, 3, 6)]
2
```

This is an independently reproducible finite fact: $a_6=2$. The same exact calculation is recorded in the authenticated [RESULT/1 source audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1567#issuecomment-6138000569). It is useful for checking the statement and notation, but a finite value cannot prove an asymptotic formula.

## What is known

Stefan Steinerberger’s [arXiv:2403.17041v5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17041) gives an earlier exponential upper bound. Its theorem, on PDF p. 1, proves that for sufficiently large $N$,

$$
\#\left\{A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}:\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\leq1\right\}\leq2^{0.93N}.
$$

This is an upper bound for a larger family, since “at most $1$” includes subsets whose sum is less than $1$. It therefore bounds $a_N$, but it is not the sharp answer to #297.

Yang P. Liu and Mehtaab Sawhney’s [arXiv:2404.07113v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07113) states the sharp rate in Theorem 1.2 on PDF p. 1. They define the unique positive $\lambda^\ast$ satisfying

$$
\int_0^1\frac{e^{-\lambda^\ast/x}}{1+e^{-\lambda^\ast/x}}\frac{dx}{x}=1
$$

and then set

$$
\gamma^\ast=\lambda^\ast+\int_0^1\log\left(1+e^{-\lambda^\ast/x}\right)\,dx.
$$

Their conclusion is

$$
e^{\gamma^\ast N-o(N)}\leq a_N\leq e^{\gamma^\ast N+o(N)},
$$

with $\gamma^\ast\approx0.631573$ and $e^{\gamma^\ast}\approx1.88057$.

Conlon, Fox, He, Mubayi, Pham, Suk, and Verstraëte give the more general [Theorem 1 in arXiv:2404.16016v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16016), published as *Discrete Analysis* 2025:28. For each fixed rational $x>0$, the number of subsets with reciprocal sum $x$ is

$$
2^{(c_x+o(1))N},
$$

where

$$
c_x=\int_0^1h\left(\frac1{1+e^{\lambda/y}}\right)\,dy
$$

and $\lambda$ is the unique real number satisfying

$$
\int_0^1\frac{dy}{y(1+e^{\lambda/y})}=x.
$$

For $x=1$, they report $c_1\approx0.91117$. These primary papers support “proved asymptotically”; this article does not claim to have independently reconstructed their analytic or combinatorial proofs.

## What remains

The mathematical resolution reported by the primary papers is asymptotic, not finite and explicit. A reader wanting exact values of $a_N$ for particular moderate $N$ needs a separate finite computation; the theorem does not provide those values or a usable error term for finite $N$. A reader wanting formal verification needs first to state the exact predicate for finite subsets and then formalize the asymptotic quantifiers, including the meaning of the constant $c_1$ and the $o(1)$ term.

The main documentary task is provenance reconciliation: preserve the unavailable 1980 page as an unavailable source, retain the inspected theorem locations and version identifiers, and avoid upgrading catalogue metadata into a formal result. The two sharp papers use different logarithm bases and constants, but their statements describe the same exponential rate after the conversion $c_1=\gamma^\ast/\log 2$; this comparison is a normalization, not a new proof.

No claim is made here that the Programme has replayed either primary proof, compiled a complete formal development, or certified the asymptotic result. The finite certificate above validates only the $N=6$ instance.
