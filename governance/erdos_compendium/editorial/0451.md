## The question in plain language

Imagine $k$ consecutive positive integers. We would like all of them to avoid prime divisors in a particular range: primes strictly bigger than $k$ but strictly smaller than $2k$.

For integers $k\geq2$ and $n>2k$, form

$$
P_k(n)=(n-k)(n-k+1)\cdots(n-1).
$$

Define $n_k$ as the **smallest such $n$** for which no prime $p$ satisfying $k<p<2k$ divides $P_k(n)$.

Erdős Problem #451 asks: **How fast does $n_k$ grow as $k$ increases?** The difficulty is not that one cannot check a proposed $n$. That is straightforward. The difficulty is proving how long one must search before the first prime-avoiding block can appear.

Erdős expected two things: $n_k$ should grow faster than every fixed polynomial $k^d$, but more slowly than $e^{\varepsilon k}$ for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, at least for sufficiently large $k$. These are distinct conjectural bounds. Knowing one does not automatically establish the other.

## A small example

For $k=5$, the only prime strictly between $5$ and $10$ is $7$. We need the first $n>10$ for which none of the five numbers $n-5,\ldots,n-1$ is divisible by $7$.

- At $n=11$, the block is $6,7,8,9,10$, which contains $7$.
- At $n=12$, the block is $7,8,9,10,11$, again containing $7$.
- At $n=13$, the block is $8,9,10,11,12$, none of which is divisible by $7$.

Hence **$n_5=13$**. This is an exact check of all earlier candidates, not an approximation.

A small enumeration using prime divisibility gives

| $k$ | Primes $p$ with $k<p<2k$ | $n_k$ |
|---|---|---:|
| $2$ | $3$ | $6$ |
| $3$ | $5$ | $9$ |
| $4$ | $5,7$ | $20$ |
| $5$ | $7$ | $13$ |

The sequence need not increase at every step. Increasing $k$ simultaneously lengthens the forbidden block and changes which primes are forbidden.

There is a general construction showing that a qualifying $n$ always exists. Set

$$
M_k=\prod_{\substack{k<p<2k\\p\text{ prime}}}p.
$$

If $n$ is any multiple of $M_k$, then for a prime $p$ in this product and $1\leq i\leq k$, we have

$$
n-i\equiv-i\not\equiv0\pmod p
$$

because $0<i<p$. No forbidden prime can divide $P_k(n)$. In particular, taking the least multiple of $M_k$ exceeding $2k$ yields the unconditional bound

$$
n_k\leq M_k\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2k}{M_k}\right\rfloor+1\right).
$$

For sufficiently large $k$, $M_k>2k$, so the right-hand side reduces to $M_k$. The prime number theorem gives $M_k=e^{(1+o(1))k}$. This upper bound establishes finite existence but is too large to prove Erdős's hoped-for subexponential upper estimate.

## What is known

The original discussion by Erdős and Graham (1980, printed p. 89) recorded a lower bound of the form $n_k>k^{1+c}$ for some $c>0$ and suggested that much more should be true.

A **major subsequent result** appeared in June 2026. In the preprint *Consecutive integers free of certain prime factors*, Wouter van Doorn and Quanyu Tang prove that for all sufficiently large $k$,

$$
\boxed{
n_k>\exp\!\left(\frac{(\log k)^2}{20\log\log k}\right).
}
$$

This is a substantial advance because its exponent dominates $d\log k$ for every fixed $d>0$. Consequently, for every such $d$,

$$
n_k>k^d
$$

once $k$ is sufficiently large. Thus the published lower bound establishes **the superpolynomial-growth prediction**, not the full determination of the growth rate.

The paper actually proves a slightly more precise theorem (Theorem 1.1). With a suitable exponent $\theta=21/40$, every sufficiently large $k$ and every $n$ satisfying

$$
2k<n\leq
\exp\!\left(\frac{(\log k)^2}{20\log\log k}\right)
$$

has a prime factor $p\in(k,k+3k^\theta)$ in its product $P_k(n)$. Because $3k^\theta<k$ for sufficiently large $k$, this prime lies inside the original forbidden interval $(k,2k)$.

The argument uses estimates on primes in short intervals and a more difficult analytic bound influenced by Konyagin's work. The authors report that a language model suggested an approach subsequently developed in a human-written paper, and that external Lean formalizations are available. Those provenance statements are not substitutes for a GCL proof replay.

What remains unresolved is the sharper quantitative size of $n_k$, including the proposed upper bound $n_k<e^{\varepsilon k}$ for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $k$. Therefore the maintained problem is properly classified as **open**, even though an important component of Erdős's prediction has been proved.

## GCL's current state

GCL's [authenticated #451 source audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1721#issuecomment-6143054330) directly examined the original Erdős–Graham question and the June 2026 primary preprint, including its abstract and Theorem 1.1.

The elementary product-of-primes upper construction and small finite examples above were checked independently. The main analytic lower-bound theorem was **read and attributed**, not independently proved, Lean-compiled, or mathematically certified by GCL.

The historical registry's generic `open` status is consistent with the present broader question, but a reader should not overlook the newer, proved 2026 lower bound. This distinction matters: a problem can remain open after a conjectured partial result has become a theorem.

## What remains

A narrowly bounded verification project can reconstruct the elementary range of van Doorn–Tang's argument in its §§2–3, checking all strict inequalities, hypotheses on $k$, and the short-prime-interval input. A separate, higher-assurance project would analyze its use of Konyagin's theorem and reproduce the external Lean evidence with exact version and axiom receipts.

On the mathematical side, the main target is to improve the upper bound or sharpen the lower bound enough to identify the true growth scale of $n_k$. Nothing in the finite enumeration or the elementary construction resolves this asymptotic problem.
