## The question in plain language

Can a given number be assembled by adding **different divisors** of another number? For example, $5$ is the sum $2+3$, and both $2$ and $3$ divide $6$. Therefore $6$ belongs to the set of integers whose distinct divisors can add up to $5$. The number $5$ itself belongs too, since it is a divisor of itself.

For each target $t\ge1$, let $E_t$ be the set of positive integers $n$ for which some set of distinct divisors of $n$ sums to $t$. Erdős [Problem #859](https://www.erdosproblems.com/859) concerns the *natural density* of this set:

$$
d_t=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{|E_t\cap\{1,\ldots,X\}|}{X}.
$$

The question is whether there are constants $c_1,c_2>0$ such that

$$
d_t\sim\frac{c_1}{(\log t)^{c_2}}\qquad(t\to\infty).
$$

The notation $\sim$ asks for a ratio tending to one, not merely upper and lower bounds of a similar general shape. The target $t$ varies in this limit; for each fixed $t$, the density is a well-defined number.

## A small example

For $t=3$, there are two ways to represent the target using distinct positive integers: $3$ and $1+2$. A number $n$ therefore belongs to $E_3$ exactly when $3$ divides $n$ or $2$ divides $n$. By inclusion–exclusion,

$$
d_3=\frac13+\frac12-\frac16=\frac23.
$$

For $t=5$, the minimal divisor patterns are $5$, $1+4$, and $2+3$. They require $n$ to be divisible by $5$, $4$, or $6$, respectively. Inclusion–exclusion gives $d_5=7/15$.

Independent exact calculations yield:

| $t$ | Density $d_t$ |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | $1$ |
| 2 | $1/2$ |
| 3 | $2/3$ |
| 4 | $1/2$ |
| 5 | $7/15$ |
| 6 | $7/15$ |
| 7 | $16/35$ |
| 8 | $3/7$ |
| 9 | $17/45$ |
| 10 | $2/5$ |
| 11 | $13/33$ |
| 12 | $43/99$ |

The sequence is not monotone at these small values. A dozen exact answers cannot determine an asymptotic power or leading constant.

## What is known

Erdős proved in [1970, printed pages 130–131](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1970-21.pdf) that $d_t$ exists for every $t$ and tends to zero as $t$ increases. He also established positive lower and upper bounds involving negative powers of $\log t$. His question asks for the sharper assertion of a single asymptotic equivalent with a fixed exponent and constant. The maintained catalogue continues to mark it **open**.

The existence of each density is much easier than its behavior as $t\to\infty$. Every summand in a representation of $t$ is at most $t$. Put

$$
L_t=\mathrm{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,t).
$$

For any selection of distinct positive integers $S$ summing to $t$, the least common multiple $m_S$ of its elements divides $L_t$. All elements of $S$ divide $n$ exactly when $m_S$ divides $n$. Thus $E_t$ is a finite union of sets of multiples of integers dividing $L_t$.

Membership in $E_t$ is therefore **periodic modulo $L_t$**. In particular,

$$
d_t=\frac{\#\{1\le n\le L_t:n\in E_t\}}{L_t},
$$

so every $d_t$ is rational. This is an elementary strengthening of the period argument implicit in Erdős's original proof; it is not presented as a novel theorem. The difficulty lies in extracting a simple uniform asymptotic from increasingly complicated exact periodic descriptions.

## What remains

The first bounded formal task is to prove that the divisor-subset definition is equivalent to divisibility by one of finitely many least-common-multiple witnesses. This yields exact periodicity, rationality, and positive density, including the simple lower bound $d_t\ge1/t$ because every multiple of $t$ contains $t$ as a divisor. The finite tests above are acceptance fixtures, not substitutes for a general proof.

The harder research boundary is to understand how the minimal witness families grow with $t$ and how their overlapping sets of multiples interact. The number of witnesses and their arithmetic dependencies change substantially with the target. Any proof of a power-law asymptotic must control these overlaps uniformly, rather than fit an exponent to a short table.

A formal implementation must also resolve the boundary conventions for $t=0$, $n=0$, and natural density. These are bookkeeping issues, but mismatched conventions can invalidate a claimed equivalence even when all positive examples work.
