## The question in plain language

A **maximal clique** in a finite simple graph is a set of vertices that are all pairwise adjacent and that cannot be enlarged by adding another vertex. Here *maximal* means **not contained in a larger complete subgraph**, not necessarily a clique of the largest size in the whole graph.

Let $g(n)$ be the greatest number of **different cardinalities** among the maximal cliques of a graph on $n$ vertices. The number of maximal cliques themselves may be exponentially large; the question counts only how many *different sizes* occur.

Erdős asked whether

$$
g(n)=n-\log_2n-\log_*n+O(1),
$$

where $\log_*n$ denotes the number of successive base-two logarithms needed to reduce $n$ below a fixed small threshold.

This is [Erdős Problem #927](https://www.erdosproblems.com/927), **disproved by Joel Spencer in 1971**. The correct asymptotic is

$$
g(n)=n-\log_2n+O(1).
$$

Thus Erdős's extra iterated-logarithm deficit is unnecessary and tends to infinity, so it cannot be absorbed in an additive constant.

## A small example

Consider a graph consisting of a triangle and one isolated vertex. The triangle is a maximal clique of size three, and the isolated vertex is a maximal clique of size one. Consequently the graph has two **different maximal-clique sizes**, even though it contains ordinary complete subgraphs of sizes one, two and three.

In contrast, the complete graph $K_n$ has only one maximal clique, of size $n$. Its many complete subgraphs are not maximal. Confusing a **complete subgraph** with an **inclusion-maximal** complete subgraph would make the question trivial and lose all of its mathematical content.

For six vertices, an exact enumeration of every labelled graph finds that the maximum possible number of different maximal-clique sizes is **three**. The values from exhaustive search on one through six vertices are

$$
g(1),\ldots,g(6)=1,1,2,2,3,3.
$$

These small values clarify the definition but do not establish Spencer's asymptotic construction.

## What is known

J. W. Moon and Leo Moser, [*On cliques in graphs*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02760024), *Israel Journal of Mathematics* **3** (1965), pp.23–28, derived strong upper and lower estimates for the number of **distinct sizes** of maximal cliques. Their Theorem 4, printed pp.27–28, gives the universal upper bound

$$
g(n)\le n-\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor
\quad\text{for }n\ge4.
$$

GCL inspected the original paper's Theorem 4 and proof pages, available in [the six-page reproduction](https://niplav.site/doc/math/on_cliques_in_graphs_moon_moser_1965.pdf). The proof is a simple injection into subsets outside a largest clique, explained below.

Paul Erdős, [*On cliques in graphs*](https://combinatorica.hu/~p_erdos/1966-08.pdf), *Israel Journal of Mathematics* **4** (1966), pp.233–234, strengthened Moon and Moser's lower construction to

$$
g(n)\ge n-\log_2n-\log_*n-O(1).
$$

The paper explicitly defines **clique** as an inclusion-maximal complete subgraph and says that its refined lower bound seems close to best possible. The historical source also appears in Erdős's [1971 list of graph-theory problems](https://www.erdosproblems.com/927), item 9 on printed p.101. The conjectured iterated-logarithm correction came from this constructive evidence, not from a proven general upper restriction.

Joel H. Spencer, [*On cliques in graphs*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/927), *Israel Journal of Mathematics* **9** (1971), pp.419–421, gave a sharper construction satisfying

$$
g(n)\ge n-\log_2n-O(1).
$$

Combining Spencer's lower bound with Moon–Moser's upper bound yields the true asymptotic $n-\log_2n+O(1)$. The exact bibliographic source is corroborated by [Spencer's university curriculum vitae](https://cs.nyu.edu/~spencer/papers/vita.pdf), listing the article among his 1971 publications, and by the reference to [Sp71] in the related [#775](https://www.erdosproblems.com/775) graph-versus-hypergraph comparison. The 1971 original proof pages were **not** independently obtained and replayed in this GCL batch; the quantitative lower bound is imported from that refereed publication and the curated source record.

The nearby hypergraph question **#775** is genuinely different. In uniform hypergraphs of rank three, the deficit in the number of maximal-clique sizes has behavior unlike the graph case; a 2026 preprint develops further refinements. One cannot transfer Spencer's graph construction to that hypergraph question by merely changing the ambient word *graph* to *hypergraph*.

The [maintained #927 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/927) labels Erdős's proposed formula DISPROVED. A separate site label on a related problem may report a Lean formalization, but no source-matched, locally compiled proof of Spencer's asymptotic result has been verified here.

## GCL's current state

The key **Moon–Moser injection** can be reconstructed independently. Fix a largest complete subgraph $T$ in an arbitrary graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, and put $S=V(G)\setminus T$. If $C$ is a maximal clique, then $C$ is completely determined by the intersection $C\cap S$. Indeed, a vertex $t\in T$ belongs to $C$ if and only if $t$ is adjacent to **every vertex of $C\cap S$**: one direction is automatic, and the other follows because all vertices of $T$ are mutually adjacent, so excluding an eligible $t$ would contradict maximality.

Consequently the number of distinct maximal cliques, and hence the number of their distinct sizes, is at most $2^{|S|}$. Independently, their sizes cannot exceed $|T|$. Therefore, when a maximum clique has size $t$, one has

$$
\#\{\text{maximal-clique sizes}\}
\le\min\{t,2^{n-t}\}.
$$

The elementary two-case argument in Moon and Moser's Theorem 4 then gives $g(n)\le n-\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor$ for $n\ge4$. This is a rigorous **unconditional upper bound**, and it does not require Spencer's later construction.

The executable program governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_926_927.py independently enumerates **every one of 33,867 labelled simple graphs on one through six vertices**. For every nonempty vertex subset it checks whether it is complete and cannot be extended by a vertex; it then records the distinct sizes of all inclusion-maximal cliques. For all graphs with at least four vertices it verifies the Moon–Moser upper bound. It also retains an explicit graph witness attaining each computed small-order maximum and verifies all definitions without substituting maximum clique for maximal clique.

The finite results do not constitute an independent reconstruction of Spencer's lower construction, since an asymptotic bound that defeats $\log_*n$ requires arbitrarily large graph orders.

## What remains

The first bounded formalizable obligation is the **maximal-clique restriction injection**: for a fixed complete subgraph $T$, prove that inclusion-maximal cliques are uniquely determined by their intersections with $V(G)\setminus T$. Derive the $2^{n-|T|}$ counting bound, the minimum with $|T|$ and the Moon–Moser upper estimate, including all floor and threshold conventions. Acceptance requires a complete elementary proof or no-placeholder, source-matched Lean with literal axioms, and exact replay of all 33,867 finite graph fixtures. A pair of distinct maximal cliques with the same intersection outside $T$, or a formula counting all complete subgraphs rather than maximal ones, would falsify the interface.

The independent source-reconstruction task is Spencer's **1971 constructive lower bound**. Retrieve his original *Israel Journal of Mathematics* pp.419–421, specify its graph family and vertex counts exactly, show it realizes at least $n-\log_2n-C$ distinct maximal-clique cardinalities for all sufficiently large $n$, and determine how small-order or rounding cases are padded. The full proof requires verifying maximality of each clique size, not merely locating complete subgraphs of different orders. Until the original construction has been independently reconstructed, credit the quantitative lower theorem to Spencer rather than to new GCL research.

The historical conjecture with a negative $\log_*n$ term is disproved, but exact values of $g(n)$ at individual large orders and hypergraph analogues are separate problems. No mathematical discovery or machine certification is claimed by the finite enumeration alone.
