## The question in plain language

The divisor-counting function $\tau(n)$ gives the number of positive divisors of an integer. If $n!$ is a factorial, then increasing $n$ changes its prime factorization, and with it the number of divisors. Erdős Problem 420 asks about the **growth of the divisor count over a short interval of consecutive factorials**, rather than only one step as in Problem 419.

For a nonnegative real-valued function $f(n)$, define

$$
F(f,n)=
\frac{\tau\bigl((n+\lfloor f(n)\rfloor)!\bigr)}
     {\tau(n!)}.
$$

The floor is important: the factorial argument is an integer. The question has several logically distinct parts. Does $F((\log n)^C,n)$ tend to infinity for all sufficiently large fixed constants $C$? Is the collection of values of $F(\log n,n)$ eventually dense throughout the real interval $(1,\infty)$? What if $f$ increases monotonically to infinity but never exceeds $\log n$? A convergence claim, a liminf claim, and a density claim are different. None follows simply from the existence of infinitely many large values.

## A small example

The prime factorization of a positive integer $N=\prod p^{a_p}$ gives

$$
\tau(N)=\prod_{p\mid N}(a_p+1).
$$

For $n=5$, the base factorial is $5!=120=2^3\cdot3\cdot5$, so $\tau(5!)=16$. With a one-step shift, $6!=720=2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5$ and $\tau(6!)=30$. Thus

$$
F(1,5)=\frac{30}{16}=\frac{15}{8}.
$$

With a two-step shift, $7!=5040=2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7$ has $60$ divisors, giving

$$
F(2,5)=\frac{60}{16}=\frac{15}{4}.
$$

The computation is exact, using integer prime exponents and rational arithmetic. The two numbers show how an additional factorial factor can change divisor counts quite abruptly. No conclusion about asymptotic density follows from two finite values.

For fixed $n$, the function $t\mapsto\tau((n+t)!)$ is nondecreasing in nonnegative integers $t$, because each new factorial is obtained by multiplying by an integer, which increases prime exponents without decreasing any. Consequently $F(f,n)\geq1$ and is monotone as the integer shift increases. This elementary statement does not imply uniform growth as $n$ tends to infinity: the denominator changes simultaneously.

## What is known

In the original Erdős–Graham *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **printed page 83**, the authors consider factorial divisor ratios after adding several factors. They already knew substantial growth for shifts comparable to square roots of $n$ and raised questions about much shorter, logarithmic shifts. The text is directly visible in the [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf).

The main relevant later primary paper is P. Erdős, S. W. Graham, A. Ivić and C. Pomerance, *On the number of divisors of $n!$*, in *Analytic Number Theory*, Progress in Mathematics 138 (1996), pp.337–355, [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-4086-0_19). Their work on the factorial divisor function also resolves adjacent Problem #419, but the claims for #420 have to be stated separately.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #420](https://www.erdosproblems.com/420) page attributes three important results to the 1996 paper. For any fixed $c>0$ it reports

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}F(c\log n,n)=1.
$$

It also reports that $F(n^{4/9},n)$ tends to infinity, and that if $f(n)=o((\log n)^2)$ then $F(f,n)\sim1$ for **almost all** integers $n$. The qualifier “almost all” does not mean every sufficiently large integer; a sparse subsequence could behave very differently. These theorems leave the intermediate regime involving $(\log n)^C$ and the value-density questions unresolved in their stated form.

The maintained discussion also connects large jumps of the ratio to primes in short intervals. A single large prime entering the factorial can nearly double the divisor count; multiple such entries can multiply the effect. Heuristics and conditional statements involving prime gaps are informative but are not unconditional solutions of the precise density questions.

## What remains

The most useful small successor task is a reproducible finite program to compute factorial divisor ratios by two independent methods. For integers $5\leq n\leq100$ and shifts $0\leq t\leq10$, compute $\tau((n+t)!)/\tau(n!)$ from prime exponent vectors; separately obtain the same rational by multiplying the one-step ratios from $n$ to $n+t-1$. Confirm exact equality for every case and check $F(1,5)=15/8$ and $F(2,5)=15/4$.

The second part is source-semantic: transcribe the relevant 1996 theorem and corollary hypotheses and distinguish convergence for almost all $n$, the liminf for logarithmic shifts, and full convergence for polynomial shifts. The successor passes if all finite identities agree and the statement table matches the primary text; it fails if an integer floor is omitted, a denominator exponent is wrong, or an almost-everywhere result is silently promoted to everywhere.
