## The question in plain language

Is the beginning of the number line the place where a fixed-length interval contains the most primes? Let $\pi(t)$ count the primes at most $t$. The **second Hardy–Littlewood conjecture**, recorded as [Erdős Problem #855](https://www.erdosproblems.com/855), concerns the inequality

$$
\pi(x+y)-\pi(x)\le\pi(y).
$$

The left side counts primes in the translated interval $(x,x+y]$; the right side counts primes in $(0,y]$. Erdős asked whether this holds when the parameters are sufficiently large. Its ordinary uniform reading requires two fixed thresholds, beyond which it holds for **every** pair of parameter values.

This looks plausible because primes become sparser on average. The subtlety is that an interval far out among the integers might contain an unusually rich cluster of primes. Average density cannot forbid exceptional clusters.

## A small example

For $x=100$ and $y=20$, direct enumeration gives

$$
\pi(100)=25,\qquad \pi(20)=8,\qquad\pi(120)=30.
$$

Consequently the translated interval $(100,120]$ contains five primes, versus eight in $(0,20]$. The inequality is satisfied in this example, since $30\le25+8$.

One example proves nothing about arbitrarily large parameters. Likewise, even a finite violation of the **all-parameters** historical version would not by itself disprove an assertion requiring violations arbitrarily far out in both variables.

## What is known

In his [1985 paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1985-17.pdf), printed pages 80–81, Erdős reported that Hensley and Richards had shown the original inequality incompatible with the Hardy–Littlewood *prime-tuples conjecture*. Under that additional conjecture, they obtained systematic excesses in prime-rich intervals, rather than a proof of an unconditional contradiction. In particular, their conditional work yields excesses of order $y/(\log y)^2$ for sufficiently large $y$ and infinitely many appropriate starting positions $x$.

Erdős also recorded Straus's proposed replacement,

$$
\pi(x+y)\le\pi(x)+2\pi(y/2),
$$

and a separate conjecture that the original inequality holds for density-one of the starting positions $x$, simultaneously over shorter values of $y$. Erdős reported only positive *lower* density for this last type of good starting position, which is weaker than density one.

A well-established upper bound from Montgomery and Vaughan's large-sieve work controls primes in short intervals on the scale $2y/\log y$ (under the relevant range assumptions). This coefficient is not the conjectured count $\pi(y)$ and does not resolve the exact problem. The general question remains marked open by the maintained catalogue.

## GCL's current state

The [authenticated source audit #2126](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2126#issuecomment-6143263453) compared Erdős's printed formulation with the protected [Formal Conjectures #855](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/855.lean). It identified a potentially important mismatch in the order of the quantifiers.

The frozen Lean file uses *nested eventuality*: first sufficiently large $x$, then sufficiently large $y$, with the threshold for $y$ potentially depending on $x$. The ordinary uniform reading instead fixes a threshold for each variable independently. To see the difference without doing any number theory, consider the elementary predicate $P(x,y)$ meaning $y\ge x$. For every fixed $x$, the predicate is true for all sufficiently large $y$. Yet no two independent thresholds make it true for every large $x$ and every large $y$.

Thus the two logical forms cannot simply be identified. This does **not** exhibit a counterexample to the prime-counting inequality. It identifies a statement-fidelity issue requiring resolution before a formalized result could be interpreted as settling the intended question. The frozen Lean theorem also contains placeholders, not a completed proof.

## What remains

First, the mathematical status of the eventual inequality must be separated carefully from the conditional incompatibility with the prime-tuples conjecture and from variants requiring the inequality for all smaller $y$. The precise hypotheses, quantified ranges, and source pages of the Hensley–Richards result require a full primary-paper check.

Second, the proposed formal theorem should be compared directly with the intended two-variable quantifiers. A bounded successor task can build a small Lean countermodel showing that nested eventuality need not be uniform, then either correct the statement or justify the intended interpretation from the historical sources. That is semantic quality control, not a proof of either side of the number-theoretic conjecture.
