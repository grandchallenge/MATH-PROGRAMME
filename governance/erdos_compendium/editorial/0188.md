## The question in plain language

Colour every point of the Euclidean plane red or blue, subject to the restriction that **no two red points have distance exactly one**. Erdős and Graham asked how long a **unit-spaced blue arithmetic progression** can be avoided, where a progression of length $k$ consists of $k$ collinear blue points

$$
x,\ x+v,\ldots,x+(k-1)v,\qquad |v|=1.
$$

The currently formulated question asks for the smallest length $k$ that is **unavoidable** in every admissible colouring. A progression with merely some arbitrary nonzero step is a different statement.

## A small example

If an admissible colouring makes a point red, every point of the unit circle around it must be blue. On a horizontal line, red points can never be adjacent by unit spacing. This yields many blue points, but the absence of unit-spaced blue runs of a specified length is a **two-dimensional constraint**; checking one line alone is insufficient.

The colour of every point in the plane matters. A finite grid experiment tests only candidate restrictions; it cannot prove that all entire-plane colourings must exhibit a particular blue progression.

## What is known

The **unit-step** problem remains **OPEN**. Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus (1975) showed the threshold is at least five; Tsaturian (2017) improved the bound to **at least six**, through constructions avoiding shorter blue runs. Erdős and Graham conjectured a very large finite upper bound, informally around ten million, but the maintained source explicitly notes that they offered no proof.

A significant **statement-capture defect** remains: the historical Erdős–Graham papers did **not explicitly restrict** the common difference of the blue progression to unit length. Noga Alon observed that with arbitrary positive step, the colouring problem has no analogous finite avoidance threshold. Restricting a line to integer points, red cannot appear at neighbouring integers, so sufficiently structured blue sets yield arbitrarily long progressions by van der Waerden's theorem. The source now presents the unit-step formulation as the **likely intended** one, not a verbatim historical quotation. This needs a semantic comparison to the pinned provider statement.

## What remains

A bounded task reads the frozen 188.lean statement and tests whether unit Euclidean distance is imposed on the **red forbidden pairs** and on the **blue common difference**. Enumerate colourings of finite rational grids under both interpretations and exhibit where their constraints differ. Acceptance: exact source-commit comparison and a finite countermodel separating the two predicates where available. Stop at semantic verification; finite computations do not settle the whole-plane question.
