## The question in plain language

An arithmetic progression here means a set of integers

$$
A_i=a_i+n_i\mathbb Z=\{a_i+t n_i:t\in\mathbb Z\},
$$

where $a_i\in\mathbb Z$ and $n_i\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$. Repetitions among the moduli are allowed. A finite family $A_1,\ldots,A_r$ *covers* a set if every integer in that set lies in at least one $A_i$; it covers all integers if

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb Z\;\exists i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\quad x\equiv a_i\pmod{n_i}.
$$

Problem 275 asks whether a family of $r$ progressions that covers one block of $2^r$ consecutive integers must cover every integer. In exact quantifier form, the assertion is

$$
\left(\exists k\in\mathbb Z\;\forall x\in\mathbb Z,\ k\le x<k+2^r\Rightarrow\exists i\le r,\ x\equiv a_i\pmod{n_i}\right)
\Rightarrow
\left(\forall x\in\mathbb Z\;\exists i\le r,\ x\equiv a_i\pmod{n_i}\right).
$$

The location of the block is irrelevant: translating all the progressions and the block by the same integer preserves the question. The important quantifiers are that the same finite family must cover the whole block, while the witnessing index $i$ may depend on $x$.

## A small example

The power $2^r$ is sharp. For $r=4$, take

$$
A_1=1\pmod 2,\quad A_2=2\pmod 4,\quad A_3=4\pmod 8,\quad A_4=8\pmod {16}.
$$

These four classes cover each of $1,2,\ldots,15$: an integer is assigned according to the position of its first $1$ in binary, with $1$ handled by the first class. But $0$ is in none of them, so this family does not cover $\mathbb Z$. Thus $2^r-1$ consecutive integers do not suffice in general, even though $2^r$ do.

Here is a reproducible finite check of the theorem’s pattern for all ordered triples whose moduli are at most $4$. Because such a family is periodic with period the least common multiple of its moduli, checking one period is exact for this finite experiment.

```python
from itertools import product
from math import gcd

def lcm(a, b): return a*b//gcd(a, b)
def hit(x, A): return any((x-a) % m == 0 for a, m in A)

checked = witnesses = 0
for S in product(*[[(a,m) for m in range(1,5) for a in range(m)] for _ in range(3)]):
    checked += 1
    q = 1
    for _, m in S: q = lcm(q, m)
    if any(all(hit(x, S) for x in range(k, k+8)) for k in range(q)):
        witnesses += 1
        assert all(hit(x, S) for x in range(q))
print(checked, witnesses)
```

The verified output is `1000 337`: all $1000$ finite systems were tested, and $337$ covered some eight-term block; every one of those $337$ covered its whole period. This is finite evidence, not a proof of the general statement.

## What is known

The result is proved externally. R. B. Crittenden and C. L. Vanden Eynden’s announcement, “A proof of a conjecture of Erdős,” *Bulletin of the AMS* **75** (1969), 1326–1329, and their full paper, “Any $n$ arithmetic progressions covering the first $2^n$ integers cover all integers,” *Proceedings of the AMS* **24** (1970), 475–481, are the original resolution cited by the source packet. The 1970 paper’s opening statement and Lemma 1 are the cited proof and sharpness locations; the original AMS full text was not directly retrievable in this audit, so no stronger page-level inspection is claimed here.

An accessible primary mathematical proof is P. Balister, B. Bollobás, R. Morris, J. Sahasrabudhe, and M. Tiba, “Covering intervals with arithmetic progressions,” *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* **161** (2020), 197–200. Its Theorem 1 (PDF p. 1, journal p. 197) states the arbitrary-consecutive-block form. The proof (PDF pp. 1–2) reduces to a period $q$, constructs a polynomial whose zero set records the covered residue classes, and obtains $2^r+1$ linearly independent translates inside a space of dimension at most $2^r$, a contradiction. The same paper gives the powers-of-two family as the sharpness example.
