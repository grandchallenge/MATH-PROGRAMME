## The question in plain language

Two elements of a group need not commute: multiplying them in one order may differ from multiplying them in the other. Draw a graph with one vertex for every element of a group $G$, joining $g$ and $h$ exactly when

$$
gh\ne hg.
$$

[Erdős Problem #1098](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1098) asks: if this *noncommuting graph* has no **infinite** clique, must it have some **finite upper bound** on the sizes of all cliques?

The group itself can be infinite. The desired upper bound can depend on the group.

## A small example

Consider $S_3$, the group of all six permutations of three objects. Its center, the elements that commute with **everything**, consists only of the identity. Therefore the center has index six.

Direct enumeration of all six permutations and their products finds a largest set of pairwise noncommuting elements of size **four**: the three transpositions together with one three-cycle. No larger clique exists. The bound of six obtained from the center index is valid but not sharp.

## What is known

**The question was solved** by B. H. Neumann (1976). The maintained catalogue states Neumann's stronger equivalence:

> A group has no infinite set of pairwise noncommuting elements exactly when its center has finite index.

One direction has a short explanation. Suppose $[G:Z(G)]=m<\infty$. Two elements in the same coset $gZ(G)$ commute, since each is $gz$ for some central $z$. Hence a pairwise noncommuting subset can take at most one element from each of the $m$ cosets:

$$
\omega(\Gamma(G))\le [G:Z(G)].
$$

Thus finite center index implies a uniform bound on clique size. The reverse implication for an arbitrary group is the substantive part of Neumann's externally published result.
