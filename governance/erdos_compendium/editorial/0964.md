## The question in plain language

Write $\tau(n)$ for the number of positive divisors of the positive integer $n$. Erdős asked whether the sequence of positive rational numbers

$$
r_n=\frac{\tau(n+1)}{\tau(n)},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,
$$

is **dense in $(0,\infty)$**: does every nonempty open interval of positive real numbers contain at least one ratio $r_n$?

**The answer is yes, and a much stronger theorem is known.** Sean Eberhard proved that **every positive rational number** $q$ occurs as $r_n=q$ for **infinitely many positive integers $n$**. The result is unconditional and was published in *Journal of Number Theory* **281** (2026), pp.426–428. [Maintained catalogue #964](https://www.erdosproblems.com/964); [published paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2025.10.002).

The infinitely-often conclusion implies density because every nonempty open interval in $(0,\infty)$ contains a positive rational. Neither a mere proof of density nor a finite computation would yield Eberhard's stronger theorem.

## A small example

*Small exact examples*

The first few divisor counts are

$$
\tau(2)=2,\quad \tau(3)=2,\quad \tau(4)=3,\quad
\tau(5)=2,\quad \tau(6)=4.
$$

Consequently

$$
r_2=1,\qquad r_3=\frac32,\qquad
r_4=\frac23,\qquad r_5=2.
$$

These values demonstrate several exact ratios, but cannot establish density or infinite recurrence.

The divisor-count formula

$$
\tau\!\left(\prod_{p}p^{e_p}\right)=\prod_p(e_p+1)
$$

makes these calculations independently reproducible with integer arithmetic. A test suite should reduce each rational pair to lowest terms before comparisons and should never infer an unbounded existence theorem from a finite prefix.

## What is known

*What the published proof uses*

Eberhard starts from a **proved sieve theorem**, a specialization of Goldston–Graham–Pintz–Yıldırım [GGPY11, Corollary 2.1]. For three carefully chosen linear forms $L_i(x)=a_i x+1$, the theorem guarantees that at least one of the three pairs of forms simultaneously takes suitable *products of two large distinct primes* infinitely often. Crucially, it does **not** assert that any arbitrary **specified** pair works.

Choose

$$
(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(a,a+1,a+2),\qquad a\ \text{even}.
$$

The identities

$$
a_2L_1-a_1L_2=1,\qquad
a_3L_2-a_2L_3=1,\qquad
\frac12a_3L_1-\frac12a_1L_3=1
$$

convert a good pair of these linear forms into **consecutive integers**. Auxiliary odd, pairwise coprime integers $r_1,r_2,r_3$, chosen coprime to the relevant coefficients, control the divisor-count ratios. Since the two large new primes contribute a factor $4$ to each relevant divisor count, their contributions cancel in the final ratio.

There is an important refinement: the sieve guarantees **one of three ratios**, not which one. Eberhard multiplies each auxiliary $r_i$ by a suitable new prime power so that all three candidate ratios become **equal**. Whichever pair the sieve returns, the *same* desired ratio consequently occurs for infinitely many $n$. The proof would be invalid if this synchronization were omitted.

## Why every positive rational occurs

Let $R\subseteq\mathbb Q_{>0}$ be the set of rational values attained infinitely often by $r_n$. The source proof introduces

$$
F(x,y)=\frac{(x+1)(y+1)}{x+y+1},
\qquad x,y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}.
$$

After specializing the sieve construction and matching the three candidates, it shows that $R$ contains the **multiplicative subgroup of positive rationals generated by** the numbers $F(x,y)$. It is *not* being claimed that an arbitrary set of recurrent ratios is automatically closed under multiplication; the subgroup inclusion is obtained by the explicit construction.

Here is the elementary, independently checked final algebra step. First

$$
F(2,3)=\frac{3\cdot4}{2+3+1}=2.
$$

For any odd prime $p=2x+1>2$,

$$
F(x,x)=\frac{(x+1)^2}{p},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
p=\frac{(x+1)^2}{F(x,x)}.
$$

Every prime factor of $x+1$ is smaller than $p$. By induction on the primes, the generated subgroup contains $p$ for **every prime** $p$. As every positive rational is a quotient of products of primes, that subgroup is all of $\mathbb Q_{>0}^{\times}$. The difficult analytic input is the GGPY sieve statement and the synchronization of the three possible outputs; the final subgroup-generation argument is elementary.

This proof outline was compared against the **three-page published paper itself**, pp.426–428. It is a paraphrase and decomposition, not an independent proof of the underlying GGPY result.

## Historical and formalization boundaries

Before Eberhard's proof, the stronger exact-rational assertion followed conditionally from the generalized prime $k$‐tuple conjecture. Eberhard instead derives it **unconditionally** from a proved almost-prime sieve theorem, building on Hasanalizade's and Schlage-Puchta's investigations. It would be incorrect to describe the 2026 theorem as conditional on the unproved prime $k$‐tuple conjecture.

The catalogue labels #964 **PROVED (LEAN)**, but its public discussion reports a Lean development **conditional on a formalized GoldstonGrahamPintzYildirimStatement** carrying most of the analytic content.
