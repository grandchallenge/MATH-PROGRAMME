## The question in plain language

A *Sidon set* is a set of positive integers whose unordered pair sums are all distinct, even when a number is paired with itself. Thus if four members satisfy $a+b=c+d$, then the unordered pairs $\{a,b\}$ and $\{c,d\}$ must be equal.

Let $f(N)$ be the maximum size of a Sidon subset of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ and let $A(N)$ count **all** Sidon subsets, including the empty set. Erdős and Cameron asked two questions, now catalogued as [Problem #861](https://www.erdosproblems.com/861): does $A(N)/2^{f(N)}$ tend to infinity, and does $A(N)=2^{(1+o(1))f(N)}$?

These are different statements. The first asks whether taking subsets of one largest Sidon set seriously undercounts all possibilities. The second predicts their leading exponential growth rate.

## A small example

The set $\{1,2,3\}$ is not Sidon, because $1+3=2+2$. The set $\{1,2,4\}$ is Sidon: its six unordered sums, including doubles, are $2,3,4,5,6,8$, all different.

Our reproducible exact-enumeration program, stored at governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_861.py, gives:

| $N$ | Sidon subsets $A(N)$ | Largest size $f(N)$ | Ratio $A(N)/2^{f(N)}$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | 216 | 4 | $216/16$ |
| 20 | 6845 | 6 | $6845/64$ |

## What is known

The classical Sidon extremal result says $f(N)=(1+o(1))\sqrt N$. The decisive count is in Saxton and Thomason, *Online containers for hypergraphs, with applications to linear equations*, Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series B **121** (2016), 248–283, [Theorem 1.10 and Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1611.01433). In the authors' notation their explicit construction implies

$$
A(N)\ge 2^{(\log_2\sqrt5+o(1))\sqrt N},
\qquad \log_2\sqrt5\approx1.160964.
$$

Because $\log_2\sqrt5>1$ and $f(N)=(1+o(1))\sqrt N$, it follows that

$$
\frac{A(N)}{2^{f(N)}}\ge
2^{(\log_2\sqrt5-1+o(1))\sqrt N}
\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

**The first question is answered yes. The second is answered no**: $A(N)$ has a proven lower exponential coefficient strictly larger than the conjectured $1$. The exact asymptotics of $A(N)$ remain more difficult.

The two original questions appear in [Erdős, *Some of My Forgotten Problems in Number Theory* (1992), printed p. 38, equations (13)–(14)](https://hrj.episciences.org/125/pdf). Both the frozen and the maintained catalogue describe #861 as solved; this is source status, not a GCL editorial admission.

## GCL's current state

The lower-bound construction can be stated precisely. Take a prime $p$, put $q=p(p-1)$, and use a modular Sidon set $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,q\}$ of size $p-1$. Such sets are supplied by Ruzsa's construction and are an explicitly cited input, not proved anew here.

For each $s\in S$, make one of **five choices**: omit it, or place it in one of four disjoint translated blocks $S,S+q,S+2q,S+3q$. No base element may be used in more than one block. The resulting subset of $\{1,\ldots,4q\}$ is Sidon. Indeed, if two unordered pair sums agree as integers, reducing them modulo $q$ yields the same underlying unordered pair in $S$. The unique block choice for each base element forces the lifted pair to be the same. The argument includes doubled pairs.

All $5^{p-1}$ choices give different subsets. At $N=4p(p-1)$ this yields

$$
A(N)\ge5^{p-1}
=2^{(\log_2\sqrt5+o(1))\sqrt N}.
$$

Extending from this subsequence to every sufficiently large $N$ uses a prime $p$ for which $4p(p-1)\le N$ and the standard fact that the ratio of consecutive primes tends to one. This is the published Section 5 argument, not a new solution.

The local verification program independently checks all Sidon subsets up to $N=20$. A further exact test of the translated construction for $p=3$ and $p=5$, with respectively $25$ and $625$ choices, is a suitable verification obligation; finite experiments alone do not certify the asymptotic proof or a Lean theorem.

## What remains

The Cameron–Erdős counting questions are settled in the literature, but GCL has not independently replayed a complete formal proof of the bound. The next bounded task is the **four-translate five-choice lemma**: prove that a Sidon set modulo $q$, with each base element either omitted or assigned to exactly one of four translated blocks, gives a Sidon set of integers.

The exact hypotheses must include unordered pair sums with repetitions. Acceptance requires a mathematical proof or a Lean theorem without placeholders, plus two independent finite checks of the $p=3$ and $p=5$ cases and a source-locked statement of the modular-base existence theorem. Falsify the lifting lemma with distinct unordered pairs sharing the same sum. Stop before claiming an independent formal solution without compiled source and actual axiom output.
