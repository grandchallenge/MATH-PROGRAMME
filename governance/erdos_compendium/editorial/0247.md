## The question in plain language

Start with a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers

$$
1\leq a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots.
$$

Its terms tell us which powers of $1/2$ to keep. Erdős Problem #247 asks whether the resulting number

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-a_n}
$$

must be transcendental whenever the exponents sometimes run arbitrarily far ahead of their indices. The exact growth condition is

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{n}=\infty.
$$

In quantifier form, for every real $C>0$ and every starting index $N$, there is some $n\geq N$ with $a_n/n>C$. Thus occasional very large excursions of the exponents are enough; the ratio need not tend to infinity at every index. The question is about transcendence, which is stronger than irrationality: a transcendental number is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients.

The apparent simplicity is deceptive. The series converges extremely quickly, but a rapidly convergent series can still have an algebraic or even rational sum if its exponents have special structure. The problem asks whether the particular amount of irregular growth above rules out every algebraic possibility.

## A small example

Take $a_n=n^2$. It is strictly increasing and

$$
\frac{a_n}{n}=n\longrightarrow\infty,
$$

so it satisfies the problem's hypothesis. The first five terms can be checked with exact integer arithmetic:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{5}2^{-n^2}
=2^{-1}+2^{-4}+2^{-9}+2^{-16}+2^{-25}
=\frac{18940417}{33554432}
\approx0.5644684136.
$$

This is a finite partial sum, not the value of the infinite series and not a proof of transcendence. The remaining tail is already tiny. For $n\geq6$, $n^2\geq6n$, so

$$
0<\sum_{n=6}^{\infty}2^{-n^2}
\leq\sum_{n=6}^{\infty}2^{-6n}
=\frac{2^{-36}}{1-2^{-6}}<2^{-35}.
$$

This gives an independently reproducible finite check of the scale of the number while preserving the exact infinite question. It also shows why numerical approximation cannot settle the problem: an enormous amount of precision would still not identify whether the limiting value is algebraic.

For contrast, $a_n=2n$ gives the geometric series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-2n}=\frac13,
$$

but $a_n/n=2$, so this sequence does not satisfy the required growth condition. The contrast is useful: the hypothesis is doing real work, but the finite examples do not say whether it does enough.

## What is known

The target with only $\limsup a_n/n=\infty$ remains unresolved in the audited source record. The public problem page labels it as an open question, has no proof exposition and no proof claim, and was last edited on 20 January 2026. The pinned catalogue snapshot likewise records `open`, with the solution marked `unformalized`.

A stronger result is classically proved and reported in Erdős's literature. The 1980 Erdős–Graham book states that transcendence follows when

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{n^t}=\infty
\qquad\text{for every positive integer }t.
$$

That family includes much faster-growing sequences, but not every sequence satisfying the problem's linear-limsup condition. In particular, $a_n=n^2$ satisfies the open condition but does not satisfy the displayed requirement for $t>2$. The 1975 paper cited by the audit contains the underlying Liouville-type results for rapidly growing reciprocal-series denominators; it should not be silently identified with a direct proof of the weaker Problem #247 statement.

The 1988 paper gives a related, stronger reported interface: it says that the series is transcendental if $a_n/n^t\to\infty$ for every positive integer power $t$, and then asks whether the weaker limsup condition might suffice. Its discussion also records the unresolved direction of proving that $a_n>c n^2$ forces the series not to be a root of a quadratic polynomial. These are external mathematical reports, not a GCL replay. No externally reported proof of the exact linear-limsup question was found in the audited packet.

## What remains

The mathematical target is still the exact implication

$$
\left(1\leq a_1<a_2<\cdots\ \land\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{n}=\infty\right)
\Longrightarrow
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-a_n}\text{ is transcendental}.
$$

A positive solution must handle every strictly increasing positive-integer sequence with the stated occasional superlinear excursions. A negative solution would require an explicit sequence satisfying that condition whose series is algebraic, not merely a sequence with bounded ratio such as $2n$.

The immediate bounded GCL task is narrower and safer: formalize the shift from a source-indexed sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ to a Lean function indexed from zero, preserve positivity, and prove exact equivalence of the limsup hypotheses for the stronger polynomial family. It should be tested on concrete sequences such as $n^2$ and a factorial-growth sequence, while keeping the weak linear-limsup condition separate. That task can repair statement semantics; it cannot solve the transcendence problem. Any attempt to promote it into a proof of the open implication would cross the evidence boundary.
