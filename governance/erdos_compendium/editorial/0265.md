## The question in plain language

Start with a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers

$$
a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots.
$$

Assume that both reciprocal series converge and have rational sums:

$$
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{a_n}\in\mathbb{Q},\qquad
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{a_n-1}\in\mathbb{Q}.
$$

The question is how quickly such a sequence can grow. The interesting scale is double exponential. Erdős conjectured that growth with $a_n^{1/n}\to\infty$ should be possible, but that the critical endpoint should satisfy

$$
a_n^{1/2^n}\longrightarrow 1.
$$

The latter says that $\log a_n=o(2^n)$: the sequence may still grow much faster than exponentially, but not at a persistent rate comparable with $c^{2^n}$ for any fixed $c>1$.

There is an important normalization warning. The 1980 source prints $1+a_n$ in the second denominator, while the 1988 source's prose and the current page use $a_n-1$. A constant shift of every term converts one problem class into the other, but it changes which displayed example represents the sequence. The sign discrepancy is therefore a genuine source conflict, not a harmless reason to quote the sources as though their formulas were identical.

## A small example

For the current minus-one normalization, take the Cantor sequence

$$
a_k=\binom{k}{2}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2},\qquad k\geq 3.
$$

The first sum is **$1$, not $2$**. Indeed,

$$
\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}=\frac{2}{k(k-1)}=2\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right),
$$

so the partial sums telescope and

$$
\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}=1.
$$

For the shifted denominator,

$$
\binom{k}{2}-1=\frac{(k-2)(k+1)}{2},\qquad
\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}-1}=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{1}{k-2}-\frac{1}{k+1}\right).
$$

Three terms survive in the second telescoping sum, giving

$$
\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}-1}
=\frac{2}{3}\left(1+\frac12+\frac13\right)=\frac{11}{9}.
$$

Thus both infinite sums are rational, namely $1$ and $11/9$. The example grows quadratically, so it demonstrates simultaneous rationality without answering the growth question. An exact finite check is reproducible with:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
for N in (3, 4, 10, 100):
    s = sum((Fraction(1, k*(k-1)//2) for k in range(3, N+1)), Fraction())
    t = sum((Fraction(1, k*(k-1)//2-1) for k in range(3, N+1)), Fraction())
    print(N, s, t)
```

This returns $(1/3,1/2)$ at $N=3$, $(1/2,7/10)$ at $N=4$, $(4/5,1516/1485)$ at $N=10$, and $(49/50,1803151/1499850)$ at $N=100$.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham recorded the problem in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980, p.64), with the plus-one denominator. Erdős's 1985 note on Straus (p.334) says that they could not decide whether the sequence could grow exponentially or faster. In the 1988 paper “On the irrationality of certain series: problems and results” (printed p.104), the prose uses $1/(n_k-1)$ but the source packet records a displayed Cantor example with $\binom{k}{2}+1$. Kovač and Tao explicitly choose the minus-one formulation and display the Cantor witness with $\binom{k}{2}-1$.

The strongest published interface identified in the source audit is Kovač–Tao, *On several irrationality problems for Ahmes series*. In §2.2.1 (pp.8–9), Theorem 2.8 and Corollary 2.9 show, for every finite number $d$ of consecutive shifted reciprocal series, that there is a strictly increasing integer sequence for which all $d$ sums are rational and

$$
a_k^{1/\beta^k}\longrightarrow\infty
$$

for some $\beta>1$. Their proof permits, when $d=2$, any $1<\beta<\sqrt{6/5}$ through equation (7.9). After a constant shift, this applies to the minus-one pair as well. It proves that simultaneous rationality can coexist with genuinely double-exponential growth at some base.

The proof interface matters. Kovač and Tao obtain a nonempty interior for a set of possible sum vectors in dimension $d$, then use the density of $\mathbb{Q}^d$ to obtain rational targets. This is an existence argument, not an explicit formula for the rapidly growing sequence. It also does not decide whether the critical base-two quantity can stay above $1$.

The same paper cites the classical one-series obstruction that $a_k^{1/2^k}\to\infty$ forces $\sum 1/a_k$ to be irrational. That rules out one extreme, but it does not rule out $\limsup a_k^{1/2^k}>1$, nor does it prove the conjectured limit $1$.
