## The question in plain language

Let $A$ be an infinite set of positive integers. Write $A(N)$ for the number of elements of $A$ at most $N$, and let $P(A)$ be the set of all finite subset sums, including the empty sum. Erdős Problem 344 asks whether there is an absolute constant $c>0$ such that

$$
A(N) \ge c\sqrt N \text{ for every sufficiently large }N
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(A)\text{ contains an infinite arithmetic progression}.
$$

An infinite arithmetic progression has the form $u,u+d,u+2d,\ldots$ with $d>0$. The word “subcomplete” is shorthand for containing such a progression. It is weaker than being complete, which means that $P(A)$ contains every sufficiently large integer. The distinction matters: a fixed congruence obstruction can prevent completeness even when arbitrarily long progressions occur.

The maintained page phrases the density assumption with the asymptotic notation $A(N)\gg N^{1/2}$. The source-audit normalization is the equivalent quantified form “there is a fixed $c>0$ and a threshold after which $A(N)\ge c\sqrt N$.” The finite sums use each member of $A$ at most once. The frozen catalogue identifies the problem as number theory about complete sequences and records it as proved, not open.

## A small example

Take the finite set $A_4=\{1,2,4,8\}$. Every subset sum is an integer between $0$ and $15$, and the binary expansion shows that each such integer occurs exactly once. An exact enumeration verifies the finite statement:

$$
P(A_4)=\{0,1,2,\ldots,15\}.
$$

For a reproducible check, enumerate all subsets by bit masks. The calculation returns 16 sums, minimum 0, maximum 15, and no missing or extra values. This illustrates why finite subset sums naturally produce intervals, but it does not establish the problem: $A_4$ is finite, it has no asymptotic counting function, and a finite interval is not an infinite arithmetic progression. Even the infinite set of powers of two requires a separate binary-expansion argument; a finite experiment cannot replace the quantified theorem.

## What is known

The primary resolution is Endre Szemerédi and Van H. Vu, “Finite and infinite arithmetic progressions in sumsets,” *Annals of Mathematics* 163 (2006), 1–35, DOI [10.4007/annals.2006.163.1](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2006.163.1). Their Theorem 1.1 on printed p. 2 states that for some positive constant $c$, every subset $B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with at least $c\sqrt n$ elements has finite subset sums containing an arithmetic progression of length $n$. This is a finite theorem with a uniform constant.

The same paper then gives the exact infinite interface needed here. Corollary 1.4 on printed p. 4 says that every increasing sequence of density at least $c\sqrt n$ is subcomplete. The proof is developed in Section 6: Lemma 6.4 (printed p. 32) constructs arbitrarily long progressions with one stabilized difference, and the final argument combines two interlaced subsequences to obtain an infinite progression. Thus the paper’s Corollary 1.4 matches the normalized Problem 344 claim, not merely a nearby finite variant.

The page also records a sharper-looking threshold as still open:

$$
A(N)\ge\sqrt{2N}.
$$

That is a different claim from the solved existence of a suitable constant. The density condition therefore has two layers: Szemerédi–Vu settle the qualitative threshold up to an unspecified absolute constant, while the extremal constant question remains outside the solved statement. Folkman’s earlier result under $N^{1/2+\epsilon}$ is historical context and a strictly stronger hypothesis, not the final answer.
