## The question in plain language

Suppose we draw $n$ distinct points in the plane and ask how many **different straight lines** pass through many of them. One line may contain two, three or more points, but it counts as one line rather than a new line for every pair of points along it.

[Erdős Problem #669](https://www.erdosproblems.com/669) asks for the greatest possible number of lines passing through **at least $k$ points** and, separately, **exactly $k$ points**. Write

$$
F_k(n)=\max_{|X|=n}\#\{\ell:|X\cap\ell|\geq k\},\qquad
f_k(n)=\max_{|X|=n}\#\{\ell:|X\cap\ell|=k\}.
$$

Here $X$ ranges over configurations of distinct planar points and $\ell$ over different geometric lines. The catalogue says the general fixed $k$ problem is **OPEN**, despite the well-known $k=3$ orchard results.

## A small example

Let $X=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(0,1)\}$. Exactly one straight line goes through three of these points: the horizontal axis. It contains exactly three, and no line contains all four. Consequently for this particular configuration

$$
\#\{\ell:|X\cap\ell|=3\} = \#\{\ell:|X\cap\ell|\geq3\}=1.
$$

Four points in general position have **zero** such lines. The problem therefore maximizes over configurations; it does not minimize the number of three-rich lines over all four-point sets.

## What is known

**Pair charging gives a general bound.** Any line containing $r\geq k$ points accounts for at least $\binom{k}{2}$ unordered point pairs. Each pair of distinct planar points determines one and only one line, so pairs assigned to different rich lines cannot overlap. Hence for each configuration

$$
\#\{\ell:|X\cap\ell|\geq k\}\binom{k}{2}\leq\binom{n}{2}.
$$

Thus

$$
f_k(n)\leq F_k(n)\leq\frac{\binom n2}{\binom k2},\qquad
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{F_k(n)}{n^2}\leq\frac1{k(k-1)}.
$$

This is an **upper bound**, not a construction reaching the bound. For $k=2$ it is sharp: choose $n$ points with no three collinear, and all $\binom n2$ pairs determine different two-point lines, giving $f_2(n)=F_2(n)=\binom n2$.

The case $k=3$ is the classical **orchard problem**. The original study of [Burr, Grünbaum and Sloane](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00147569), *The orchard problem*, *Geometriae Dedicata* **2** (1974), 397–424, gives matching leading quadratic bounds for the maximum number of lines containing **exactly three points**. Its publisher abstract gives an estimate with leading term $n^2/6$ and a linear-scale remainder. The maintained catalogue also reports that the maximum for **at least three points** has leading term $n^2/6$. A source audit should check this latter extension independently: the publisher abstract on exactly-three-rich lines alone does not prove it.

For larger $k$ the pair-counting coefficient $1/[k(k-1)]$ leaves a genuine gap between broad combinatorial counting and geometric constructions.
