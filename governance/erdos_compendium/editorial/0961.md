## The question in plain language

Fix an integer \(k\ge1\). An integer \(m>1\) is **\(k\)-smooth** if every prime factor of \(m\) is at most \(k\). Erdős defines \(f(k)\) as the **least positive integer** \(\ell\) such that every \(\ell\) consecutive integers greater than \(k\) include at least one number with a prime divisor **greater than \(k\)**. He asked for its growth, in particular whether
\[
f(k)\ll(\log k)^{O(1)}.
\]
This polylogarithmic conjecture remains **OPEN**. [Erdős Problem #961](https://www.erdosproblems.com/961).

It is not a conjecture about finding a **prime integer** inside every interval. It concerns finding any integer that has a sufficiently large prime *factor*.

## The exact longest-run interpretation

Let \(L(k)\) be the largest length of any consecutive run of integers greater than \(k\) which are all \(k\)-smooth. The Sylvester–Schur theorem implies \(L(k)\) is finite, and
\[
\boxed{f(k)=L(k)+1}.
\]
A run of length \(L(k)\) shows that no shorter forcing length suffices; its maximality proves every run of \(L(k)+1\) contains an integer with a prime factor exceeding \(k\). The shift by one is essential for both computations and theorems.

## Small examples and reproducible checks

For \(k=1\), every integer greater than one has a prime factor exceeding one, so \(f(1)=1\). For \(k=2\), the only \(2\)-smooth numbers above two are powers of two; two such numbers cannot be consecutive. Since \(4=2^2\) gives a one-term run, \(f(2)=2\).

For \(k=3\), both \(8=2^3\) and \(9=3^2\) are \(3\)-smooth. Thus \(L(3)\ge2\). Sylvester–Schur guarantees \(f(3)\le3\), so
\[
L(3)=2,\qquad f(3)=3.
\]
A finite exact sieve through 120 independently reproduces maximum run lengths one for \(k=2\) and two for \(k=3\). The **global** conclusions above follow from the mathematical upper bounds, **not** from the finite search.

An exact finite checker should remove from each tested integer all factors belonging to primes at most \(k\); the remainder equals one exactly for \(k\)-smooth integers. A previous naive trial-division implementation can mistakenly classify \(9\) as \(2\)-smooth if it removes *all* prime factors before checking their sizes. This is precisely the kind of bug the source-fidelity test must prevent.

## Classical upper bounds

Sylvester and Schur, referenced by Erdős [Er34], established
\[
f(k)\le k.
\]
Erdős [Er55d] strengthened the result to
\[
f(k)<\frac{3k}{\log k}
\]
for sufficiently large \(k\). Jutila [Ju74] and Ramachandra–Shorey [RaSh73] improved this further:
\[
f(k)\ll \frac{k}{\log k}\,
\frac{\log\log\log k}{\log\log k}
\qquad(k\to\infty).
\]
All iterated logarithms here refer to the sufficiently large range in which they are positive. These are **proved upper bounds**, but remain much larger than the conjectured polylogarithmic bound.

The primary Ramachandra–Shorey paper is [*On gaps between numbers with a large prime factor*](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-24-1-99-111), *Acta Arithmetica* **24** (1973), pp.99–111. Its publication metadata is independently confirmed by the publisher. Jutila's 1974 result and the exact iterated-log formulation are attributed by the maintained catalogue; extraction and comparison of the complete original proofs remain separate verification obligations.

## Why the problem is difficult

The bound concerns the **longest exceptional run anywhere among all sufficiently large integers**, not the average frequency of smooth numbers. Global smooth-number density estimates alone cannot rule out unusual long runs, and ordinary prime-gap results do not directly control the prime divisors of all the integers inside one run. The older upper bounds use difficult number-theoretic information about consecutive products but are far from polylogarithmic.
