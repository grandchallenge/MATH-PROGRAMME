## The question in plain language

Let $f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n$ be a **transcendental entire function**, meaning its power series has infinitely many nonzero coefficients and converges everywhere. Define its *maximum term* and *maximum modulus* at radius $r>0$:

$$
\mu(r)=\max_{n\ge0}|a_n|r^n,\qquad
M(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.
$$

Erdős asked whether, **if the limit** $\lim_{r\to\infty}\mu(r)/M(r)$ exists, it must equal zero. Existence of the limit is an explicit assumption: oscillations of the ratio do not refute the conjecture.

## A small example

For $f(z)=e^z$, the maximum modulus is $M(r)=e^r$. Its maximal Taylor-series term is $\max_n r^n/n!$, which is asymptotically $e^r/\sqrt{2\pi r}$. Thus $\mu(r)/M(r)$ tends to zero. This supports the initial guess but provides no evidence that **every** entire function behaves this way.

The triangle inequality gives $M(r)\ge\mu(r)$ via Cauchy's coefficient bound. Hence any such limit lies between zero and one. There is no general reason for the ratio to be monotone in $r$.

## What is known

**DISPROVED.** Clunie and Hayman, [*The Maximum Term of a Transcendental Entire Function*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/227), 1964, showed that the limit can be any prescribed value in the interval

$$
[0,1/2].
$$

Every positive value in that range contradicts Erdős's proposed zero-only conclusion. A separate unpublished result of Clunie reportedly proves the zero limit under the additional hypothesis **$a_n\ge0$ for all $n$**. Complex cancellations permit counterexamples that this nonnegative-coefficient result excludes.

The negative theorem concerns an entire function with a **genuine global limit**; an example with a large limsup and vanishing liminf would not be enough. The maintained [Erdős #227](https://www.erdosproblems.com/227) preserves this boundary.
