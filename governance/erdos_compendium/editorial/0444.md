## The question in plain language

Let $A$ be any infinite set of positive integers, and let $d_A(n)$ count how many selected elements divide the integer $n$:

$$
d_A(n)=\#\{a\in A:a\mid n\}.
$$

Write $H_A(x)=\sum_{a\in A\cap[1,x)}1/a$ for the harmonic weight of the selected elements and $M_A(x)=\max_{1\leq n<x}d_A(n)$. Erdős Problem 444 asks whether, for **every fixed positive integer $k$**, the ratio $M_A(x)/H_A(x)^k$ has unbounded limsup as $x$ tends to infinity.

In ordinary terms, no matter how an infinite set of possible divisors is chosen, some integers have extraordinarily many of those divisors relative to any fixed polynomial in their total reciprocal weight. The assertion involves a maximum over all integers below $x$, not an average over those integers. Moreover the quantifier is “for every $k$”; proving the assertion for just one exponent is insufficient.

## A small example

If $A=\{2,3,6\}$ and $n=6$, each of $2,3,6$ divides $6$, so $d_A(6)=3$. Its reciprocal weight is $1/2+1/3+1/6=1$. This gives the finite ratio $3$ for any exponent $k$, but finite $A$ does not satisfy the theorem's hypothesis and no asymptotic conclusion follows from it.

A more instructive infinite example takes $A=\mathbb N$, so $d_A(n)$ is the ordinary divisor-counting function $\tau(n)$ and $H_A(x)$ is approximately $\log x$. Let $N_r$ be the product of the first $r$ distinct primes. Unique factorization gives $\tau(N_r)=2^r$: each prime is either included or excluded in a divisor. Taking $x=N_r+1$ guarantees $M_A(x)\geq2^r$.

By Bertrand's postulate one may crudely bound prime number $r$ by $2^r$, giving $\log N_r=O(r^2)$. Hence $H_A(N_r+1)=O(r^2)$ and $M_A(N_r+1)/H_A(N_r+1)^k\geq2^r/O(r^{2k})$, which diverges for every fixed $k$. This is an elementary proof of the theorem for this particular set, not for an arbitrary infinite set $A$.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham included the question in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 88. The general affirmative result was proved by Erdős and Sárközy [ErSa80], as documented in the maintained [Erdős Problems #444](https://www.erdosproblems.com/444) entry.

The theorem says that **every** infinite $A$, including an extremely sparse one, satisfies the unbounded-limsup conclusion for every fixed $k$. The primorial example above shows why the result may be plausible for a dense set of divisors, but the full strength is its independence from any density assumption on $A$.

One should not silently replace the maximum with an average. The expected value of $d_A(n)$ over a finite interval has a natural relation to $H_A(x)$, but the theorem detects rare integers with much larger divisor multiplicities. Similarly, the theorem does not claim that the ratio tends to infinity along *every* sufficiently large $x$; an unbounded limsup is a weaker and more precise assertion.

## What remains

A bounded numerical fixture can choose several explicitly infinite sets, such as all natural numbers, even numbers, perfect squares and an increasing sequence of prime powers, then compute exact truncated reciprocal sums and maxima of $d_A(n)$ for small $x$. Two implementations should agree: one tests divisibility directly and the other factors $n$ and generates its divisors before testing membership in $A$. These checks make the definitions executable without pretending to test an infinite limsup.

The documentary obligation is to read Erdős–Sárközy's 1980 primary theorem and verify the order of quantifiers: all infinite $A$, each fixed exponent $k$, and unbounded limsup as $x\to\infty$. A finite set, a growing exponent $k=k(x)$, or an ordinary limit in place of limsup would be materially different. Success requires exact arithmetic and the correct theorem statement. Falsification includes counting divisors from outside $A$, omitting the denominator's square-free harmonic weights, or promoting the primorial special case into a proof for arbitrary $A$.
