## The question in plain language

Rosen constructed an increasing sequence of integers

$$
0=a_0<a_1<a_2<\cdots,\qquad a_0=0,\quad a_1=1,
$$

using a greedy rule designed to make the number of **unordered representations, with repetition permitted**, of sums at most $x$ stay close to $x$. Erdős and Rosen asked whether its representation count satisfies

$$
R(x)=x+O\!\left(x^{1/4+o(1)}\right)\quad(x\to\infty).
$$

The conjecture is **open**. They could not even prove the weaker bound $R(x)=(1+o(1))x$. [Maintained statement, #954](https://www.erdosproblems.com/954).

Here a representation means an index pair $0\le i\le j$, $j\ge1$; in particular $a_i+a_j$ and $a_j+a_i$ are **one** representation, and $a_i+a_i$ is permitted. The object being counted is the number of pairs whose sums are **at most** $x$, not the number whose sum equals $x$. Confusing those conventions changes the problem.

## A small example

*The exact greedy algorithm*

For the finite prefix $a_0,\ldots,a_k$ define

$$
R_k(t)=\#\bigl\{(i,j):0\le i\le j\le k,\ j\ge1,\ a_i+a_j\le t\bigr\}.
$$

Then $a_{k+1}$ is the least integer $t$ for which $R_k(t)<t$. This rule produces

$$
0,1,3,5,9,13,17,24,31,38,45,\ldots
$$

as recorded by the source and independently reproduced using exact integer comparisons.

To see the first steps, for $a_0=0,a_1=1$ there are two eligible pairs, with sums $1$ and $2$. Thus $R_1(1)=1$, $R_1(2)=2$, and $R_1(3)=2<3$, giving $a_2=3$. For the prefix $0,1,3$, the pair sums are $1,2,3,4,6$, so $R_2(4)=4$ but $R_2(5)=4<5$, giving $a_3=5$.

The greedy construction is well-defined at each finite stage: $R_k(t)\le k(k+3)/2$ is bounded independently of $t$, so $R_k(t)<t$ eventually. The constructed terms are strictly increasing: at stage $k$, adding $a_k$ fills the first previous deficit, and for each later $t$ until the next deficit the count remains at least $t$.

## What the construction proves automatically

The construction supplies the elementary but important lower bound

$$
R(x)\ge x
$$

for every **positive integer** $x$, where $R(x)$ counts representations from the infinite sequence. Here is the local mechanism. By minimality of the next term $t=a_{k+1}$, we have $R_k(t-1)\ge t-1$, and since $R_k(t)<t$ and the count is nondecreasing, in fact $R_k(t)=t-1$. On adjoining $a_{k+1}=t$, the new pair $(0,k+1)$ has sum $t$, while every other newly formed pair has sum strictly larger than $t$. Thus $R_{k+1}(t)=t$. Between successive new terms, the old prefix already supplies $R_k(m)\ge m$ by the definition of the first deficit. Every positive integer lies in one of these intervals, proving the bound.

For real $x\ge1$, the corresponding statement is $R(x)\ge\lfloor x\rfloor$, rather than $R(x)\ge x$ literally, since $R(x)$ is integer-valued and constant between integers. This distinction should be retained whenever the conjecture is stated with a real cutoff.

For example, with the initial terms through $5$, the seven unordered pairs having sum at most $6$ give $R(6)=7$; no later term can contribute to this cutoff. Thus the deviation $R(6)-6$ is exactly $1$.

## Why the open upper bound is difficult

The rule corrects a deficit at the first point where it appears, but controls neither the number nor the magnitude of *surplus* representations generated at larger thresholds by a newly added term. Pair sums of a growing set can collide and pile up. Proving $R(x)\ge x$ uses only the immediate deficit correction; proving $R(x)\le x+x^{1/4+o(1)}$ requires a genuinely global estimate on surplus across many greedy stages. No finite prefix or fitting of a power law can prove that asymptotic.

## What is known and GCL's present evidence

The maintained catalogue identifies this as Rosen's sequence and cites Erdős [Er77c, p.71]; its current status is **OPEN**. The catalogue links OEIS A390642, but the entry and a matching finite prefix are not a proof of asymptotic behavior. The initial eleven terms above were independently regenerated with an exact greedy enumerator. The historic source paper and any purported improved bounds beyond the catalogue summary still require page-specific source audit before certification. There is **no** claim here of a proof of the $1/4+o(1)$ exponent or even of the weaker asymptotic.
