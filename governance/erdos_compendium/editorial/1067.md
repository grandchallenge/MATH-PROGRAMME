## The question in plain language

The *chromatic number* of a graph is the smallest number of colours needed to colour its vertices without giving an edge's endpoints the same colour. For infinite graphs, it can be an infinite cardinal, such as $\aleph_1$, the first uncountable cardinal.

A graph is **infinitely vertex-connected** when every pair of distinct vertices can be joined by infinitely many paths that have no internal vertices in common. [Erdős Problem #1067](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1067) asked whether every graph of chromatic number $\aleph_1$ contains an infinitely vertex-connected subgraph whose chromatic number is still $\aleph_1$.

The distinction between having many paths and retaining uncountable chromatic number is central.

## A small example

In the finite complete graph $K_4$, each pair of vertices has three internally vertex-disjoint connecting paths: their direct edge and two length-two routes through the other vertices. But three paths are not **infinitely many**.

This illustrates what connectivity counts. A finite example can never establish or refute the uncountable-graph statement.

## What is known

**The original universal assertion is false.** Komjáth obtained a consistency result in 2013. Soukup constructed a ZFC counterexample in 2015, and Bowler and Pitz published a simpler construction in 2024. These published mathematical results distinguish a counterexample available in ordinary set theory from a separate, vertex-cardinality-restricted formulation that has an independence phenomenon.

There is also an **edge-connectivity** variant, where one may remove finitely many edges rather than vertices. Thomassen studied a related negative version. Changing between vertex and edge connectivity changes the claim and must not be treated as a cosmetic notation difference.
