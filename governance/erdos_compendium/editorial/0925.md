## The question in plain language

A graph $G$ is **Ramsey for the triangle** if every way of coloring its existing edges red or blue produces a red triangle or a blue triangle. If there is even one red-blue edge-coloring without a monochromatic triangle, the graph is **not Ramsey** for $K_3$.

Erdős asked whether being *not Ramsey* forces a graph to have an unusually large **independent set**, a vertex set containing no graph edges. Is there one universal positive real number $\delta$ such that every sufficiently large non-Ramsey graph on $n$ vertices contains an independent set of size at least a constant multiple of

$$
n^{1/3+\delta}?
$$

This is [Erdős Problem #925](https://www.erdosproblems.com/925). Its answer is **no**. Alon and Rödl disproved the proposed power improvement in 2005. They obtained a much more precise answer in terms of the three-color Ramsey number $R(3,3,m)$: it grows like $m^3$ **up to logarithmic factors**. The cubic polynomial exponent cannot be decreased by any fixed positive amount.

The exponent is about the size of an **ordinary vertex independent set** of the original graph. It is not asking whether a *particular* edge color class has a large independent set.

## A small example

Color each edge of the complete graph on five vertices red if it lies on a fixed five-cycle, and blue otherwise. The red graph is a five-cycle; the blue graph is also a five-cycle. Neither contains a monochromatic triangle.

Thus $K_5$ is **not** Ramsey for triangles. Its independence number as a graph, however, is just one: all pairs of vertices are adjacent. This small example does **not** disprove an assertion meant for all sufficiently large $n$, but it clarifies that even a graph with very few independent vertices can admit a triangle-avoiding edge coloring at small orders.

Now introduce a **third color**, green, to mean a missing graph edge. Every unordered vertex pair in the complete graph on the same vertex set receives exactly one of three colors:

- Red if it is an edge of $G$ colored red.
- Blue if it is an edge of $G$ colored blue.
- Green if it is not an edge of $G$.

The graph is non-Ramsey exactly when neither the red nor blue color contains a triangle. A set of $m$ vertices forms an independent set in $G$ exactly when the same vertices form a **green complete graph** $K_m$ in the three-color complete-graph picture.

This exact correspondence is the bridge to multicolor Ramsey numbers.

## What is known

Let $R(3,3,m)$ denote the least integer $N$ such that every coloring of all the edges of $K_N$ with red, blue and green has either a red triangle, a blue triangle, or a green clique of order $m$.

The elementary correspondence above proves that a universal guarantee of a large independent set in **every** non-Ramsey graph is equivalent to an **upper bound** on $R(3,3,m)$. Erdős's proposed gain of a fixed positive exponent beyond $1/3$ corresponds, at the polynomial exponent level, to asking for

$$
R(3,3,m)\ll m^{3-\varepsilon}
$$

for some fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $m$. The quantifiers matter: a single fixed $m$ or a bound only along a sparse sequence of values would not settle the original asymptotic question.

Noga Alon and Vojtěch Rödl, [*Sharp bounds for some multicolor Ramsey numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00493-005-0011-9), *Combinatorica* **25** (2005), no.2, pp.125–141, prove an asymptotically sharp **cubic exponent**. Their authors' complete [final manuscript](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~nalon/PDFS/arodl4.pdf) states, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $m$,

$$
\frac{c_\varepsilon m^3}{(\log m)^{4+\varepsilon}}
\le R(3,3,m)
\le
\frac{C m^3\log\log m}{(\log m)^2},
$$

with positive constants depending on the displayed parameters. The lower bound constructs three-colorings with no red or blue triangle and no green clique of size $m$ on nearly cubic-many vertices; the upper bound guarantees one of the forbidden configurations above its threshold. The authors record Sudakov's improvement removing the $\log\log m$ factor from the upper bound.

GCL examined the **original authors' manuscript** as page images, including its abstract, introduction and Theorem 3.2 region, which proves for a fixed number of triangle-avoiding colors that the relevant multicolor Ramsey number has polynomial exponent one more than that number of colors, up to logarithmic factors. The case of two triangle-avoiding colors gives the cubic exponent required for #925.

The proof methods mix pseudorandom regular graphs, spectral eigenvalue estimates, control on independent-set counts and probabilistic shifts. No short elementary construction of the nearly cubic lower-bound examples is claimed here. The maintained [Erdős catalogue #925](https://www.erdosproblems.com/925) lists the conjecture as **DISPROVED** and credits the same 2005 paper. It has no verified formalized statement in the catalogue metadata.

## GCL's current state

The finite three-color **encoding lemma** is entirely elementary and independently verifiable. For any fixed set of $n$ vertices, the data consisting of a graph $G$ and a red-blue coloring of its edges corresponds bijectively to a three-coloring of all pairs in the complete graph, where the third color means **absence of an edge**. This bijection preserves the property of having no red or blue triangle and turns independent sets of $G$ into green cliques of the same order. It works for every finite $n$ and does not depend on probabilistic constructions.

The executable exact fixture at governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_924_925.py enumerates all three-colorings of the edges of complete graphs on one to five vertices satisfying the red-blue no-triangle condition. Across **29,769** such colorings, it independently compares the largest green clique to the maximum independent set of the reconstructed graph, finding exact equality in every case. It also enumerates all **32,768** red-blue edge-colorings of $K_6$ and verifies that none avoids a monochromatic triangle, while some colorings of $K_5$ do.

These calculations are useful semantic and finite proof regressions; they do not establish an asymptotic lower bound for $R(3,3,m)$, which is the decisive imported ingredient.

The **quantitative inversion** can be checked separately. A lower bound of order $m^3/(\log m)^{4+\varepsilon}$ on $R(3,3,m)$ supplies graphs on suitable large vertex counts whose independence numbers are no more than order $n^{1/3}(\log n)^{(4+\varepsilon)/3}$. Because every fixed positive power of $n$ eventually dominates every fixed power of $\log n$, these examples contradict a universal guarantee of an independent set of size $\gg n^{1/3+\delta}$, for any fixed $\delta>0$.

Conversely, the upper bound $R(3,3,m)\ll m^3\log\log m/(\log m)^2$ guarantees independent sets of size at least order

$$
n^{1/3}
\frac{(\log n)^{2/3}}{(\log\log n)^{1/3}}
$$

in all sufficiently large non-Ramsey graphs. These conclusions require correct inequality directions: the **lower** Ramsey bound yields counterexamples; the **upper** Ramsey bound yields universal guarantees.

## What remains

A bounded mathematical task is to formalize the **graph–three-coloring bijection** and the equality between independence number and the largest green clique. Acceptance is a complete elementary proof or source-normalized pinned Lean with no placeholders and a literal axiom report, plus independent replay of all 29,769 finite conversion cases and the 32,768 colorings of $K_6$. A missing graph edge assigned red or blue, or an independent vertex set not corresponding to a green clique, falsifies the model.

The second bounded task is the **asymptotic inversion lemma**, preserving the distinction between a lower Ramsey bound and an upper one, including integer rounding and polylogarithmic factors. Acceptance must make explicit the quantifier for every fixed positive $\delta$ and show why a bound of order $n^{1/3}(\log n)^C$ cannot imply the stronger desired power. A proof with the Ramsey inequality reversed does not solve the original problem.

The independent source-review obligation is to reconstruct Alon–Rödl's full 2005 spectral and probabilistic argument, verify the theorem's detailed constants and logarithmic exponents, and distinguish their argument from the simpler finite coloring conversion. Any statement that the finite brute-force verifier establishes the asymptotic counterexample family would be incorrect. The original yes-or-no conjecture is already disproved in the published literature; improvements to logarithmic powers are separate successor problems.
