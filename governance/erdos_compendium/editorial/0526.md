**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #526 is **SOLVED**. It asks when randomly placed arcs cover **every point** of a circle. Larry Shepp found the exact necessary-and-sufficient series criterion in 1972, resolving Dvoretzky's problem. A source-fidelity qualification is important: Shepp states his theorem for **nonincreasing** arc lengths. For the general order-free problem, the positive lengths must first be arranged in nonincreasing order.

## The question in plain language

Consider a circle of circumference one, represented by $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. At step $n$, place an arc $I_n$ of length $a_n$ at an independently sampled uniform random location. Suppose the deterministic lengths obey

$$
a_n\ge0,\qquad a_n\to0,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=\infty.
$$

What condition ensures that the **entire circle** is covered by the union of these arcs, with probability one?

There are two different statements:

- **Almost-everywhere coverage:** The uncovered set has total arc length zero.
- **Everywhere coverage:** The uncovered set is **empty**.

The first follows easily from the divergent sum of lengths. The second is substantially harder: an uncovered random set may have zero arc length yet still contain points.

For the exact criterion, suppose initially that all $0<a_n<1$, and arrange the lengths as a nonincreasing sequence

$$
\ell_1\ge\ell_2\ge\cdots>0,\qquad\ell_n\to0.
$$

Then Shepp's theorem says that the circle is covered with probability one **if and only if**

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_n)}{n^2}=\infty.}
$$

The ordering is not arbitrary bookkeeping. The original theorem assumes monotone lengths. The geometric covering event does not depend on the *temporal order* in which independently, uniformly placed lengths are assigned. Thus when the original sequence is not monotone, use the **decreasing rearrangement** $\ell_n$ of the lengths, not its unsorted cumulative sums.

If any arc has length at least the entire circumference, coverage is immediate. Zero-length arcs can be discarded. These trivial cases should be removed before applying the positive-length theorem.

## A small example

Choose arc lengths $\ell_n=c/n$ for a positive constant $c$, interpreted as a tail and with any early lengths at least one handled separately. Then

$$
\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_n=cH_n,
\qquad H_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k.
$$

Since

$$
\log(n+1)\le H_n\le1+\log n,
$$

we have the two-sided estimates

$$
\frac{(n+1)^c}{n^2}
\le \frac{e^{cH_n}}{n^2}
\le e^c n^{c-2}.
$$

This reduces the convergence test to an ordinary power series. If $0<c<1$, the series converges because $c-2<-1$. If $c\ge1$, it diverges because its terms dominate a divergent series of order $n^{c-2}$.

Therefore the sharp boundary is

$$
\boxed{
\ell_n\sim \frac c n
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{full coverage a.s. for }c\ge1,
\text{ failure for }0<c<1,
}
$$

with any finite initial arcs shorter than one handled as a finite modification. **At the borderline $c=1$, the weaker assumption $\ell_n\sim 1/n$ alone does not guarantee coverage:** a correction of size $-2/(n\log n)$ eventually gives a convergent Shepp series. In particular, the *borderline* $c=1$ lies on the covering side. This matches Erdős's previously unpublished borderline result.

For contrast, fix one deterministic point $x$. The probability that arc $n$ misses $x$ is $1-\ell_n$, and independent placements give

$$
\mathbb P(x\text{ missed by every arc})
=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\ell_n)=0
$$

when $\sum\ell_n=\infty$. Integration over $x$ shows the uncovered set has arc length zero almost surely, but it does **not** show that the set is empty. That gap is the essence of the problem.

## What is known

### Erdős's 1961 formulation

Erdős describes Dvoretzky's covering problem in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed pp.253–254](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=33). Both original pages were read. Aryeh Dvoretzky had constructed examples satisfying the divergent length-sum assumption where the circle **is** covered and others where exceptional points remain. His original study is *On covering a circle by randomly placed arcs*, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA* **42** (1956), 199–203, [original article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC528251/).

Erdős credits Jean-Pierre Kahane (1959) with coverage for lengths $(1+c)/n$, $c>0$, and reports his own unpublished improvements: coverage at $1/n$ and failure at $(1-c)/n$ for $c>0$ within the natural positive-length range. At the time, no necessary-and-sufficient criterion was known.

### Shepp's exact 1972 solution

Larry A. Shepp, *Covering the circle with random arcs*, *Israel Journal of Mathematics* **11** (1972), 328–345, [DOI 10.1007/BF02789327](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02789327), supplied the answer. The original journal abstract confirms both the **monotonicity hypothesis** $0<\ell_{n+1}\le\ell_n<1$ and the displayed exponential series criterion. The complete eighteen-page original proof was not independently reproduced in this tranche.

The condition is strikingly sensitive to the *cumulative length* of the early, largest arcs. An ordinary divergence test $\sum\ell_n=\infty$ is sufficient only for almost-everywhere coverage. The stronger Shepp test,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(L_n)}{n^2}=\infty,
\qquad L_n=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_n,
$$

is necessary and sufficient for **everywhere coverage almost surely**.

For a nonmonotone original sequence, the events of being covered by each independently uniform arc are preserved in law when the lengths are permuted. Because the original lengths tend to zero, their positive terms can be enumerated in nonincreasing order. This justifies the decreasing-rearrangement formulation rather than an unjustified use of original-order partial sums.

### A borderline warning

The exact model $\ell_n=1/n$ lies precisely on the covering side, but merely assuming $\ell_n\sim1/n$ is insufficient at the boundary. For example, for all sufficiently large $n$ consider

$$
\ell_n=\frac1n-\frac{2}{n\log n},
$$

after choosing a positive, decreasing finite prefix. Integral comparison gives

$$
\sum_{k\le n}\ell_k
=\log n-2\log\log n+O(1).
$$

Its Shepp summand is consequently comparable to $1/[n(\log n)^2]$, which **converges**. Thus the full circle is **not** covered almost surely despite $\ell_n\sim1/n$. The exact cumulative criterion, not the leading coefficient alone, resolves such borderline cases.
