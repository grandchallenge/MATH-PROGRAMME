## The question in plain language

Choose complex numbers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$, each of absolute value at least $1$. For each of the $2^n$ possible choices of signs $\varepsilon_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, form the sum

$$
S_\varepsilon=\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i z_i.
$$

Fix a point $c\in\mathbb C$ and consider the **open unit disc**

$$
D(c,1)=\{w\in\mathbb C:|w-c|<1\}.
$$

How many different **sign choices** can have their sum inside that disc? Multiple sign choices may give the same geometric sum, but each must still be counted separately.

Erdős conjectured the sharp universal bound

$$
\boxed{
\#\{\varepsilon\in\{-1,+1\}^n:S_\varepsilon\in D(c,1)\}
\le\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
}
$$

His 1961 paper explicitly says **interior** of a circle of radius one. The interior is open; points at distance exactly one are excluded. This detail cannot be dropped merely because modern mathematical prose sometimes uses “disc” for either an open or closed set.

The bound belongs to the **Littlewood–Offord** family of anti-concentration theorems. It says that sums of many nontrivial vectors cannot cluster too heavily inside a small target region, even when the vectors point in different directions in the complex plane.

## A small example

Consider the case $n=2$, with both $z_1=z_2=1$. The four sign combinations produce

$$
-2,\quad 0,\quad0,\quad2.
$$

The open disc $D(0,1)$ contains both zero sums, so it contains **two sign choices**, equal to the central binomial coefficient $\binom21=2$. Although the two corresponding sums coincide geometrically, they must both be counted.

This example generalizes. Take every $z_i=1$. When $n$ is even, use center $c=0$; when $n$ is odd, use $c=1$. In each case, all sign assignments with exactly $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ minus signs have sum exactly $c$. Exactly

$$
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
$$

such assignments exist, and all the other sign sums differ from $c$ by at least $2$. Hence the upper bound, if true, is **best possible**.

### Why the open boundary matters

For $n=1$, choose $z_1=1$ and center $c=0$. The sums are $-1$ and $1$. Neither lies in the *open* unit disc, but **both** lie in the *closed* unit disc. Thus the closed-disc count is $2$, contradicting the proposed upper bound $\binom10=1$.

Even for $n=2$, choose $z_1=z_2=1$ and center $c=1$. The **closed** disc includes both zero sums and the sum $2$, giving three sign choices, whereas the sharp bound is only two. Therefore the boundary issue is not a harmless endpoint technicality.

## What is known

### Erdős's original real-variable result

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.240, Problem II.3, Erdős recalls his earlier theorem for **real numbers** $z_i\ge1$: any **open** interval of length two contains at most $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ sign sums. He cites *On a lemma of Littlewood and Offord*, *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* **51** (1945), 898–902. The 1961 primary page was inspected directly, including the explicit word **interior**, the complex question and the mention of Hilbert-space extensions.

The real theorem has a short proof using **Sperner's theorem**. A sign choice corresponds to the subset $P$ of indices carrying plus signs. If $P$ is strictly contained in $Q$, the corresponding real sums differ by

$$
S_Q-S_P=2\sum_{i\in Q\setminus P}z_i\ge2.
$$

Such a pair cannot both belong to the same **open** interval of length $2$. Thus the subsets corresponding to admissible sign choices form an antichain. Sperner's theorem bounds its size by $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$. For arbitrary real $z_i$ with $|z_i|\ge1$, absorbing each original sign into $\varepsilon_i$ gives the same argument.

This is a **complete elementary proof** of the real one-dimensional case, not the general complex theorem. In the plane there is no inclusion order making the same separation argument work for arbitrary directions.

### Kleitman's complex theorem

Daniel J. Kleitman resolved the complex-vector version in *On a lemma of Littlewood and Offord on the distribution of certain sums*, *Mathematische Zeitschrift* **90** (1965), 251–259, DOI [10.1007/BF01158565](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01158565). The [publisher record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01158565) confirms the author, title, volume, pages and publication date. The article is subscription-restricted; its full original proof was **not independently inspected** in this editorial audit.

Kleitman later treated linear combinations of vectors in *On a lemma of Littlewood and Offord on the distributions of linear combinations of vectors*, *Advances in Mathematics* **5** (1970), 155–157. The maintained [Erdős #498](https://www.erdosproblems.com/498) reports the sharp complex theorem as **PROVED (LEAN)** and cites both works.

The result is much stronger than merely showing the probability that a random sign sum falls in a small disc is $O(n^{-1/2})$. It gives the exact combinatorial constant

$$
\Pr\{S_\varepsilon\in D(c,1)\}
\le2^{-n}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
$$

with the $2^n$ signs equally likely. The all-$z_i=1$ construction realizes equality.
