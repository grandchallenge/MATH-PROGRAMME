## The question in plain language

For $n$ distinct planar points **not all on a common line**, an *ordinary line* is a line passing through **exactly two** of the points. Let $f(n)$ be the smallest number of ordinary lines that any such $n$ point set can determine. Erdős and de Bruijn asked whether $f(n)$ tends to infinity and how quickly.

A line that contains three or more original points is **not ordinary**, even though it contains many pairs of points. Ordinary lines are distinct geometric lines rather than ordered point pairs.

## A small example

Three noncollinear points form three ordinary lines. For four points with no three collinear, every pair determines an ordinary line, giving six. If three of the points lie on one line and the fourth is off it, that shared line is not ordinary, while the three lines from the off-line point to each point on the shared line are ordinary.

The elementary Sylvester–Gallai theorem guarantees **at least one** ordinary line whenever the point set is not collinear. It does not on its own show that their number grows linearly.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Motzkin (1951) established $f(n)\to\infty$. Kelly and Moser (1958) gave $f(n)\ge3n/7$, and Csima and Sawyer (1993) improved this to $6n/13$ for $n\ge8$. Ben Green and Terence Tao, [*On sets defining few ordinary lines*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.4714), *Discrete & Computational Geometry* 50 (2013), proved that

$$
f(n)\ge\frac n2
$$

for every sufficiently large $n$. Their theorem also describes asymptotic extremal configurations. For sufficiently large **odd** $n$, the more precise lower bound is $3\lfloor n/4\rfloor$.

The even-order bound is sharp, via suitable near-cubic configurations. Historical descriptions involving points at infinity are projective models and should not be presented as literal finite configurations in the affine plane without a deprojection argument.

## What remains

A bounded task enumerates all point pairs from small rational-coordinate configurations, groups pairs by an exactly normalized line equation, and counts lines with exactly two incident points. Acceptance: three noncollinear points give three ordinary lines, while three collinear and one off-line give exactly three, and the algorithm does not double-count a line with three points. Stop short of extrapolating the extremal theorem for large $n$.
