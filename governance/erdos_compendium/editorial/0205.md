## The question in plain language

Can every sufficiently large positive integer $n$ be written as

$$
n=2^k+m,\qquad k\ge0,\quad m>1,\quad \Omega(m)<\log\log m,
$$

where $\Omega(m)$ counts **prime factors with multiplicity**? Erdős thought the answer should be yes, even though representing a number as a power of two plus a **prime** is already known to fail on a positive-density set.

Using the multiplicity-sensitive function $\Omega$, rather than the number of **distinct** prime factors, matters. The natural logarithms and the strict inequality are also material.

## A small example

For $m=12=2^2\cdot3$, $\Omega(m)=3$, whereas the number of its **distinct** prime divisors is two. A proposed decomposition $n=2^k+m$ must check the numerical value of $\log\log m$, not merely whether $m$ looks smooth or has few distinct divisors.

The case $m=1$ requires an explicit convention since $\log\log1$ is undefined; it is excluded from the formula above. In studying counterexamples, all powers $2^k<n$ must be tested, not just $k$ near $\log_2 n$.

## What is known

**DISPROVED** for the unrestricted question. The maintained [Erdős #205](https://www.erdosproblems.com/205) reports a construction by Kevin Barreto and Leeham using ChatGPT and Aristotle, with further quantitative analysis by Terence Tao and Boris Alexeev, that produces **infinitely many** integers $n$ for which every positive remainder $n-2^k$ has at least

$$
\gg\left(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)^{1/2}
$$

prime factors counted with multiplicity. This eventually exceeds $\log\log(n-2^k)$ when the remainder is at most $n$ (with the small-remainder convention handled separately), thereby contradicting the asserted universal decomposition.

The constructed integers are divisible by large powers of two. A **separate residual question remains open**: are there arbitrarily large *odd* counterexamples? Romanoff's 1934 theorem gives positive density for primes-plus-powers-of-two representations, but does not establish a representation of **every** sufficiently large integer.

This is an externally reported AI-assisted mathematical solution, not a new GCL proof. Neither its full construction nor its external Aristotle formalization has been replayed at a pinned source and toolchain here.

## What remains

A bounded task computes $\Omega(m)$ by exact trial division, enumerates powers $2^k<n$ for a sample of numbers, and records the minimum observed remainder complexity with the strict logarithmic threshold. Acceptance: distinguish primes counted with multiplicity from distinct primes, preserve the exclusion of undefined logarithms, and label all finite evidence noncertifying. Proof replay must isolate the infinite construction and demonstrate each uniform lower bound.
