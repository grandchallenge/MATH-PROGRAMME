## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 326 asks for a very economical additive building set. Write its elements in increasing order as $A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots\}$. It should be a minimal basis of order $2$: every sufficiently large natural number must be expressible as the sum of two elements of $A$, while deleting any element must destroy that eventual covering property. The question is whether such an $A$ can have a stable quadratic scale,

$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{a_k}{k^2}=c
$$

for a nonzero constant $c$. The word “minimal” is doing substantial work. Without it, the problem is about constructing a thin basis with regular growth. With it, the same set must both cover all sufficiently large integers and have every element remain essential.

The live problem page gives this minimal-basis formulation and says that Erdős and Graham conjectured a negative answer. It also records that Erdős originally asked the broader question for an arbitrary basis, not necessarily a minimal one.

## A small example

Here is an exact finite calculation illustrating the covering mechanism, not a solution of the infinite problem. In the Fibonacci instance of Cassels’s construction take $q_1=q_2=1$, $q_3=2$, and $q_4=3$. The first two block lengths are $m_1=q_2+q_3=3$ and $m_2=q_3+q_4=5$, and the second block begins at $Q_2=m_1q_1=3$. Thus

$$
A_1=\{0,1,2,3\},\qquad A_2=\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}.
$$

All computations are in the integers. Since each block is an interval,

$$
2A_1=\{0,1,\ldots,6\},\quad A_1+A_2=\{3,4,\ldots,11\},\quad 2A_2=\{6,7,\ldots,16\}.
$$

Their union is exactly $\{0,1,\ldots,16\}$. This is reproducible by listing the endpoint sums and observing that sums of two integer intervals are integer intervals. It demonstrates finite coverage and the useful overlap between adjacent blocks. It does not establish coverage beyond 16, minimality, or convergence of an infinite counting sequence. It also uses nonnegative integers, as does the standard construction; changing the convention for whether zero belongs to the natural numbers is a harmless issue for this finite illustration, but must be fixed in a formal statement.

## What is known

The primary reference identified by the maintained page is J. W. S. Cassels, “Über Basen der natürlichen Zahlenreihe,” *Abhandlungen aus dem Mathematischen Seminar der Universität Hamburg* 21 (1957), 247–257, DOI [10.1007/BF02941936](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02941936). The University of Hamburg archive confirms the author, title, volume, year, and page range. The primary article’s landing page was reachable, but its full text was not readable in this audit because the publisher redirected the document to an access-controlled Springer identity flow.

An accessible secondary source is Melvyn B. Nathanson, “Cassels bases,” [arXiv:0905.3144](https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.3144), especially Section 6. It gives the hypotheses for Cassels’s arithmetic-progression blocks, proves that the resulting infinite set is a basis of order $2$, and states the consequence that there is an order-2 basis $C=\{c_n\}$ with $c_n=\lambda n^2+O(n)$ for some positive $\lambda$. This verifies the broad, non-minimal construction through a readable exposition. It does not prove the minimal-basis question in the live entry, and no claim here treats the secondary exposition as a substitute for reading Cassels’s original proof.
