## The question in plain language

For $x>0$, let $R_n(x)$ be the **largest sum below $x$** of precisely $n$ distinct positive unit fractions $1/m_i$. Erdős and Graham asked whether, for almost every real $x$, sufficiently large best sums are formed from the preceding best sum by adding the largest *new* unit fraction still keeping the result strictly below $x$.

The words **almost every** mean outside a Lebesgue measure-zero exceptional set. The conjecture is weaker than claiming that the usual greedy algorithm is optimal at *every* stage, because finitely many initial failures are allowed.

## A small example

For $x=11/24$, the best one-term underapproximation is $R_1(x)=1/3$. The best two-term sum, however, is

$$
R_2(11/24)=\frac14+\frac15=\frac9{20},
$$

which is larger than extending $1/3$ by its next admissible new fraction: $1/3+1/9=4/9$. Both lie strictly below $11/24$. This shows a **finite-stage** greedy failure, not that this particular rational is *never eventually greedy*.

Distinct denominators and the strict comparison with $x$ are both necessary. Permitting repeated denominators or equality with $x$ changes the optimization problem.

## What is known

**DISPROVED in the strongest measure-theoretic sense.** Vjekoslav Kovač, [*On eventually greedy best underapproximations by Egyptian fractions*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2024.09.004), *Journal of Number Theory* 268 (2025), 39–48, proves that the set of real $x>0$ whose best underapproximations are **eventually greedy** has Lebesgue measure **zero**.

The negative result does not provide a simple explicit irrational counterexample: the maintained [Erdős #206](https://www.erdosproblems.com/206) notes that finding an explicit irrational which is not eventually greedy remains open. For many rational numbers, including $x=1/m$, eventual or complete greedy optimality is known; almost-all failure is consistent with all rationals occupying a measure-zero set.

The paper is also available as [arXiv:2406.07218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07218). Its full metric proof is source-attributed, not independently recreated.

## What remains

A bounded task enumerates exact reduced rational unit-fraction sums of length one and two with denominators at most 30. Acceptance: verify the values $R_1(11/24)=1/3$ and $R_2(11/24)=9/20$ with explicit finite-exhaustiveness arguments, check the best new unit fraction after $1/3$ is $1/9$, and preserve strictness below $x$. Do not infer an almost-everywhere theorem from rational enumeration.
