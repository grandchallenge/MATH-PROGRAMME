**Documentary editorial draft — not admitted.** This article distinguishes a classical open problem, a published 2024 theorem, and a stronger **unreviewed 2026 claim**. No new theorem or GCL mathematical certificate is asserted.

## The question in plain language

Take a prime number $p$. Calculate the factorials

$$
1!,2!,3!,\ldots,(p-1)!
$$

and reduce each one modulo $p$. How many *different* nonzero residues occur?

For example, modulo $5$, the values are

$$
1!\equiv1,\quad 2!\equiv2,\quad
3!\equiv1,\quad 4!\equiv4.
$$

There are four factorials but only **three** distinct residues, because $1!$ and $3!$ agree modulo $5$.

Write

$$
A_p=\{k!\bmod p:1\leq k<p\}.
$$

Erdős Problem #478 asks whether, along the prime numbers,

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
|A_p|\sim\left(1-\frac1e\right)p
\qquad(p\longrightarrow\infty).}
$$

The predicted proportion $1-1/e\approx0.6321$ comes from a random-sampling heuristic: if a random experiment chooses approximately $p$ residues uniformly from $p$ possibilities, it will typically occupy about $(1-e^{-1})p$ classes. But factorials are not independent random samples. They satisfy the exact recurrence $(k+1)!=(k+1)k!$, and proving that their residue set behaves randomly is difficult.

The asymptotic conjecture remains **open**. In particular, a lower bound that grows faster than $\sqrt p$ does not by itself show that a positive fraction of all residue classes occur.

## A small example

The following values come from direct, exact modular computation:

| Prime $p$ | Distinct factorial residues $A_p$ | $|A_p|$ |
|---:|---|---:|
| $3$ | $\{1,2\}$ | $2$ |
| $5$ | $\{1,2,4\}$ | $3$ |
| $7$ | $\{1,2,3,6\}$ | $4$ |
| $11$ | $\{1,2,5,6,10\}$ | $5$ |
| $13$ | $\{1,2,3,5,6,7,9,11,12\}$ | $9$ |

For $p=5$, exactly three residues occur, matching $p-2$. But these are small examples. The conjecture concerns the limit through arbitrarily large primes; neither a small ratio near $1-1/e$ nor an exceptional prime proves the prediction.

An elementary calculation gives a useful lower bound for **every** prime $p$. Form the quotient set

$$
A_p/A_p=\{ab^{-1}:a,b\in A_p\}
\subseteq\mathbb F_p^\times.
$$

For $2\leq k<p$, the two factorial residues $k!$ and $(k-1)!$ both belong to $A_p$ and

$$
\frac{k!}{(k-1)!}\equiv k\pmod p.
$$

The residue $1$ is also in $A_p/A_p$, as $1!/1!=1$. Consequently,

$$
A_p/A_p=\mathbb F_p^\times.
$$

A set with $m$ elements has at most $m^2$ ordered quotients. Thus

$$
|A_p|^2\geq p-1,
\qquad |A_p|\geq\sqrt{p-1}.
$$

This square-root bound is rigorous, but much weaker than the conjectured linear growth.

There is also an upper bound. Wilson's theorem gives $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. Dividing by $p-1\equiv-1$ yields

$$
(p-2)!\equiv1\equiv1!\pmod p.
$$

For $p\geq5$, the indices $1$ and $p-2$ are different, so at least two of the $p-1$ factorials collide. Therefore $|A_p|\leq p-2$.

**The condition $p\geq5$ matters.** At $p=2$ and $p=3$, the inequality $|A_p|\leq p-2$ is false. A formula that omits these exceptional small primes is incorrect.

## What is known

The exact conjecture appears on printed page 96 of Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham's *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980). It asks whether the number of distinct factorial residues divided by $p$ tends to $1-1/e$.

A substantial published advance is due to **Alexandr Grebennikov, Arsenii Sagdeev, Aliaksei Semchankau and Aliaksei Vasilevskii**, *On the sequence $n!\bmod p$*, *Revista Matemática Iberoamericana* **40** (2024), 637–648.

Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, on printed page 638, give a product-set estimate and its consequence:

$$
|A_pA_p|=p-O\left(p^{13/14}(\log p)^{4/7}\right),
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
|A_p|\geq(\sqrt2-o(1))\sqrt p.}
$$

The first formula is written with the natural lower-bound interpretation: the published theorem uses an error term of the displayed magnitude. It says the products of pairs of factorial residues occupy almost every nonzero residue class. It does **not** say the factorial residues themselves occupy almost every class.

How does an almost-complete product set imply the stronger square-root constant? Multiplication is commutative. If $m=|A_p|$, at most $m(m+1)/2$ distinct products can be formed using unordered pairs (with repetition). Therefore,

$$
|A_pA_p|\leq\binom{m+1}{2}.
$$

Since the product set has $p-o(p)$ elements,

$$
\frac{m(m+1)}2\geq p-o(p),
$$

which yields the coefficient $\sqrt2$.

### An unreviewed July 2026 improvement claim

A later manuscript by **Xiyu Hu**, *A Super-Square-Root Lower Bound for Factorial Residues Modulo a Prime*, posted in an author-maintained GitHub repository in July 2026, claims

$$
|A_p|\gg p^{8/15}.
$$

Because $8/15>1/2$, this would surpass the square-root exponent itself, not merely its leading constant.

The manuscript derives many arithmetic transitions from the exact identity

$$
(n+2)!=(n+1)!+\frac{((n+1)!)^2}{n!}
$$

in $\mathbb F_p$, where all denominators are nonzero for the relevant indices. It associates the transition family with affine lines and applies the published point-line incidence theorem of **Stevens and de Zeeuw**. The author supplies a Lean development for internal algebraic lemmas, but states that the crucial external incidence theorem remains an imported, unproved dependency in that formalization.

The algebraic identity is valid, and the first transition-counting lemma is checkable. The claimed exponent, however, depends on the entire multiplicity and incidence argument. **GCL has not independently verified the complete proof, its field-characteristic conditions, or the external Lean dependency.** The preprint is not yet a peer-reviewed, independently certified improvement. It is recorded as a *research claim requiring adjudication*, not silently promoted to the established bound.

Even if the claimed improvement holds, the original $(1-1/e)p$ conjecture remains open because $p^{8/15}=o(p)$.

A related but different question concerns so-called **socialist primes**: primes for which $2!,3!,\ldots,(p-1)!$ are all distinct modulo $p$. The known example $p=5$ satisfies this. Whether there are infinitely many such primes is separate from the conjecture about the typical cardinality $|A_p|$.

## What remains

A focused mathematical review should independently examine the July 2026 proof. It must match every hypothesis of the Stevens–de Zeeuw incidence theorem, check multiplicities in the associated affine-line family, and verify that the characteristic bound applies in the full claimed parameter range. It must also test the distinction between the internal Lean lemmas and the externally assumed incidence estimate.

For the original Erdős problem, the actual missing theorem is much stronger:

$$
\lim_{\substack{p\to\infty\\p\ \mathrm{prime}}}
\frac{|A_p|}{p}=1-\frac1e.
$$

Neither the published 2024 square-root bound nor the claimed 2026 super-square-root bound approaches a proof of this specific limit.

The source-critical successor can end when it has an independently checked theorem–hypothesis correspondence, exact source commits and hashes, finite adversarial fixtures, and a proof-validity verdict. If an indispensable published theorem or formal dependency cannot be verified, it should preserve the blocker instead of claiming mathematical closure.
