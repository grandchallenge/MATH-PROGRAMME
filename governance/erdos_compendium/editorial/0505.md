**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #505 is **DISPROVED**: Borsuk's conjecture fails in sufficiently large Euclidean dimensions. Kahn and Kalai proved this in 1993. A peer-reviewed construction gives a counterexample in dimension **64** (Jenrich–Brouwer, 2014); a newer **May 2026 unpublished, script-verified** construction reports dimension **63** (Max Grinsztajn). The smallest dimension in which the conjecture fails is still unknown.

## The question in plain language

The **diameter** of a bounded set $S$ in Euclidean space is

$$
\mathrm{diam}(S)=\sup_{x,y\in S}\|x-y\|.
$$

Karol Borsuk asked whether every bounded set $S\subseteq\mathbb R^d$ with **positive diameter** can be divided into at most $d+1$ pieces $S_i$, each with **strictly smaller** diameter:

$$
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{d+1}S_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{diam}(S_i)<\mathrm{diam}(S).
$$

The diameter can be rescaled to one, but the strict inequality is essential. Allowing pieces of equal diameter would make the question trivial. Overlap among the pieces does not improve a minimal finite covering: each point can be assigned to one of the pieces containing it, so a covering can be replaced by a partition without increasing any diameter.

The assertion is true for $d=1,2,3$; Eggleston proved the three-dimensional case. Erdős recorded the higher-dimensional question in 1961. It is false in general. The maintained [Erdős #505 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/505) records **DISPROVED (LEAN)**, although its account of the *smallest currently known failing dimension* predates a May 2026 development.

## A small example

In the plane, the three vertices of an equilateral triangle have the same pairwise distance. To place them into pieces with smaller diameter, **no two vertices may share a piece**. Thus this three-point configuration requires three pieces, exactly the number $d+1$ with $d=2$.

In dimension $d$, the $d+1$ vertices of a regular $d$‑simplex similarly all lie at the same positive distance from each other. All $d+1$ pieces are necessary. These are *sharpness examples*, not counterexamples to Borsuk: they require **exactly** the permitted number of pieces, not more.

For a finite point set $S$, the problem can be translated exactly into **graph colouring**. Make a graph whose vertices are the points and whose edges join pairs at the **full diameter** of $S$:

$$
xy\in E(G_S)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\|x-y\|=\mathrm{diam}(S).
$$

A subset of $S$ has diameter strictly smaller than $\mathrm{diam}(S)$ **if and only if** it contains no edge of $G_S$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{minimum number of smaller-diameter pieces of finite }S=\chi(G_S),}
$$

where $\chi$ denotes the graph's chromatic number. This equivalence is elementary and exact for **finite** configurations; for arbitrary infinite sets one must account for suprema of distances that need not be attained.

The graph viewpoint explains a simple counting obstruction. If every independent set in $G_S$ contains at most $r$ vertices, then each colour class has size at most $r$, so at least

$$
\left\lceil\frac{|S|}{r}\right\rceil
$$

pieces are needed. Both the 2014 and 2026 low-dimensional constructions exploit this principle with highly structured graphs.

## What is known

### The 1993 Kahn–Kalai counterexample

P. Erdős, *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.244, III.3, [original scan](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=24), states the Borsuk problem and cites its successful low-dimensional cases. The original page was visually inspected.

Jeff Kahn and Gil Kalai, *A counterexample to Borsuk's conjecture*, *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* **29** (1993), 60–62, [complete original preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9307229), proved that if $b(d)$ is the largest number of smaller-diameter parts required by a bounded set in $\mathbb R^d$, then

$$
b(d)\ge(1.2)^{\sqrt d}
\qquad\text{for all sufficiently large }d.
$$

This grows faster than $d+1$, disproving Borsuk's universal conjecture. The original Theorem 1, construction and remarks on printed pp.1–3 were visually inspected. Their proof converts equal bipartitions of a finite set into a Euclidean distance configuration and invokes Frankl and Wilson's forbidden-intersection theorem. The paper explicitly reports counterexamples at $d=1325$ and for every $d>2014$. The imported Frankl–Wilson proof and all analytic asymptotic estimates were not independently reconstructed here.

### The peer-reviewed dimension-64 construction

Thomas Jenrich and Andries E. Brouwer, *A 64-dimensional counterexample to Borsuk's conjecture*, *Electronic Journal of Combinatorics* **21** (2014), P4.29, provide a concrete two-distance set of **352 points** in $\mathbb R^{64}$.

Their [complete three-page publisher PDF](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/v21i4p29/pdf/) was read directly. The paper uses the strongly regular $G_2(4)$ graph, with $416$ vertices and parameters $(416,100,36,20)$. Its Euclidean representation has squared distances $144$ and $192$ depending on adjacency. The authors select a $352$‑point subset in a $64$‑dimensional subspace. Every smaller-diameter subset corresponds to a graph clique and has at most **five** points. Consequently at least

$$
\left\lceil\frac{352}{5}\right\rceil=71>65=64+1
$$

pieces are required. Their Theorem 1 on printed p.3 is a published, rigorous counterexample, independently of any more recent construction. The group-theoretic existence facts invoked in that three-page proof were not rederived from first principles in this audit.

### The newer May 2026 dimension-63 claim

Max Grinsztajn's [May 2026 proof note and verification repository](https://github.com/maaxgrin/borsuk-63-counterexample) reports a modification of the $G_2(4)$ construction: **321 points** in $\mathbb R^{63}$, with every subset of smaller diameter containing at most five points. The counting obstruction gives

$$
\left\lceil\frac{321}{5}\right\rceil=65>64=63+1.
$$

The [Terence Tao optimization-problems record](https://teorth.github.io/optimizationproblems/constants/28a.html) credits Grinsztajn with the dimension-63 bound and identifies the remaining possible first-failure range as dimensions $4$ through $62$. The later construction is **unpublished and not peer reviewed**; it must not be presented as a 2014 result.

That is valid for its publication date but does not reflect Grinsztajn's later public claim. The general DISPROVED classification has not changed.
