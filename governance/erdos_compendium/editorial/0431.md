## The question in plain language

Could all sufficiently large prime numbers be expressed as sums from two fixed **infinite** sets, and could those sums avoid all sufficiently large composite numbers? Erdős Problem 431 asks whether there exist infinite sets $A,B\subseteq\mathbb N$ whose sumset

$$
A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\}
$$

agrees with the set of primes **up to finitely many exceptions**. This means that beyond some threshold every prime is in $A+B$ and every integer in $A+B$ is prime. The formulation permits a finite number of missing primes and unwanted composites, but no infinite collection of either.

The question is known as **Ostmann's inverse Goldbach problem**. Ordinary Goldbach-type questions ask whether each even integer can be represented by primes. Ostmann asks the opposite kind of structural question: can a set with the irregular pattern of the primes itself be decomposed into the complete sumset of two infinite component sets?

One must not confuse this with constructing infinitely many **individual prime sums** or a restricted triangular collection of prime sums. A full Cartesian sumset $A+B$ includes every pair, not only selected pairs.

## A small example

Take $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{2,3\}$. Their sumset is

$$
A+B=\{3,4,5\}.
$$

The element $4$ is composite, so this finite example is not a set consisting only of primes. However, a finite defective example does not disprove Ostmann's conjecture, because the original question concerns *infinite* sets and explicitly tolerates finitely many exceptional values.

There is an elementary parity obstruction to any proposed full decomposition. Suppose $A$ contains one even and one odd number. For any sufficiently large $b\in B$, the two sums formed with those two elements have opposite parity. One is even and greater than $2$, so it is composite. Since $B$ is infinite, this gives infinitely many distinct composite members of $A+B$, contradicting agreement with the primes outside a finite set. Thus **all members of $A$ must have one parity**. The same argument applies to $B$.

Since all sufficiently large primes are odd, the two fixed parity classes must be opposite: one summand set contains only even integers, the other only odd integers. This constraint is necessary, but far from sufficient. Removing parity obstructions does not control composite values arising from other small prime divisors.

## What is known

The problem was proposed by Alexander Ostmann and discussed by Erdős in 1961 and 1977, in his 1980 survey, and with Graham in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed p. 85 of the [historical monograph](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf).

Christian Elsholtz and Adam Harper, *Additive decompositions of sets with restricted prime factors*, *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* (2015), Theorem 2.6, proved strong restrictions on any hypothetical representation. If infinite $A,B$ existed as described, each counting function would satisfy, for large $x$,

$$
\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log x\,\log\log x}
\ll |A\cap[1,x]|
\ll \sqrt{x}\log\log x,
$$

and the same for $B$. These are necessary size bounds, not a construction of the desired sets or a proof of impossibility.

A related theorem of Elsholtz (2001) rules out a decomposition into **three** nontrivial summand sets. Tao and Ziegler's 2023 *Infinite partial sumsets in the primes* constructs infinite sequences with certain **triangular** cross-sums prime, which does not imply that all pairs form primes. These distinctions are part of the established mathematical landscape.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #431](https://www.erdosproblems.com/431) page, last edited April 8, 2026, labels the full two-set problem **OPEN**. But there has been a material development **after that page's last edit**. On October 6, 2026, OpenAI published a [mathematical research collection](https://github.com/openai/math). Its [overview, family 013](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/overview.tex) claims that no finite modification of the primes is a sumset $A+B$ even when both summands merely contain at least two nonnegative integers. The linked manuscript is titled *The additive indecomposability of the primes* (dated September 24, 2026). If mathematically correct, this stronger theorem would answer Ostmann's original question **negatively**.

An announced proof and its independent verification are different evidence classes. The repository explicitly states that its manuscript families have different levels of formal verification and that some unverified results may contain errors. An inspected [Ostmann comparator file](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/ComparatorChallenges/OstmannComplete.lean) defines both the strong inverse-Goldbach statement and the original infinite-summand impossibility statement, but the displayed proof bodies are **by sorry**. Other proof modules exist in the repository; that observation alone neither invalidates them nor certifies a complete Lean replay. Consequently the October announcement should be treated as a **new externally claimed resolution requiring mathematical and formal-source review**, not silently substituted for a verified theorem.
