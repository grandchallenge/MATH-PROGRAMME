**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #516, a version of **Pólya's gap-series conjecture**, is **PROVED** by W. H. J. Fuchs (1963). It concerns how close the smallest and largest moduli of a finite-order entire function can be along circles when the nonzero Taylor coefficients are sparse. A source-fidelity issue is essential: Erdős's 1961 printed notation uses $m(r)$ for the *largest Taylor term*, whereas the Pólya–Fuchs theorem concerns the *minimum modulus on a circle*. These are different quantities.

## The question in plain language

Suppose an entire function has a **gap power series**

$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_k z^{n_k},\qquad a_k\ne0,
$$

with strictly increasing positive integers $n_k$. Assume that the gaps are sparse enough in the **Fabry sense** that

$$
\frac{n_k}{k}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

Also require $f$ to be of **finite order**, meaning, informally, that its maximum modulus grows no faster than $\exp(Cr^\rho)$ for some fixed constants $C,\rho$ and all sufficiently large $r$.

For each radius $r>0$, set

$$
M(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|,\qquad
m_{\min}(r)=\min_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.
$$

Pólya's question asks whether

$$
\boxed{
\limsup_{r\to\infty}
\frac{\log m_{\min}(r)}{\log M(r)}
=1.
}
$$

At a radius where $m_{\min}(r)=0$, interpret $\log m_{\min}(r)$ as $-\infty$ when appropriate. The assertion concerns the **limit superior**, so it is about arbitrarily large circles where the minimum modulus is close to the maximum modulus on a *logarithmic scale*. It does **not** say that the ratio tends to one along every increasing sequence of radii.

For large radii where the logarithms are positive, $m_{\min}(r)\le M(r)$ and hence the ratio is at most one. The theorem says that along an unbounded sequence of suitable radii this upper limit is attained.

## A small example

First examine a polynomial, which is **not** a general gap-series witness but sharply illustrates the definitions:

$$
f(z)=1+z^q,\qquad q\ge1.
$$

On a circle of radius $r$, the complex number $z^q$ runs through the circle of radius $r^q$. The largest and smallest possible magnitudes of $1+z^q$ are

$$
M(r)=1+r^q,\qquad m_{\min}(r)=|1-r^q|.
$$

The **largest Taylor term**, by contrast, is

$$
\mu(r)=\max\{1,r^q\}.
$$

These are **three different functions**. At $r=1$, $m_{\min}(r)=0$, but $\mu(r)=1$ and $M(r)=2$. This exact example explains why silently exchanging the minimum modulus and maximum term changes the problem.

Now consider two exponent sequences:

$$
n_k=2^k \quad\text{and}\quad n_k=k^2.
$$

Both satisfy $n_k/k\to\infty$. But the reciprocal gap sums behave differently:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-2^k}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}<\infty,
$$

whereas

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)^2-k^2}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2k+1}=\infty.
$$

The second divergence follows by comparison with the harmonic series. This distinction matters because Erdős and Macintyre's 1954 theorem handled the **stronger reciprocal-gap condition**. Fuchs's later finite-order theorem covers both sequences under the Fabry condition.

A concrete entire example with square exponents is

$$
g(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k^2}}{(k^2)!}.
$$

It has infinitely many nonzero coefficients, hence is not a polynomial, and obeys

$$
|g(z)|\le\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|z|^n}{n!}=e^{|z|}.
$$

Therefore it has finite order at most one and fulfills the hypotheses of the Fuchs theorem. The inequality alone, however, is **not a proof** of the minimum-modulus conclusion.

## What is known

### Erdős's printed notation versus Pólya's original problem

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed [p.249](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=29) and [p.250](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=30), Erdős poses Pólya's problem, including the Fabry gaps $n_k/k\to\infty$. Both original pages were visually inspected.

However, **Problem IV.5 on the same printed p.249 defines $m(r)$ as the largest Taylor term**, namely $\max_n|a_n r^n|$. Problem IV.7 then uses $m(r)$ in a purported Pólya statement, without replacing that earlier definition. The modern [Erdős #516 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/516), following Pólya and later proofs, defines $m(r)$ as the **minimum modulus on the circle**.

This discrepancy is substantive. An inequality about a large Taylor coefficient concerns the composition of the series. An inequality about minimum modulus controls **all points** of an entire circle. The article uses $\mu(r)$ for largest term and $m_{\min}(r)$ for minimum circle modulus.

### The 1954 partial theorem and the 1963 solution

Paul Erdős and A. J. Macintyre, *Integral functions with gap power series*, *Proceedings of the Edinburgh Mathematical Society* (2) **10** (1954), 62–70, [original paper](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1954-01.pdf), proved a stronger ratio estimate under a stronger hypothesis. Their Theorem 1 treats increasing exponents with

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k+1}-n_k}<\infty,
$$

and obtains

$$
\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{m_{\min}(r)}{M(r)}=1.
$$

That theorem does not need finite order. However, the convergent reciprocal-gap condition is stronger than $n_k/k\to\infty$, as the square-exponent example demonstrates. Their Theorem 2 exhibits sharpness of the reciprocal-gap requirement for this **unlogged** ratio. The hypotheses were cross-checked against source-indexed original-paper analysis; the complete 1954 proof was not independently reconstructed in this tranche.

W. H. J. Fuchs, *Proof of a conjecture of G. Pólya concerning gap series*, *Illinois Journal of Mathematics* **7** (1963), no.4, 661–667, [DOI 10.1215/ijm/1255645102](https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1255645102), established the intended finite-order result:

$$
\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log m_{\min}(r)}{\log M(r)}=1
\quad\text{if }n_k/k\to\infty.
$$

The primary publisher bibliography was verified. The complete original seven-page proof could not be retrieved, so analytic estimates are **attributed to the published theorem**, not independently reproduced. A source review describes the stronger consequence that for every $\varepsilon>0$, $\log m_{\min}(r)>(1-\varepsilon)\log M(r)$ outside a set of radii of logarithmic density zero; that result was not independently reproved here.

Thomas Kővári, *A gap-theorem for entire functions of infinite order*, *Michigan Mathematical Journal* (1965), 133–140, treats stronger gaps, such as $n_k>k(\log k)^{2+c}$, which can compensate for omitting finite order. This does not remove every restriction.
