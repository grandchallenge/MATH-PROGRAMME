## The question in plain language

Let \(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\) be a **nonconstant irreducible polynomial** that takes **positive integer values** \(f(n)\ge1\) for all sufficiently large positive integers \(n\). Write
\[
\tau(m)=\#\{d\in\mathbb Z_{>0}:d\mid m\}
\]
for the number of positive divisors of \(m\). Erdős asked whether there is a constant \(c(f)>0\) such that
\[
\boxed{\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))\sim c(f)X\log X}
\qquad(X\to\infty).
\]
The notation means that **the quotient of the sum by \(X\log X\) converges to one specific positive limit**. Matching upper and lower bounds of order \(X\log X\) are **not** sufficient.

The maintained [Erdős Problem #975](https://www.erdosproblems.com/975) classifies the **general polynomial assertion OPEN**. It is known for every admissible **irreducible quadratic** but remains unproved in general for higher degrees. A July 2026 source and computation audit found no valid published asymptotic for a fixed irreducible cubic such as \(f(x)=x^3+2\); that is a report of a literature search, not a proof no such result exists. [Independent technical report](https://erdosproblemaday.com/report/975).

## Why the hypotheses are necessary

**Nonconstant** excludes the trivial counterexample \(f(x)=1\), for which \(\tau(f(n))=1\) and \(\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))\asymp X\), not \(X\log X\).

**Irreducible** excludes polynomials such as \(f(x)=x(x+1)\), whose divisor statistics have different, typically \(X(\log X)^2\)-scale main terms. A result about an irreducible quadratic cannot be applied to a product of two linear factors.

**Eventually positive** ensures the ordinary divisor-count function on positive integers is defined on every term of the asymptotic tail. Finitely many omitted or undefined initial values must be handled by beginning the sum at a fixed large threshold; they do not affect the asymptotic. If \(f\) has even degree and negative leading coefficient, eventual positivity fails; the conjecture does not assert a positive-\(n\) divisor asymptotic in that case.

The sum counts divisors of **the values \(f(n)\)**, not the number of roots of \(f\) modulo \(n\), and not the number of divisors of the coefficient polynomial.

## An exact small example

For \(f(n)=n^2+1\), which is irreducible over \(\mathbb Z\), the first eight values and divisor counts are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
n&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\
f(n)&2&5&10&17&26&37&50&65\\
\tau(f(n))&2&2&4&2&4&2&6&4
\end{array}
\]
and hence
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{8}\tau(n^2+1)=26.
\]
The maintained catalogue states the established asymptotic
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(n^2+1)
=\frac3\pi X\log X+O(X).
\]
The finite sum 26 is an exact calculation. It is **not** a numerical proof of the coefficient \(3/\pi\), and the linear-sized error term is too large to make tiny-\(X\) ratios reliable.

For a **linear** example \(f(n)=n+1\), the classical Dirichlet divisor asymptotic gives
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(n+1)=X\log X+O(X),
\]
so \(c(f)=1\) in this elementary case. This does not extend by the same argument to a higher-degree polynomial.

## What is established

Van der Corput [Va39] proved the lower estimate
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))\gg_f X\log X,
\]
and Erdős [Er52b] proved the matching upper estimate
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))\ll_f X\log X.
\]
Together they give
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))\asymp_f X\log X.
\]
These statements confirm **the correct order of magnitude**, but they do **not** imply the ratio converges, let alone that its limit equals a particular arithmetic constant \(c(f)>0\).

Christopher Hooley proved the required asymptotic for irreducible quadratic polynomials in [*On the number of divisors of quadratic polynomials*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02391856), *Acta Mathematica* **110** (1963), pp.97–114. Later papers by James McKee [Mc95], [Mc97] and [Mc99] give formulas for the leading constants of various quadratic families. Those constants are **not universally equal to 2**, nor do they depend only on the polynomial's degree. They reflect arithmetic properties of the polynomial, including splitting behavior in the associated quadratic field. For the example \(n^2+1\), the established coefficient is \(3/\pi\).

No primary proof of the full higher-degree conjecture was identified during this source review. The 2026 technical audit checked contemporary divisor-in-short-interval and *binary* cubic-value results and explained why neither supplies an asymptotic for an irreducible **univariate cubic**. [Detailed background and exact finite work](https://www.erdosproblemaday.com/report/975). This distinction rules out falsely promoting a two-variable polynomial average to the one-variable question.

## A useful exact reduction to congruence roots

For a positive integer \(q\) define
\[
\rho_f(q)=\#\{a\bmod q:f(a)\equiv0\pmod q\}.
\]
The number of \(n\le X\) for which \(q\mid f(n)\) is exactly a count of up to \(\rho_f(q)\) arithmetic progressions of modulus \(q\). For each **fixed** \(q\),
\[
\#\{1\le n\le X:q\mid f(n)\}
=\frac{\rho_f(q)}qX+O(\rho_f(q)).
\]
The error here follows by counting terms in each residue class; it is uniform in \(X\), but the number and size of relevant moduli grow with \(X\).

The divisor identity
\[
\tau(f(n))=\sum_{q\mid f(n)}1
\]
then gives the **exact finite** rearrangement
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\tau(f(n))
=\sum_{q\ge1}
 \#\{1\le n\le X:q\mid f(n)\},
\]
where only the finitely many \(q\) dividing some \(f(n)\) for \(n\le X\) contribute, and terms with \(f(n)\le0\) must be excluded if the sum begins before eventual positivity.

This is the tempting route to a leading constant built from local congruence-root densities. But replacing every individual counting term by \(\rho_f(q)X/q\) and summing all \(O(\rho_f(q))\) errors is **not a proof**: the moduli may be as large as \(O_f(X^{\deg f})\), so their cumulative endpoint errors can far exceed \(X\log X\).

## The large-divisor obstruction beyond degree two

Divisors of a positive integer \(M\) pair as \(q\) and \(M/q\). Up to square correction,
\[
\tau(M)=2\sum_{\substack{q\mid M\\q\le\sqrt M}}1
 - {\bf1}_{M\text{ is a square}}.
\]
Applying this to \(M=f(n)\), if \(\deg f=d\) and \(n\le X\), the relevant *small-divisor side* reaches moduli of order \(X^{d/2}\).

For \(d=2\), that is approximately \(X\), near the length of the \(n\)-averaging interval, and Hooley's method can exploit arithmetic information about roots of quadratic congruences. For \(d=3\), the divisor cutoff is on the order of \(X^{3/2}\), **larger** than the interval of sampled \(n\). The bound
\[
\#\{n\le X:q\mid f(n)\}
=\frac{\rho_f(q)}qX+O(\rho_f(q))
\]
becomes too weak to sum naively over that much larger range. A successful cubic proof needs a genuinely new mechanism for averaged root-distribution/cancellation at these large moduli, not a simple extension of quadratic bookkeeping.

The sharpest research obligation is to show that the contribution of the **long-divisor range** has a well-defined coefficient and negligible remainder, uniformly for the given irreducible cubic. Merely finding a plausible local-density Euler product for \(c(f)\) is insufficient without such a power-saving or \(o(X\log X)\) control.

## Formal-statement fidelity

The 2025 catalogue revision clarified that the polynomial is **nonconstant** and **irreducible**, after the original web paraphrase allowed the constant polynomial and an earlier comment discussed reducible cases. The positive-value assumption, integer coefficients and the dependency of the limit constant on \(f\) are likewise essential.

The power \(X\log X\) and \(c(f)>0\) must not change.

## GCL's current state

The definition, elementary congruence-root counting lemma, divisor identities and small data are directly checked mathematical facts. Van der Corput, Erdős, Hooley and McKee are source-attributed established results; the article does **not** claim to reprove their analytic estimates. The broad asymptotic remains **OPEN**. Independent computation can test a conjectured constant for a named cubic but cannot certify convergence of the normalized average.
