## The question in plain language

Does EVERY fixed integer a admit infinitely many positive n with phi(n) dividing n+a? The a=0 subcase has infinitely many trivial examples but does not solve all shifts. An auxiliary catalogue sentence incorrectly omits restrictions on a,b exponents when characterizing phi(n)|n.

## A small example

phi(1)=1 divides1; phi(2)=1 divides2; phi(6)=2 divides6; phi(9)=6 does not divide9. For a=0 the infinite family 2^a (a>=1) works, but this proves nothing about every shift a.

The example can be checked exactly. A finite witness does not replace the universal, asymptotic, or infinitely-often quantifiers in the original question.

## What is known

Maintained https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/828 OPEN; Graham's conjecture. Lehmer's n-1 conjecture separately says phi(n)|n-1 only for primes, with n>1; open. Catalogue shorthand 'n=2^a3^b' is false if a=0,b>=1, e.g. n=3 gives phi(3)=2 not dividing 3. Exact positive n characterization: n=1 OR n=2^a 3^b with a>=1,b>=0; formal-conjectures Lean accompanying theorem states n<=1 or a>0. The formal conjecture's Lean `sorry` is not GCL proof replay.

The catalogue's auxiliary shorthand needs a boundary correction. For **positive** $n$,

$$

\varphi(n)\mid n
\iff
n=1\quad\text{or}\quad
n=2^a3^b\ \text{for }a\geq1,\ b\geq0.

$$

A positive power of 3 alone fails: $\varphi(3)=2$ does not divide 3. The result follows by inspecting the factors $p-1$ in $\varphi(n)=\prod_{p^e\Vert n}p^{e-1}(p-1)$; every odd prime divisor supplies a factor two, constraining its possible values. Even a rigorous proof of the $a=0$ shift case does not settle all other shifts $a\in\mathbb Z$.
