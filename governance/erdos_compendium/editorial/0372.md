## The question in plain language

For an integer $n>1$, let $P(n)$ be its largest prime factor. Erdős Problem #372 asks whether the prime factors at the tops of three consecutive integers can fall strictly at every step:

$$
P(n)>P(n+1)>P(n+2).
$$

The question is existential and infinite: are there infinitely many starting values $n$ with this pattern? It is not asking that every triple behave this way, nor that the three integers themselves be prime. For example, a composite integer can have a very large prime factor, while its neighbors may have only smaller prime factors. Strict inequalities matter: ties do not count.

The maintained problem page says that Erdős and Pomerance conjectured this descending pattern and that Balog later proved it. It also reports a stronger quantitative statement, namely that the number of such $n\le x$ is at least a constant multiple of $\sqrt{x}$ for all sufficiently large $x$. That stronger assertion is useful context, but it is kept separate here from the exact infinitude statement because the primary Balog paper could not be opened during this audit.

## A small example

The first simple witness is $n=13$. Exact factorization gives

$$
13=13,\qquad 14=2\cdot7,\qquad 15=3\cdot5.
$$

Therefore

$$
P(13)=13>7=P(14)>5=P(15).
$$

This calculation can be reproduced by trial division: divide each of $13$, $14$, and $15$ by primes up to its square root, retain the largest prime divisor, and compare the three retained values. An exact enumeration using the same integer factorization procedure found 72 witnesses among $2\le n\le500$, beginning with $13,14,34,37,38,43,61,62,73,79,86,94$. The adjacent starts $13$ and $14$ are not a mistake: they refer to overlapping triples.

The finite calculation establishes only that the pattern occurs at these checked values. It cannot establish infinitude, the asymptotic lower bound reported for Balog's result, or any density such as the later heuristic value $1/6$. A computation also does not substitute for a proof that the factorization routine was applied to every integer beyond the tested range.

## What is known

The live [Erdős Problems entry for #372](https://www.erdosproblems.com/372), accessed 2026-10-13, gives the exact statement and attributes the resolution to Antal Balog, “On triplets with descending largest prime factors,” *Studia Scientiarum Mathematicarum Hungarica* 38 (2001), 45–50, DOI [10.1556/SScMath.38.2001.1-4.3](https://doi.org/10.1556/SScMath.38.2001.1-4.3). The public abstract says that infinitely many consecutive triples have descending largest prime factors. The maintained page additionally reports the $\gg\sqrt{x}$ lower bound for starts $n\le x$.

The primary paper is the relevant proof source, but its publisher page returned an access denial in this investigation and no stable full-text copy was located within the bounded timebox. Consequently, author, journal, year, volume, pages, DOI, and the abstract-level conclusion are recorded; a theorem number, exact proof hypotheses, and the precise location of the quantitative lower bound remain unverified from the paper itself. This is a source-access limitation, not evidence against the published result.

The earlier primary paper by Paul Erdős and Carl Pomerance, “On the largest prime factors of $n$ and $n+1$,” *Aequationes Mathematicae* 17 (1978), 311–321, is available from [Carl Pomerance's copy](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper17.pdf). It studies comparisons of largest prime factors of consecutive integers and discusses the difficulty of extending such questions to three or more consecutive values. It is historical and contextual evidence for #372, not the proof of the descending triple claim.
