## The question in plain language

Take the binomial coefficient

$$
\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

For $k\ge2$, look at the $k$ consecutive integers $n,n-1,\ldots,n-k+1$. Erdős and Selfridge observed that, when $n\ge2k$, at least one of these integers fails to divide $\binom nk$.

Define $n_k$ as the smallest $n\ge2k$ for which **exactly one** of those $k$ divisibility tests fails. [Erdős Problem #1063](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1063) asks how quickly $n_k$ grows with $k$.

## A small example

For $k=4$ and $n=8$,

$$
\binom84=70.
$$

Both $8$ and $6$ fail to divide $70$, so $n=8$ does not qualify. For $n=9$, however,

$$
\binom94=126.
$$

Among $9,8,7,6$, only $8$ fails to divide $126$. Therefore $n_4=9$, since $n\ge2k=8$ rules out any smaller candidate.

Independent exact-integer enumeration gives

$$
(n_2,n_3,\ldots,n_9)
=(4,6,9,12,75,30,70,56).
$$

The sequence is not monotone across these small values. This is a finite observation, not a general growth theorem.

## What is known

Erdős and Selfridge posed the question as Problem 6447 in the *American Mathematical Monthly* (1983), page 710. Jean-Marie Monier's published solution (1985), pp. 435–436, records an upper bound $n_k\le k!$ for $k\ge3$. Stijn Cambie's later observation improves this to

$$
n_k\le k\,\mathrm{lcm}(2,3,\ldots,k-1),
$$

and the standard asymptotic for the least common multiple yields an exponential upper bound of roughly $\exp((1+o(1))k)$. These bounds do not determine the true order of growth.

The maintained [catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1063) remains open. The full original 1983 and 1985 proofs have not yet been independently inspected in GCL's source audit.

Our local enumeration checks the first eight values directly from integer binomial coefficients. No deep upper-bound argument or later external formal development has been independently kernel-replayed by GCL.

## What remains

A true resolution would determine the growth of $n_k$ much more precisely. Near-term source work should inspect the original papers and prove the least-common-multiple upper bound from first principles, preserving the exact “one exceptional divisor” quantifier. Larger exact searches and proof replay are useful tests but cannot establish the asymptotic order by themselves.
