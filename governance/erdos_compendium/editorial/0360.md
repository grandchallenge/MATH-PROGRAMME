## The question in plain language

Fix a positive integer $n$. Colour each of the integers $1,2,\ldots,n-1$ with one of some number of colours. A monochromatic subset sum is a sum of distinct integers that all have the same colour. Let $f(n)$ be the least number of colours for which one can arrange the colouring so that no monochromatic subset sum equals $n$.

The distinctness condition matters: an integer may be used at most once in a sum, because the available objects are the elements of the set $\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$. The question asks for the growth of $f(n)$ as $n$ becomes large. It is not asking whether $n$ has some representation as an unrestricted sum, and it is not the different problem of avoiding a target sum inside an arbitrary subset of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.

The problem is Erdős Problem 360 in the [original GCL issue](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1630). The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/360) attributes the question to Erdős and records its resolution through successive estimates.

## A small example

The smallest nontrivial check already illustrates the definition. For $n=5$, use two colour classes

$$
C_1=\{1,2\},\qquad C_2=\{3,4\}.
$$

The subset sums of $C_1$ are $0,1,2,3$, while those of $C_2$ are $0,3,4,7$. Neither list contains $5$, so two colours suffice. One colour cannot suffice, because the single class $\{1,2,3,4\}$ contains the distinct-element sums $1+4=5$ and $2+3=5$. Therefore the exact finite calculation is $f(5)=2$.

This is an exact-arithmetic verification of one small instance, not numerical evidence for the asymptotic theorem. It checks neither the constants hidden in asymptotic notation nor the analytic and additive-combinatorial arguments needed for large $n$.

## What is known

The live entry reports the earlier bounds of Alon and Erdős and the improvement by Vu. The directly inspected primary source is David Conlon, Jacob Fox, and Huy Tuan Pham, “Subset sums, completeness and colorings,” [arXiv:2104.14766v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14766v1), submitted 30 April 2021. In Section 1.2.1, Theorem 1.5, they use exactly the definition above and prove

$$
f(n)=\Theta\!\left(\frac{n^{1/3}(n/\phi(n))}{(\log n)^{1/3}(\log\log n)^{2/3}}\right),
$$

where $\phi(n)$ is Euler’s totient function. In particular, the paper supplies matching upper and lower bounds up to absolute constant factors. Its proof uses colourings organized by size and congruence information for the upper bound, and subset-sum structure for the lower bound.

The same paper identifies the earlier primary references as Noga Alon and Paul Erdős, “Sure monochromatic subset sums,” *Acta Arithmetica* 74 (1996), 269–272, and Van H. Vu, “Some new results on subset sums,” *Journal of Number Theory* (2007), 229–233. The live entry also gives Erdős and Graham, *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980), p. 59, as the catalogue source for the question. Those references explain the historical chain; the Conlon–Fox–Pham paper is the source directly checked here for the final order of growth.

There is also an important scope distinction inside the formula. The factor $n/\phi(n)$ means that the answer can fluctuate with the prime divisors of $n$; two nearby integers need not have comparable values for the same elementary reason. The theorem is therefore stronger than a bare statement such as $f(n)=n^{1/3+o(1)}$: it records the arithmetic dependence that controls those fluctuations. The paper explains this phenomenon immediately after Theorem 1.5 and describes the result as confirming the conjectured order, not as producing a single universal exact integer value for every $n$.

## What remains

A useful bounded successor would first freeze a statement record containing the set-theoretic definition of $f(n)$, the quantifiers in Theorem 1.5, the meaning of $\Theta$, and the domain convention for logarithms. It could then formalize a finite lemma such as $f(5)=2$, with explicit colour classes and exhaustive checking of the finitely many subsets. A later, separately scoped task could formalize the elementary upper-bound constructions for selected $n$ or selected congruence classes.

The asymptotic theorem is a substantially larger target. A responsible formalization would need definitions for colourings, distinct subset sums, Euler’s totient, asymptotic comparison, and the imported additive-number-theory estimates. A successful finite replay would not certify the theorem, and a Lean declaration containing `sorry` would be a theorem interface with an unresolved proof, not a checked proof.
