## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 405 asks about an exponential Diophantine equation mixing a factorial and a power. In the original positive-integer formulation, choose positive integers $a$ and $k$, and let $p$ be an odd prime. The question is whether

$$
(p-1)! + a^{p-1} = p^k
$$

can hold for only finitely many triples $(a,k,p)$. The factorial is the product of all positive integers below $p$; the right side is a pure power of the same prime $p$. The restriction to odd primes matters. If $p=2$, the equation becomes $1+a=2^k$, and the choice $a=2^k-1$ gives infinitely many solutions.

The question was recorded by Erdős and Graham in *Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory* (1980, p. 80). The original issue for this audit is [MATH-PROGRAMME issue #1675](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1675), assignment `ERDOS-CATALOGUE-0405-S01`.

In other words, the issue is not merely whether examples can be found: it asks for a global finiteness statement across every odd prime and every permitted pair of exponents.

## A small example

The three solutions reported by the modern problem entry can be checked with ordinary integer arithmetic:

$$
2!+1^2=2+1=3=3^1,
$$

$$
2!+5^2=2+25=27=3^3,
$$

and

$$
4!+1^4=24+1=25=5^2.
$$

I reproduced these calculations exactly, and an exhaustive finite search over odd primes $p\leq 11$, $1\leq a\leq20$, and $1\leq k\leq10$ returned precisely `[(1, 1, 3), (5, 3, 3), (1, 2, 5)]`. This is a useful sanity check on ordering and arithmetic, not a proof that no larger solution exists. It also does not check the analytic arguments in the papers.

## What is known

The readable primary proof is B. Brindza and P. Erdős, “On some diophantine problems involving powers and factorials,” *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society* 51 (1991), 1–7, [doi:10.1017/S1446788700033255](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788700033255). Their equation (6), printed p. 3, is the same equation with positive integers $a,k$ and prime $p>2$. Theorem 2, printed p. 4, states that an effectively computable absolute constant $C$ bounds every solution: $\max\{p,a,k\}<C$. That is a direct proof of finiteness, although it does not itself list the solutions.

The live [Erdős Problems #405 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/405), accessed 2026-10-13, reports the subsequent complete list and cites K. Yu and D. Liu, “A complete resolution of a problem of Erdős and Graham,” *Rocky Mountain Journal of Mathematics* 26 (1996), 1235–1244, [doi:10.1216/rmjm/1181072047](https://doi.org/10.1216/rmjm/1181072047). The authors’ institutional abstract states the same three triples. The Project Euclid full-text endpoint was captcha-blocked during this audit, so the Yu–Liu proof pages were not independently read here; the exact-list attribution is therefore reported as an external source claim, not as a new certification.

There is a small but important normalization issue. The papers say positive integers, while the formal Lean set uses natural numbers for $a$ and $k$ and omits explicit positivity. For odd prime $p$, the equation should force the zero cases away, but that implication is a separate lemma and has not been certified in this audit. Any formal reuse should state whether it is proving the natural-number encoding as written or first proving equivalence with the positive-integer statement.

## What remains

A bounded successor should obtain a readable copy of Yu–Liu (1996), record its theorem and page locations, and compare every quantifier and variable convention against Brindza–Erdős equation (6), the live page, and the later Lean file. It should separately prove or document the zero-parameter exclusion needed to align positive integers with the natural-number set. A lightweight formalization target is the finite arithmetic lemma for the three displayed triples plus the implication that any solution has $a>0$ and $k>0$; it must not be presented as the global theorem.
