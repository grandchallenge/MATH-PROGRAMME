## The question in plain language

Choose a finite set of primes $P$ with at least two members. List, in increasing order, every positive integer whose prime divisors all belong to $P$:

$$
1=a_1<a_2<a_3<\cdots.
$$

For each $n$, form the least common multiple of the first $n$ listed numbers, and ask whether the resulting reciprocal series is irrational:

$$
S(P)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{lcm}(a_1,\ldots,a_n)}.
$$

The quantifier is important. The problem asks whether, for every finite prime set $P$ satisfying $|P|\ge 2$, the corresponding number $S(P)$ is irrational. It does not ask whether one particular choice such as $P=\{2,3\}$ works, nor whether a finite partial sum is irrational. The same $P$ determines the whole infinite list and every later least common multiple.

The convention $a_1=1$ matters: $1$ has no prime divisors, so it is included for every $P$. Also, successive denominators may repeat. For example, adjoining $8$ after $6$ changes the LCM, but adjoining $9$ after $8$ changes it again; other terms can leave it unchanged. “Removing duplicate summands,” mentioned in the historical source, is a different series and must not be silently substituted for the one above.

## A small example

Take the set $P=\{2,3\}$. The first twelve numbers smooth with respect to $P$ and their running LCMs are

$$
(a_1,\ldots,a_{12})=(1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,16,18,24,27),
$$

and

$$
(L_1,\ldots,L_{12})=(1,2,6,12,12,24,72,72,144,144,144,432),
$$

where $L_n=\mathrm{lcm}(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$. Exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{12}\frac1{L_n}
=1+\frac12+\frac16+\frac1{12}+\frac1{12}+\frac1{24}+\frac1{72}+\frac1{72}+\frac1{144}+\frac1{144}+\frac1{144}+\frac1{432}
=\frac{52}{27}.
$$

This is independently reproducible with Python’s exact `Fraction` type:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import gcd
a = [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 16, 18, 24, 27]
L, current = [], 1
for n in a:
    current = current * n // gcd(current, n)
    L.append(current)
assert sum((Fraction(1, x) for x in L), Fraction()) == Fraction(52, 27)
```

The calculation checks the indexing and repeated denominators, not the infinite claim. No finite computation can decide irrationality of the limit merely by producing rational partial sums.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham state in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 65, that if the prime set is infinite, the analogous LCM-reciprocal series is irrational, and then ask what happens for a finite set with more than one prime. Erdős’s 1988 survey, p. 106, calls the infinite-prime assertion a simple exercise and says that the finite version “probably remains true.” Those passages establish the historical shape of Problem 269: the finite case is the unresolved target, not a consequence of the infinite case.

For an infinite set of primes, the smooth numbers have enough new prime-power structure for the elementary irrationality argument recorded by Erdős to apply. That result does not provide the needed conclusion for a fixed finite $P$. In the finite case, every number is built from a fixed collection of prime powers, and the LCM growth has a much more rigid multi-dimensional pattern.

The latest source audit also records a public reduction for the two-prime case. With $P=\{p,q\}$, it rewrites the series through a floor-sum and a Hecke–Mahler value, and reports a transcendence route using cited external theorems. The audit explicitly says that this route is not formalized or independently peer-reviewed there. It is therefore useful evidence about a possible resolution of the two-prime subfamily, but it is not a certified proof in this article and it does not settle all finite sets, beginning with three primes.

The live maintained entry labels the problem `OPEN` and says that finite computation cannot resolve it. Its note that the infinite-prime case is known is consistent with the two original papers. The historical Fibonacci Quarterly letter, including its statement about removing duplicate summands, was not inspected in this source audit; that unavailable source remains an exception rather than a basis for a stronger claim.

## What remains

The original finite-prime question remains open at the programme’s documentary boundary. A complete resolution would need a proof for every finite $P$ with $|P|\ge2$, or one rigorously verified counterexample. Establishing the two-prime route would still leave the three-prime and higher-prime cases. Conversely, a failed finite search is not evidence that the universal statement is false: the missing quantifier ranges over an infinite series and all admissible finite prime sets.

The most sharply bounded next task is the first unresolved case $P=\{2,3,5\}$. The audit proposes proving or refuting the exact cofinal residue-escape condition used by the three-prime carry reduction. Acceptance would require every quantifier, denominator, starting index, and tail estimate, or a rigorous counterexample showing that the condition fails infinitely often. Computation can test initial windows, but it cannot discharge a cofinal assertion.

Any formal or computational result must first reconcile the $a_0$ versus $a_1$ indexing and the additive $1$ in the formal series. A successful build, an external repository, or a theorem tagged as solved is evidence about an artifact, not by itself a proof of the mathematical statement. The source audit also identifies the exact three-prime artifact hashes and preserves their own boundary: structural LCM identities do not imply irrationality, and the carry-escape premise remains unproved.
