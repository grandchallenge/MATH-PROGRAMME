## The question in plain language

Let $U$ contain the **odd positive integers** which cannot be expressed as the sum of a prime and a power of two. Erdős asked whether $U$ could be decomposed into **one infinite arithmetic progression** together with a set of natural density zero.

[Erdős Problem #16](https://www.erdosproblems.com/16) has been **disproved**. The crucial distinction is that a set can *contain* an entire infinite arithmetic progression without being *equal* to a progression plus a negligible set. These statements say very different things about the rest of the exceptional integers.

## A small example

The integer $9=7+2$ is representable, as are $11=3+8$ and $13=11+2$. Including exponent zero also gives $3=2+1$. But for an odd sum $p+2^e$ with $e=0$, the prime must be $2$; therefore the exponent-zero convention only adds this one small odd represented number. Finite representation checks cannot decide the density or large-scale structure of all exceptions.

Suppose a theorem says that $U$ is **not** a union of *any finite collection* of infinite arithmetic progressions plus a density-zero set. Then it certainly cannot be a union of **one** progression plus such a set. This logical implication is simple; proving the stronger input theorem is not.

## What is known

Yong-Gao Chen, *A conjecture of Erdős on $p+2^k$*, arXiv:2312.04120 (2023), proves the stronger negative statement: the set of odd integers not represented in this manner is not a finite union of infinite arithmetic progressions and a density-zero remainder. This resolves Erdős's one-progression conjecture **negatively**.

Erdős had earlier proved that the exceptional set *contains* an infinite arithmetic progression. The negative theorem does not contradict that earlier existence result.
