## The question in plain language

In the cube $[3]^n=\{0,1,2\}^n$, how many vertices can be chosen without **three distinct collinear points**? Let $f_3(n)$ be the largest such set size. Erdős and Moser asked whether $f_3(n)/3^n\to0$ as the dimension grows. Lines are geometric: coordinates may rise or fall, and this is stronger than avoiding ordinary Hales–Jewett combinatorial lines.

## A small example

In $[3]^1$, at most two vertices avoid a line. In $[3]^2$, the diagonal $(0,0),(1,1),(2,2)$ and antidiagonal $(0,2),(1,1),(2,0)$ are both geometric lines. The antidiagonal is **not** a standard combinatorial line, since its coordinates vary in opposite directions. Exact finite optima begin $f_3(1)=2$, $f_3(2)=6$.

## What is known

**PROVED.** The density Hales–Jewett theorem of Furstenberg and Katznelson (1991) and the [D. H. J. Polymath elementary proof](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2012.175.3.6) imply the conjecture. Every fixed-density subset of $[3]^n$ contains a **combinatorial** line, hence a geometric line. Therefore the maximum density of a **geometric-line-free** subset tends to zero. This implication does not identify the exact finite values of $f_3(n)$. Moser's construction gives $f_3(n)\gg3^n/\sqrt n$, consistent with vanishing density.

## What remains

A bounded successor enumerates every geometric three-point line in $[3]^n$ for $n=1,2,3$, obtains exact maximal line-free subsets, and distinguishes them from Hales–Jewett combinatorial lines. Acceptance: the antidiagonal example is recognised as geometric but not combinatorial, and the first two optima are $2$ and $6$. Stop at finite semantics; do not infer the density theorem from these checks.
