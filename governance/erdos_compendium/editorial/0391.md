## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $N$, take the factorial $N!$. We want to split it into exactly $N$ positive integer factors, allowing repetition, and make the smallest factor as large as possible. Write the factors in nondecreasing order, $a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_N$, and define $t(N)$ to be the largest value of $a_1$ for which

$$
N!=a_1a_2\cdots a_N.
$$

The question asks how close $t(N)/N$ gets to $1/e$ as $N$ grows. More precisely, the historical formulation asks whether $t(N)/N$ tends to $1/e$ and whether it is eventually below $1/e$ by a correction of order $1/\log N$ for infinitely many $N$. The factors are not required to be distinct, and the statement has no upper bound on any individual factor. Those quantifiers matter: changing “exactly $N$ factors”, allowing only prime powers, or imposing a size cap gives a different problem.

The elementary upper bound comes from multiplying the factors: if every factor is at least $t$, then $N!\geq t^N$. Stirling's formula therefore gives $t(N)/N\leq 1/e+O(\log N/N)$. The interesting issue is whether the prime factors of $N!$ can be rearranged into $N$ factors that nearly attain this bound.

## A small example

The case $N=4$ can be checked without approximation. There is a valid factorization

$$
4!=24=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot3,
$$

so $t(4)\geq2$. If four factors were all at least $3$, their product would be at least $3^4=81>24$, so $t(4)\leq2$. Hence $t(4)=2$ exactly. This also illustrates why the finite value need not lie below $N/e$: here $2>4/e$.

The calculation verifies one instance of the definition and the basic product obstruction. It says nothing by itself about a limit, an asymptotic error term, or the validity of a proof for all sufficiently large $N$. Those are quantified statements over an unbounded parameter and require the source theorem.

## What is known

The primary proof source is Boris Alexeev, Evan Conway, Matthieu Rosenfeld, Andrew V. Sutherland, Terence Tao, Markus Uhr, and Kevin Ventullo, “Decomposing a factorial into large factors”, arXiv:2503.20170v4, revised 3 April 2026. Its introduction defines the same $t(N)$ and states Theorem 1.3 on printed page 6. Part (iv) proves, for sufficiently large $N$,

$$
\frac{t(N)}{N}=\frac{1}{e}-\frac{c_0}{\log N}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{1+c}N}\right),
$$

for an absolute constant $c>0$ and explicit $c_0=0.30441901\ldots$. Thus the limit is $1/e$, and the correction is substantially more precise than the earlier question required. The same theorem records effective finite bounds, including $t(N)\leq N/e$ for $N\neq1,2,4$ and $t(N)\geq N/3$ for $N\geq43632$, with the threshold stated as best possible.

The paper distinguishes established arguments from computation-assisted certificates. Its methods combine elementary prime-factor accounting, greedy constructions, linear and integer programming, rearrangement of small prime powers, and an asymptotic construction using numbers with no prime factor $2$ or $3$. The authors state that the certificates used for the theorem were checked in exact arithmetic, but that external report is not a GCL mathematical certification. The historical record is also important: Erdős, Selfridge, and Straus were reported to have had an earlier proof of the limiting statement, but the notes were lost; Erdős later described that assertion as only a conjecture. The current primary source is therefore the accessible proof of the registered statement.
