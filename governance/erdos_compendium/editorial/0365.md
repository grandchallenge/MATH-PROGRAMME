## The question in plain language

A positive integer is powerful when every prime factor occurs at least twice in its prime factorization. If $P(n)$ denotes that property, Erdős Problem 365 asks two related questions about consecutive integers. First, if $P(n)$ and $P(n+1)$ both hold, must one of the two numbers be a perfect square? Second, if

$$
N(x)=\#\{n:0<n\leq x,\ P(n),\ P(n+1)\},
$$

is the number of such pairs up to $x$, is $N(x)$ bounded by a fixed power of $\log x$ for all sufficiently large $x$? The first question has a negative answer. The second remains open in the sources checked here. These are different assertions: an infinite supply of square-containing pairs says nothing by itself about the growth of the pairs in which neither member is a square.

The relevant ambient convention matters. The maintained page speaks about positive integers, while the current formal file notes that Lean’s `Nat.Powerful` also includes $0$ and therefore adds the hypothesis $0<n$. The source-normalized first statement is therefore: for every $n\in\mathbb N$, if $0<n$, $P(n)$, and $P(n+1)$ hold, then either $n$ or $n+1$ is a square. The answer is false.

## A small example

The decisive finite example is

$$
12167=23^3,\qquad 12168=2^3\,3^2\,13^2,
$$

and $12168=12167+1$. Every prime divisor in these displayed factorizations has exponent at least two, so both numbers are powerful. They are not squares: exact arithmetic gives

$$
110^2=12100<12167<12168<12321=111^2.
$$

Thus this one pair refutes the universal square assertion. The calculation is reproducible directly from integer arithmetic: $23^3=12167$, $2^3\cdot3^2\cdot13^2=12168$, and the two strict square bounds above all evaluate exactly. It establishes a counterexample, not a classification of all consecutive powerful pairs, not an asymptotic estimate for $N(x)$, and not a proof that the displayed pair belongs to any particular infinite family.

The first part of the current external Lean source records this same example, including explicit checks of prime divisors and the square bounds. Its theorem is stated with `answer(False)`. That source is useful formalization evidence, but this article does not claim a Lean build or GCL certification.

## What is known

The original references distinguish two types. Type I pairs have a square member and arise from Pell equations. The maintained problem page gives the basic example $x^2=2^3y^2+1$: the pair $8y^2,x^2$ is consecutive and powerful whenever the Pell equation is solved, so there are infinitely many pairs of this kind.

Golomb’s primary paper, “Powerful Numbers,” *The American Mathematical Monthly* 77 (1970), 848–852, is the historical source associated with the definition and the initial consecutive-pair discussion. The journal record is available through [the DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.1970.11992598). The maintained page attributes the explicit $12167,12168$ observation to Golomb.

Walker’s primary paper, “Consecutive Integer Pairs of Powerful Numbers and Related Diophantine Equations,” *The Fibonacci Quarterly* 14 (1976), 111–116, is available as [the scanned article](https://www.fq.math.ca/Scanned/14-2/walker.pdf). Section 2 treats the Pell-equation, square-containing type. Section 3 treats the non-square type through equations of the form $mX^2-nY^2=\pm1$ together with a divisibility condition ensuring that $mX^2$ and $nY^2$ are powerful. Theorem 3.5 states the corresponding odd-power parametrization, and the page-116 example computes a consecutive pair from $7X^2-3Y^2=1$.

There is a normalization point to retain. The maintained page displays the related equation $7^3x^2=3^3y^2+1$ and attributes infinitely many counterexamples to Walker. The page-116 example in the scanned paper is printed in the squarefree-coefficient form $7X^2-3Y^2=1$. These may be related by the paper’s general framework, but the exact change of variables and the claim that the displayed cubic-coefficient equation itself is the one proved were not reconstructed here. The safe statement is that Walker gives a primary-source Type II mechanism and a concrete infinite-family framework; the exact displayed equation needs source-semantic reconciliation before stronger attribution.

## What remains

The smallest useful successor is a source-normalization task for the counting question. Its input should be the positive-integer definition of powerful numbers, the exact set counted by $N(x)$, and the original Golomb and Walker references. Its target should be one of two bounded outcomes: either a primary-source theorem with exact hypotheses that implies a polylogarithmic upper bound for $N(x)$, or a rigorously documented reduction showing the first missing lemma needed for such a bound.

The task should first reconcile the $7^3$ equation with Walker’s squarefree-coefficient notation, then isolate what Walker proves about Type II families versus what he does not say about their total counting function. Acceptance requires a page-located citation, normalized quantifiers, and a proof or reduction whose variables and positivity conditions match $N(x)$. A falsification is either a counterexample to the proposed reduction or a source passage showing that the claimed hypothesis is absent. Stop after the source reconciliation and one bounded reduction attempt if the primary text does not yield a counting theorem; do not turn a citation or finite computation into an asymptotic claim.
