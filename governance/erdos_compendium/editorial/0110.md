## The question in plain language

Graphs can have infinitely many vertices and require uncountably many colours. In particular, chromatic number $\aleph_1$ means no **countable** proper vertex colouring exists, but a colouring with the first uncountable cardinality of colours does.

Erdős, Hajnal and Szemerédi asked whether there is one universal function $F(n)$ such that every graph $G$ with $\chi(G)=\aleph_1$ contains, for all sufficiently large finite $n$, a finite subgraph with chromatic number $n$ on **at most $F(n)$ vertices**. The same $F$ must work for every such $G$; allowing the bound to depend on the host graph changes the assertion.

## A small example

Every finite $K_n$ needs $n$ colours, so a graph containing $K_n$ offers an $n$ chromatic subgraph with exactly $n$ vertices. However, there are high-chromatic graphs with no triangles. The existence of finite chromatic obstructions does not force a uniform upper bound on how many vertices are needed.

The classical de Bruijn–Erdős compactness theorem implies that an infinite-chromatic graph has finite subgraphs of arbitrarily high finite chromatic number. That proves their **existence**, not a host-independent rate at which they appear.

## What is known

**DISPROVED in ZFC.** Chris Lambie-Hanson's [*On the growth rate of chromatic numbers of finite subgraphs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08177), *Advances in Mathematics* 369 (2020), article 107176, shows that finite chromatic obstructions can appear arbitrarily slowly, even inside a graph with uncountable chromatic number.

In the primary theorem's formulation, for an arbitrary growth function $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ one constructs an uncountably chromatic graph $G$ such that every finite subgraph with fewer than $f(k)$ vertices has chromatic number less than $k$ (for $k\ge3$). This excludes any universal $F(k)$ by choosing $f$ to dominate it. The distinction between $<$ and $\le$ and integer shifts is harmless to this diagonal argument, but must be handled explicitly when formalizing.

An earlier theorem of Komjáth and Shelah gave the negative answer in a model of set theory; Lambie-Hanson's construction removes the extra-set-theoretic consistency qualification. This is not a statement about all graphs with chromatic number only $\aleph_0$.

## What remains

A bounded logical successor is to formalize the **contradiction in quantifiers**: assume a universal function $F$, apply Lambie-Hanson's imported theorem with $f(k)=F(k)+1$, and derive the absence of the asserted small $k$ chromatic subgraph in the constructed host. Acceptance: make the $\exists F\,\forall G\,\forall^\text{large}k$ versus $\forall f\,\exists G\,\forall k$ ordering explicit. Import the deep construction as a stated theorem; do not misrepresent this exercise as a proof of it.
