## The question in plain language

A positive integer $n$ is *practical* when every smaller positive integer can be expressed as a sum of **distinct** positive divisors of $n$. Let $h(n)$ be the largest, over target integers $1\le m\le n$, of the *smallest number of divisors* needed in such a representation. [Erdős Problem #18](https://www.erdosproblems.com/18) asks several related questions about finding practical numbers with surprisingly small $h(n)$, including growth conditions involving factorials.

The original statement uses $m<n$ rather than $m\le n$. For practical $n\ge2$ these produce the same maximum because the extra target $n$ is represented by the single divisor $n$; the boundary $n=1$ must be treated separately.

## A small example

Take $n=8$, whose positive divisors are $1,2,4,8$. Every integer from 1 through 7 has a binary expansion into distinct divisors. However, $7=1+2+4$ needs **three** summands, and no two of these divisors sum to 7. Thus $h(8)=3$.

More generally, for every integer $k\ge1$,

$$
h(2^k)=k.
$$

The binary representation supplies the upper bound, and the target $2^k-1$ requires all $k$ smaller powers of two. The endpoint $h(1)=1$ is a separate exception to the displayed formula with $k=0$.

## What is known

The external registry separates three unresolved questions, including an eventual subpolynomial estimate for $h(n!)$ and a sharper polylogarithmic form. It reports earlier bounds credited to Erdős and a theorem of Vose giving infinitely many practical integers with a square-root-logarithmic upper bound for $h(n)$. Those published claims are **source-reported** in the [audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1287#issuecomment-6104115714); their entire original analytic proofs were not independently replayed.

## GCL's current state

GCL's source audit directly checked the frozen practical-number and maximal-length definitions and reproduced $h(2^k)$ for $0\le k\le10$ with a distinct-summand subset-sum program. It identified that all three formal problem statements still have unfilled proofs and that a finite binary example does not resolve the growth questions.

## What remains

The central gap is between elementary representation lengths for powers of two and uniform efficient representations by divisors of much more complicated numbers, particularly factorials. Formalizing the exact binary lemma is useful mathematical infrastructure, but proving any global factorial bound needs additional arguments.
