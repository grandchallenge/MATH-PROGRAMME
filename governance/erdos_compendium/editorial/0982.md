# Erdős Problem #982 — Distinct distances from a convex polygon vertex

## The exact question

Let $P=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}\subset\mathbb R^2$ be the **distinct vertices of a convex polygon**, with $n\ge3$. From a vertex $v$, define its set of distances to the other vertices by

$$
D_P(v)=\{\|v-w\|:w\in P,\ w\ne v\}.
$$

Erdős asked whether every such polygon has **at least one vertex** satisfying

$$
|D_P(v)|\ge\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor.
$$

This statement remains **OPEN / FALSIFIABLE**, the wording of the maintained [Erdős problem #982](https://www.erdosproblems.com/982). A *falsifiable* label means a single finite strictly convex polygon with **every** vertex seeing fewer than the required number of distances would refute it. It does not mean that such a counterexample has been found.

The order of quantifiers matters: **for each polygon there must exist a vertex**. A claim that every vertex sees this many distances is stronger and is not the question. The distinct-distance count treats equal lengths as one value; it does not count the number of other vertices.

## A sharp example: a regular polygon

Place the $n$ vertices on a circle of radius $R>0$. From one fixed vertex, the squared distance to the vertex $j$ steps around the circle is

$$
d_j^2=4R^2\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi j}{n}\right),
\qquad 1\le j<n.
$$

The equality $d_j=d_{n-j}$ accounts for the reflection symmetry. For $1\le j\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, the values are strictly increasing, so there are **exactly**

$$
|D_P(v)|=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor
$$

different distances from **every** vertex. Consequently Erdős's proposed lower bound is **best possible** if true: it cannot be replaced by a universally larger integer. This sharpness example is not a proof of the proposed lower bound for arbitrary convex polygons.

For instance a regular octagon has four distinct chord lengths from each vertex, while a regular nonagon also has four. An exact certificate can use the index pairs $j$ and $n-j$ rather than floating-point comparison of sine values.

## An elementary finite verification model

To test a candidate counterexample, represent the vertices by exact rational or algebraic coordinates. For each index $i$, compute the **squared distances**

$$
\Delta_i=\{(x_i-x_j)^2+(y_i-y_j)^2:j\ne i\}.
$$

Because all distances are nonnegative, two distances are equal **if and only if** their squares are equal, so $|\Delta_i|=|D_P(v_i)|$ without any square-root numerical error. The exact acceptance test for a proposed disproof is

$$
\max_i|\Delta_i|<
\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor
$$

together with a separate, **exact** convexity and distinct-vertex certificate. Rational determinants of successive edges and nonintersection/hull checks can certify a strictly convex polygon when its vertices are cyclically ordered.

One instructive family is $v_i=(i,i^2)$ for $i=0,\ldots,n-1$. These points are in convex position: each point lies on the lower boundary of the convex hull of the sampled parabola, with distinct supporting tangents. An exact-integer check for $3\le n\le10$ found the maximum distance-count to be $n-1$, much **larger** than the conjectured threshold. These computations show that generic convex configurations are often not near-extremal; they do **not** imply the conjecture, and they do not test all convex polygons.

## Established lower bounds

Write the guaranteed maximum as

$$
g(n)=\inf_{\substack{P\subset\mathbb R^2\\
P\text{ in strictly convex position}\\|P|=n}}
\ \max_{v\in P}|D_P(v)|.
$$

The published historical progression is:

- Leo Moser, [Mo52], proved $g(n)\ge\lceil n/3\rceil$.
- Erdős and Peter Fishburn, [ErFi94], improved this to $g(n)\ge\lfloor n/3+1\rfloor$.
- Adrian Dumitrescu, [Du06b], proved $g(n)\ge\lceil(13n-6)/36\rceil$.
- Gabriel Nivasch, János Pach, Rom Pinchasi and Shira Zerbib, [NPPZ13], proved the asymptotic improvement

$$
g(n)\ge\left(\frac{13}{36}+\frac1{22701}\right)n-O(1).
$$

The hidden absolute constant in $O(1)$ is important. This last inequality cannot, without an explicit constant, be used as an exact finite guarantee for a specified small $n$.

The best quoted coefficient is just above $0.3611$, whereas Erdős conjectures essentially $0.5$. These bounds do **not** establish the main conjecture for all sufficiently large $n$. The source-audited research record identifies the conjecture as proved for all $n\le7$ and $n=9$, with **$n=8$ the smallest remaining open case**. The small-case statement rests on published lower bounds and their exact integer roundings, not on an exhaustive numerical search.

[Published-bound bibliography and case assessment](https://erdosproblems.ai/wiki/problems/distance_problems/E0982).

## A more recent, unreviewed partial claim

A July 25, 2026 forum submission attributed to Scott Duke Kominers, involving AI-assisted drafting, claims a refinement of the Nivasch–Pach–Pinchasi–Zerbib iteration:

$$
g(n)\ge\left(\frac{13}{36}+\frac3{5270}\right)n-O(1).
$$

This would modestly increase the known lower-bound coefficient, but it would remain far below $1/2$. The independently maintained claim inventory records **no full mathematical review** of the write-up and says that it settles no additional instance of Erdős's target. ## A tempting stronger statement is false

Erdős also suggested in 1946 that for each convex curve there might be a boundary point $p$ for which **no three other points are at the same distance** from $p$. Such a point would immediately create large distinct-distance sets, since each distance could occur at most twice. But this stronger continuous-curve conjecture is **false**: Imre Bárány and Edgardo Roldán-Pensado observe that the boundary of any acute triangle supplies a counterexample.

They prove a weaker existence theorem for an arbitrary planar convex body: at some boundary point, circles centered there meet the body's boundary in a bounded number of points, **with a constant dependent on the body**. Their conjecture that this number is bounded by one absolute constant is a *nearby* question, not a proved route to the exact finite-polygon half-bound.

This distinction matters: a counterexample to the stronger three-equidistant-vertices principle does **not** automatically refute Erdős #982.
