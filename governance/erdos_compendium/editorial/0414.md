## The question in plain language

Let $\tau(n)$ be the number of positive divisors of $n$, including $1$ and $n$. Define an arithmetic function by $h(n)=n+\tau(n)$. Erdős Problem 414 asks whether **all two positive starting integers** eventually reach the same number after repeatedly applying $h$, allowing a different number of applications for each start. If $h_0(n)=n$ and $h_{k+1}(n)=h(h_k(n))$, the problem is

$$
\forall\,m,n\geq1,\quad
\exists\,i,j\geq0:\ h_i(m)=h_j(n).
$$

This is an intersection problem about the eventual paths of a deterministic discrete dynamical system. It does not require $i=j$ or that a path approaches a fixed numerical limit. In fact, because $\tau(n)\geq1$, the paths increase without bound. For two paths to merge, they must hit exactly the same integer; being close or having similar growth does not suffice.

The proposal is attributed to Christopher Spiro in the original discussion. It resembles the preceding van Wijngaarden divisor-sum question, Erdős #412, but counts divisors instead of adding them. These are distinct operations, and evidence for one conjecture is not evidence for the other.

## A small example

Starting at $2$, exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
2\longmapsto4\longmapsto7\longmapsto9
\longmapsto12\longmapsto18\longmapsto24.
$$

The first step is $2+\tau(2)=2+2=4$. At $4$, the divisors are $1,2,4$, so $4+\tau(4)=7$. At $7$, $\tau(7)=2$, giving $9$. At $9$, the divisors are $1,3,9$, so $9+\tau(9)=12$. At $12$, there are six divisors, yielding $18$.

Starting at $3$, we find $3,5,7,9,12,18,24,\ldots$. The two paths therefore meet at $7$, reached in two steps from each seed. The path starting at $5$ reaches $7$ in one step. Another pair also meets: starting at $4$ gives $4,7,9,12,18$, whereas starting at $6$ gives $6,10,14,18$. Thus the paths from $4$ and $6$ meet at $18$, after four steps and three steps respectively. These are exact finite **witnesses of intersection**, not proofs about every possible pair.

All values were calculated by enumerating divisor pairs up to the square root of the current integer, counting each distinct divisor once. A useful independent check is the factorization formula

$$
\tau(p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r})
=(a_1+1)\cdots(a_r+1).
$$

For example, $12=2^2\cdot3$ gives $(2+1)(1+1)=6$ divisors and hence $h(12)=18$. This formula provides a second verification route for every step in a finite table.

## What is known

The original printed source is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **page 82**. The [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf) attributes this particular question to Spiro and expresses it as the conjecture that there is essentially only one infinite iteration sequence. The authors say an affirmative answer seemed certain to them. Their confidence should not be mistaken for a theorem: the printed passage does not present a proof.

The maintained [Problem 414 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/414) continues to identify it as **OPEN** and specifically connects it to Problems #412 and #413. The page also points to [OEIS A064491](https://oeis.org/A064491), a sequence related to repeated applications to the smallest starting value. Such an observed sequence describes one orbit; it is not a universal intersection theorem.

One rigorously elementary observation is that $h(n)\geq n+1$. This yields strict increase and also proves that no finite cycle or fixed point can occur among positive integers. The stronger common-orbit assertion does not follow: two strictly increasing integer sequences need not intersect at all. In particular, knowing that $h$ is increasing as a map would not by itself settle this question, and $\tau$ is not a monotone function of $n$ anyway.

The number of divisors has an irregular dependence on prime factorization. For a prime $p$, $h(p)=p+2$, whereas for $p^2$, $h(p^2)=p^2+3$. These exact observations help explain why a simple constant-step or monotonic-increment argument will not provide a general meeting theorem.
