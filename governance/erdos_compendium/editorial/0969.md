## The question in plain language

An integer \(m\ge1\) is **squarefree** if no square of a prime divides \(m\). Equivalently, if \(m=\prod p^{e_p}\), every exponent \(e_p\) is \(0\) or \(1\). By convention \(1\) is squarefree. Let
\[
Q(x)=\#\{1\le m\le x:m\text{ is squarefree}\},
\qquad x\ge1.
\]
The natural density of the squarefree integers is \(6/\pi^2\), so
\[
Q(x)=\frac6{\pi^2}x+E(x).
\]
**Erdős Problem #969 asks for the true order of magnitude of the error \(E(x)\).** The question remains **OPEN** even assuming the Riemann hypothesis. The expected critical power is \(x^{1/4}\), interpreted carefully in upper and lower estimates rather than as an exact two-sided bound valid for every \(x\). [Maintained statement](https://www.erdosproblems.com/969), sources [Er65b] and [Er81h, p.176].

## A small exact example

Among the integers from \(1\) through \(10\), the squarefree ones are
\[
1,2,3,5,6,7,10,
\]
while \(4\), \(8\) and \(9\) fail because they are divisible by \(2^2\) or \(3^2\). Hence
\[
Q(10)=7,\qquad E(10)=7-\frac{60}{\pi^2}.
\]
This is an **exact expression**; numerical approximations to \(\pi\) are not part of the squarefree-count certificate. The value \(Q(x)\) is an integer-valued step function; the linear main term is continuous and nonintegral for almost every \(x\). Thus a statement such as \(E(x)\ll x^{1/4}\) would mean a uniform magnitude bound, not that the error is exactly of the same sign or a monotone function.

## An exact identity using Möbius inversion

Let \(\mu\) denote the Möbius function: \(\mu(n)=0\) if \(n\) has a squared prime factor and \(\mu(n)=(-1)^r\) if \(n\) is the product of \(r\) distinct primes. For every positive integer \(m\),
\[
\mu(m)^2
 =\sum_{d^2\mid m}\mu(d).
\]
This identity is checked prime-power by prime-power: when \(m\) is squarefree, only \(d=1\) contributes; when \(m\) contains a nontrivial squared prime, the signed contributions cancel.

Summing over \(m\le x\) and exchanging the **finite** sums gives
\[
\boxed{
Q(x)=\sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\mu(d)
                  \left\lfloor\frac{x}{d^2}\right\rfloor .
}
\]
This is a practical exact finite algorithm and the source of the elementary error estimate. It automatically includes \(1\) as a squarefree number. The floor is **inside** the Möbius sum; dropping it does not preserve the exact formula.

## The elementary \(O(\sqrt x)\) upper bound

Use
\[
\frac1{\zeta(2)}
=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}
=\frac6{\pi^2}.
\]
Subtracting the main term from the exact identity yields
\[
E(x)=
\sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\mu(d)
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{x}{d^2}\right\rfloor-\frac{x}{d^2}\right)
-x\sum_{d>\sqrt x}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}.
\]
The first sum has at most \(\sqrt x\) terms of absolute value less than one, and the tail is bounded by
\[
x\sum_{d>\sqrt x}\frac1{d^2}=O(\sqrt x).
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{E(x)=O(\sqrt x).}
\]
This proof is unconditional, elementary and **complete** at the stated level. It uses no analytic continuation, conjecture about primes, or unproved cancellation. Establishing an exponent *strictly below* \(1/2\), let alone close to \(1/4\), demands substantial additional cancellation.

## Stronger known estimates and lower bounds

The prime number theorem implies \(E(x)=o(x^{1/2})\). Arnold Walfisz [Wa63] strengthened the unconditional bound to the form
\[
E(x)=O\!\left(
x^{1/2}\exp\left[-c\,
\frac{(\log x)^{3/5}}{(\log\log x)^{1/5}}\right]\right)
\]
for some constant \(c>0\) and sufficiently large \(x\). This **saves a stretched-exponential factor** over the elementary \(O(\sqrt x)\), but it is still \(x^{1/2-o(1)}\), **not** \(O(x^{1/2-\delta})\) for any fixed \(\delta>0\). The formula is the Walfisz estimate given in standard analytic-number-theory references; exact original theorem numbering and parameter ranges require a separate primary-text audit.

Evelyn and Linfoot [EvLi31] proved a lower-bound fluctuation on the scale \(x^{1/4}\):
\[
E(x)=\Omega(x^{1/4}).
\]
Here the big-\(\Omega\) assertion means **at arbitrarily large arguments the absolute error is at least a constant times \(x^{1/4}\)** (with any one-sided strengthening to be checked separately). It is **not** a lower bound \(|E(x)|\ge c x^{1/4}\) for all large \(x\). The catalogue writes \(E(x)\gg x^{1/4}\), which is ambiguous in isolation; oscillation terminology is the mathematically safer paraphrase.

Assuming the **Riemann hypothesis**, H.-Q. Liu [Li16] proved
\[
E(x)\ll_\varepsilon x^{11/35+\varepsilon}
\qquad\text{for every fixed }\varepsilon>0,
\]
equivalently \(x^{11/35+o(1)}\) in the catalogue's shorthand. Since \(11/35\approx0.3142857>1/4\), this does **not** attain the conjectured critical exponent even assuming RH. This estimate is **conditional**, not a present unconditional fixed-power improvement over Walfisz. The published paper is H.-Q. Liu, [*On the distribution of squarefree numbers*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2015.07.013), *Journal of Number Theory* **159** (2016), pp.202–222.

An often-stated conjectural formulation is
\[
E(x)=O_\varepsilon(x^{1/4+\varepsilon})
\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.
\]
This is consistent with the Evelyn–Linfoot \(\Omega(x^{1/4})\) scale. A **sharper unqualified** \(E(x)=O(x^{1/4})\) would imply the Riemann hypothesis, as noted by the maintained problem catalogue, but **RH itself currently gives only the weaker Liu exponent**. Neither implication should be reversed.

## Why zeros of the zeta function enter

The squarefree indicator has the Dirichlet generating series
\[
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\mu(n)^2}{n^s}
=\prod_p(1+p^{-s})
=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)},
\qquad\Re(s)>1.
\]
The pole at \(s=1\) gives the linear main term \(x/\zeta(2)\). Zeros of \(\zeta(2s)\) and the analytic behavior of the ratio govern possible oscillations and errors beyond this main term. But knowing all nontrivial zeros of \(\zeta\) have real part \(1/2\) would only locate potential singularities near \(\Re(s)=1/4\); it does **not** by itself give the desired uniform summatory bound without sufficiently sharp control of residues, horizontal contour integrals and cancellation among zero contributions.

A tempting but insufficient proof approach is to replace the floor error in the exact Möbius formula by random independent signs. The Möbius values and fractional parts \(\{x/d^2\}\) have arithmetic correlations, and a heuristic cancellation of \(\sqrt x\) terms does not amount to an unconditional \(x^{1/4}\)-scale estimate.
