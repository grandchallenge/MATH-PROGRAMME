## The question in plain language

The sum-of-divisors function $\sigma(n)$ adds every positive divisor of an integer $n$, including $1$ and $n$ themselves. For example, $\sigma(6)=1+2+3+6=12$. One can repeat this operation: first calculate $\sigma(n)$, then $\sigma(\sigma(n))$, and so on. Erdős Problem 412 asks whether **every two starting integers at least 2 eventually reach a common value**, possibly after different numbers of steps. With $\sigma_0(n)=n$ and $\sigma_{r+1}(n)=\sigma(\sigma_r(n))$, the question is whether for all $m,n\geq2$ there are nonnegative integers $i,j$ such that

$$
\sigma_i(m)=\sigma_j(n).
$$

The two indices need not agree. Once a common value occurs, all subsequent values agree after an appropriate shift. Conversely, inspecting two orbits for only finitely many steps cannot prove that they never meet. An affirmative answer would be a global coalescence statement, not the assertion that individual sequences converge to finite limits. Van Wijngaarden suggested the question to Erdős in the 1950s. The original GCL audit is [issue #1682](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1682).

## A small example

Direct integer arithmetic starting at $2$ gives

$$
2\longmapsto3\longmapsto4\longmapsto7\longmapsto8
\longmapsto15\longmapsto24\longmapsto60.
$$

For instance, $\sigma(4)=1+2+4=7$, $\sigma(8)=1+2+4+8=15$, and $\sigma(15)=1+3+5+15=24$. Starting at $3$ gives $3,4,7,8,15,\ldots$, so the first two orbits meet at $3$: one iteration from $2$, zero iterations from $3$. Starting from $5$ instead gives

$$
5\longmapsto6\longmapsto12\longmapsto28\longmapsto56
\longmapsto120\longmapsto360.
$$

For these last two starting points, $2$ and $5$, no intersection was found in the first nine recorded values of each orbit. That is only an observation about a declared finite computation, not a counterexample to Problem 412. The paths might merge at a much greater height.

The calculation is independently reproducible using integer divisor pairs: for every integer $d$ with $1\leq d\leq\sqrt{n}$ that divides $n$, add both $d$ and $n/d$, counting a square-root divisor only once. Applying this rule successively produces the listed values. It also guards against a common mistake: summing only *proper* divisors computes $\sigma(n)-n$, a different dynamical system. All arithmetic in these examples is exact and involves no numerical approximation.

## What is known

The original printed discussion occurs in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **printed page 81** of the [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). It explicitly credits van Wijngaarden and reports Selfridge's numerical evidence suggesting a negative answer. That statement is historical testimony about calculations, **not** a proved counterexample. The authors themselves do not resolve the question there.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #412 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/412), checked on 13 October 2026, still labels this formulation **OPEN**. It also references the nearby iteration problems #413 and #414. Those use different arithmetic maps, so neither a finite example nor a theorem about them automatically transfers to iterated divisor sums. This bounded source audit found no primary publication resolving the general universal intersection assertion.

There is an easy independent observation. For every $n>1$, both $1$ and $n$ divide $n$, giving $\sigma(n)\geq n+1$. Hence every individual orbit strictly increases and eventually exceeds any fixed threshold. But this fact is not an answer: two increasing sequences can diverge without sharing any value, and increase alone does not establish coalescence either. The problem is about intersections across *all* starting points.

## What remains

A useful bounded task is an exact audit of orbit intersections within a fixed sample: enumerate $\sigma$ orbits starting from $2$ through $50$, stop after ten steps each, and record the first verified common value for every pair that meets. For pairs without an observed common value, report only that none was seen **within the stated bound**. The successor must include the two step indices for each positive collision and replay them from scratch.

Independently test the divisor enumerator against the prime-power identity

$$
\sigma(p^a)=1+p+\cdots+p^a
=\frac{p^{a+1}-1}{p-1}.
$$

The second task is to compare the exact historical quantifiers with the specialized binder and index conventions in the pinned Lean statement. Completion of these two bounded checks produces a semantic and computational fixture, not a decision about the conjecture. Falsification consists of an incorrect recorded collision, a divisor-sum mismatch, or a changed quantifier. Stop at those checks rather than claiming to resolve the infinite question from finite data.
