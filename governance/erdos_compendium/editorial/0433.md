## The question in plain language

The **Frobenius coin problem** asks which amounts of money can be formed using a specified collection of coin denominations, with unlimited repetitions allowed. If the denominations have greatest common divisor $1$, then every sufficiently large integer can be expressed as a nonnegative integer combination of them, but some smaller integers may remain unreachable. The greatest unreachable integer is called their **Frobenius number**.

Let $G(A)$ be this largest missing integer for a finite set of denominations $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $\gcd(A)=1$. Erdős Problem 433 considers the largest possible Frobenius number among all such sets of exactly $k$ distinct denominations:

$$
g(k,n)=\max_{\substack{A\subseteq[1,n]\\|A|=k,\ \gcd(A)=1}}G(A).
$$

The question asked whether, for **fixed** $k\geq2$ and $n\to\infty$,

$$
g(k,n)\sim\frac{n^2}{k-1}.
$$

The fixed-$k$ qualification matters. An asymptotic formula proved for each fixed $k$ does not automatically hold uniformly when $k$ grows with $n$.

For a set containing denomination $1$, every positive integer is representable and the set of missing nonnegative values is empty. Therefore the definition needs an explicit convention, such as $G(A)=-1$, in that degenerate case. The extremal problem's large maximizers are not affected by the convention.

## A small example

Take the denominations $A=\{8,9,10\}$. The integer $31$ cannot be formed. Any sum of at most three coins is at most $3\cdot10=30$, whereas a sum of four or more coins is at least $4\cdot8=32$. Thus $31$ is absent.

On the other hand, every integer from $32$ through $39$ can be formed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
32&=4\cdot8, &33&=3\cdot8+9,\\
34&=2\cdot8+2\cdot9, &35&=8+3\cdot9,\\
36&=4\cdot9, &37&=8+9+10+10,\\
38&=8+3\cdot10, &39&=9+3\cdot10.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the coin $8$ is available, adding any further multiple of $8$ extends these eight consecutive representations to every integer greater than $39$. Hence **$G(\{8,9,10\})=31$**, proved exactly without relying on a finite search horizon.

This example exposes a real defect in the formula printed on the maintained Erdős Problems page. Its displayed upper bound reads

$$
g(k,n)\leq
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k-1}\right\rfloor-1\right)n-1.
$$

At $k=3,n=10$, it would give $g(3,10)\leq29$. But the explicit set above shows $g(3,10)\geq31$, so **that printed inequality is false**. The page's own discussion records the correction: the upper bound should use a **ceiling**, not a floor. Then the right-hand side is $39$, consistent with the example. The typo is in the maintained transcription, not a counterexample to the published theorem of Dixmier.

An independent exhaustive exact-integer search over all three-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ with gcd $1$ finds $g(3,10)=31$, attained by $\{8,9,10\}$ and $\{5,9,10\}$.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham studied this extremal Frobenius problem in [*On a linear diophantine problem of Frobenius*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/search_bib/ErGr72), *Acta Arithmetica* (1972), pp. 399–408, and discussed it again in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), in the [historical monograph](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). They proved an upper bound smaller than $2n^2/k$ and constructed examples with lower bounds of the form $n^2/(k-1)-5n$.

Jacques Dixmier proved the conjectured leading asymptotic in [*Proof of a conjecture by Erdős and Graham concerning the problem of Frobenius*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-314X(90)90150-P), *Journal of Number Theory* 34(2) (1990), pp. 198–209. The result establishes the proposed growth for each fixed $k$ and provides more precise extremal inequalities for $2\leq k<n$. Dixmier also identified exact extremal values when $k-1$ divides $n$, $n-1$ or $n-2$.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #433](https://www.erdosproblems.com/433) entry says **PROVED (LEAN)**. Its remarks reproduce the historical bounds and Dixmier's conclusion, but the displayed sharp upper inequality contains the floor/ceiling transcription error demonstrated above. A November 2025 discussion comment by Stijn Cambie explicitly identifies this mistake and recommends the ceiling correction. That is stronger evidence of a concrete source defect than a merely stylistic editorial preference.

A related question, [Erdős #434](https://www.erdosproblems.com/434), concerns another extremal Frobenius formulation. Its claims and formalization status must be reviewed separately; a shared source paper does not make the problem statements interchangeable.

## What remains

The leading-order mathematical question is already resolved by Dixmier. The nearest useful task is therefore to correct the **maintained mathematical transcription** from the actual 1990 journal theorem. Read the printed bound and its floor and ceiling symbols directly, then compare it with the known discussion correction and the small certificate above. Distinguish the lower and upper inequalities and the fixed-$k$ asymptotic from a hypothetical uniform-in-$k$ statement.

A bounded computational fixture should evaluate $g(k,n)$ for $2\leq k\leq5$ and $n\leq14$ using independent algorithms: exhaustive subsets with gcd checks, and a numerical-semigroup residue shortest-path method for each $G(A)$. The task succeeds if both algorithms agree, the regression at $g(3,10)=31$ holds, and all printed inequalities match the primary source. It fails if the false floor bound survives, or if a set containing $1$ is assigned a nonexistent largest missing positive integer without a stated convention. Stop after source repair and small exact enumeration. Any claim of formal proof replay needs separate compiler evidence.
