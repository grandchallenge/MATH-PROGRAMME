## The question in plain language

[Erdős Problem #9](https://www.erdosproblems.com/9) concerns odd integers that **cannot** be expressed as a prime plus two powers of two. More precisely, let

$$
A=\{n\in\mathbb N:n\text{ is odd and }n\ne p+2^k+2^\ell
\text{ for all primes }p\text{ and }k,\ell\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

The exponents may be zero or equal. The question is whether

$$
\limsup_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,X])}{X}>0.
$$

The denominator counts **all** integers, not just odd ones. Positive *upper natural density* means that some fixed positive proportion of integers are exceptions along arbitrarily large cutoffs. It is stronger than saying the set is infinite. The question remains open in the October 2026 source record.

## A small example

Exponent zero matters:

$$
5=3+2^0+2^0,\quad
11=2+2^0+2^3,\quad
17=13+2^1+2^1.
$$

The numbers 1 and 3 are exceptions because the smallest possible sum is $2+1+1=4$. The [GCL exact arithmetic audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1278#issuecomment-6103585999) enumerated the odd integers through 200,000 and found exactly these two exceptions. The observed exceptional fraction among all 200,000 positive integers is $2/200000=10^{-5}$. This is a **finite sample**, not an asymptotic density estimate.

There are three parity possibilities for a representation of an odd $n$: (1) an odd prime $p=n-2$ with $k=\ell=0$; (2) an odd prime and both exponents positive; or (3) $p=2$, exactly one zero exponent, giving $n=3+2^j$ with $j\ge1$. Ignoring zero exponents would falsely classify 5 as exceptional.

## What is known

Roger Crocker's *On the sum of a prime and of two powers of two*, *Pacific Journal of Mathematics* **36** (1971), 103–107, proves an infinitude statement with **positive** exponents. It cannot be transferred without qualification to the present definition that permits zero exponents.

Hao Pan, *On the integers not of the form $p+2^a+2^b$*, *Acta Arithmetica* **148** (2011), 55–61, Theorem 1.1 (printed p. 56), gives a stronger quantitative result. His proof (printed p. 59) permits $0\le a\le b$ and yields, for some $C>0$,

$$
\#(A\cap[1,X])\gg
X\exp\left(-C\,\frac{\log X\,\log_4 X}{\log_3 X}\right),
$$

where $\log_j$ denotes an iterated logarithm. The source's bound concerns a subset excluding prime-power summands, hence also lower-bounds $A$. In particular the exception count grows at least as fast as $X^{1-\varepsilon}$ (up to an epsilon-dependent multiplicative constant) for each fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and sufficiently large $X$.

This does **not** prove a positive density: the lower-bound expression divided by $X$ tends to zero. Nor does the small finite sample refute Pan's theorem.

## GCL's current state

[GCL source audit #1278](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1278#issuecomment-6103585999) records the exact zero-inclusive pinned formal statement, checks its parity decomposition, and independently verifies 99,998 finite representation witnesses for odd integers from 5 to 199,999. The source packet provides the canonical witness digest and primary-source locations. These checks are bounded arithmetic, **not** proof certification of an asymptotic claim.

## What remains

To establish positive upper density one must find $c>0$ and an unbounded sequence $X_j$ with $\#(A\cap[1,X_j])\ge cX_j$. To refute it one must show the upper density is zero. Neither Pan's nearly linear lower bound nor bounded enumeration decides the alternatives.
