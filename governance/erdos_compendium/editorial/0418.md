## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, Euler's totient function $\varphi(n)$ counts integers from $1$ to $n$ that are coprime to $n$. The difference $n-\varphi(n)$ therefore counts the integers in that range that are *not* coprime to $n$. A positive integer $m$ is called a **cototient** if $m=n-\varphi(n)$ for at least one positive integer $n$. If no such $n$ exists, $m$ is a **noncototient**.

Erdős Problem 418 asks whether infinitely many positive integers are noncototients. This is a qualitative existence question: it does not require a positive fraction of all integers to be noncototients, or a formula for every noncototient. In the historical formulation it was asked by Erdős and Sierpiński. The distinction between infinitely many and positive density is crucial because the latter remains a separate, more demanding question.

The noncototient problem must not be confused with the *nonaliquot* problem involving $\sigma(n)-n$, where $\sigma$ denotes the sum of positive divisors. That related function has its own theorems and density estimates. Here the map is exactly $n-\varphi(n)$.

## A small example

The small integer $10$ is a noncototient. This can be checked **exhaustively** using a finite calculation, provided the input range is justified rather than chosen arbitrarily. If $n$ is prime, $\varphi(n)=n-1$ and $n-\varphi(n)=1$, so no prime produces $10$. If $n>1$ is composite, let $p$ be its smallest prime divisor. Since $p\leq\sqrt{n}$ and all multiples of $p$ are excluded by the totient, we have

$$
n-\varphi(n)\geq\frac{n}{p}\geq\sqrt{n}.
$$

Thus if $n-\varphi(n)=10$, then necessarily $n\leq100$. A complete exact-integer check of $n=1,\ldots,100$ finds no solution. The first small noncototients under $30$ identified by this same justified scan include $10$, $26$, and $29$. Since any witness for a specified $m$ is either prime or at most $m^2$, these are genuine finite certificates after the range is fully checked, not merely unexplained numerical searches.

For comparison, $n=12$ satisfies $\varphi(12)=4$, so $12-\varphi(12)=8$; and $n=25$ satisfies $\varphi(25)=20$, so its cototient value is $5$. The computation of $\varphi$ can be independently verified by listing coprime residues or by the exact multiplicative formula

$$
\varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$

Showing a single number such as $10$ is missing does not establish that infinitely many distinct numbers are missing.

## What is known

The original statement occurs in Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **printed page 83** of the [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). Their discussion credits Erdős and Sierpiński, distinguishes odd from even target integers, and does not resolve the infinitude question. The maintained [Erdős Problems #418 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/418) also cites an earlier Erdős source from 1974 and records the later solution.

The exact affirmative theorem was published by J. Browkin and A. Schinzel, *On integers not of the form $n-\varphi(n)$*, **Colloquium Mathematicum 68 (1995), 55–58**, DOI [10.4064/cm-68-1-55-58](https://doi.org/10.4064/cm-68-1-55-58). The [publisher's abstract](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/colloquium-mathematicum/all/68/1/108751/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/colloquium-mathematicum/all/68) expressly states the stronger constructive conclusion: for every integer $k\geq1$, the integer

$$
2^k\cdot509203
$$

is a noncototient. These are pairwise distinct, so the originally posed infinitude question has a positive answer. This article attributes that result to the published paper, rather than pretending that a short small-integer check reproduces its proof.

The density of noncototients remains a different question. The maintained page says positive density is open. Its discussion distinguishes noncototients from nonaliquots, whose positive-density results come from the *sum-of-divisors* map and do not imply the corresponding cototient result.

## What remains

For the original infinitude question, the substantive mathematical resolution is already available in the Browkin–Schinzel publication. The useful bounded next task is therefore **source and formal concordance**, not the invention of a different proof. Read the theorem and proof in the four-page 1995 paper and compare its exact domain, the exponent restriction $k\geq1$, and the definition of a noncototient with both the maintained problem and the pinned Lean statements.

As an executable warm-up, implement the justified finite decision test for a single target $m$. First prove the composite bound $n\leq m^2$ above; then check every candidate $n$ up to the bound using exact totients. Reproduce $m=10$ and one ordinary cototient such as $m=8$. The acceptance condition is a complete finite certificate with no unchecked $n$, followed by a documented theorem-to-formal-statement comparison. Falsification includes a wrong prime/composite bound, a hidden $n>m^2$ witness, or treating a Lean placeholder as a proof. Stop after this verification; the positive-density question remains research rather than editorial reproduction.
