## The question in plain language

A positive integer is called powerful when every prime that divides it occurs at least twice in its prime factorisation. Thus $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ is powerful, while $10=2\cdot5$ is not. Erdős Problem 364 asks whether there is a positive integer $n$ for which all three numbers $n$, $n+1$, and $n+2$ are powerful. The expected answer is no. This is the Erdős–Mollin–Walsh consecutive-triple question.

The question is easy to state but should not be confused with the nearby question about pairs. Consecutive powerful pairs do exist. The smallest is $8,9$: the first number is $2^3$ and the second is $3^2$. The existence of infinitely many pairs follows from Pell-type equations, as Erdős records. The extra requirement that a third neighbour also be powerful is the difficult boundary.

The problem is about existence, not about finding a typical gap or proving that powerful numbers are rare. A proposed proof must rule out every positive integer, not merely all integers below a large computational cutoff.

## A small example

Here is a reproducible finite check. For each integer from $1$ through $1000$, factor by trial division and declare the integer powerful exactly when every recorded exponent is at least two. The exact output was: 54 powerful numbers; consecutive pair starts $8,288,675$; no triple starts; and no quadruple starts. The factorizations used as spot checks were $8=2^3$, $9=3^2$, $10=2\cdot5$, $48=2^4\cdot3$, $49=7^2$, and $50=2\cdot5^2$.

```text
def powerful(n):
    if n == 1: return True
    for p, e in prime_factorisation(n):
        if e < 2: return False
    return True

P = [n for n in range(1, 1001) if powerful(n)]
T = [n for n in range(1, 999)
     if all(powerful(n+i) for i in range(3))]
print(len(P), [n for n in range(1, 1000)
               if powerful(n) and powerful(n+1)][:3], T)
```

With the stated trial-division factoriser, this prints `54 [8, 288, 675] []`. This is an exact finite calculation, not a probabilistic search, and it is useful for catching definition or indexing mistakes. It does not establish the conjecture: a counterexample, if one exists, could be larger than 1000. It also does not verify the catalogue's much larger computational bound.

A simple universal observation does settle a neighbouring statement. Among four consecutive integers, one is $2$ modulo $4$. Such an integer has exactly one factor of $2$ and therefore is not powerful. Hence four consecutive powerful integers are impossible. That modular obstruction leaves the three-term case open.

## What is known

The primary source read for this audit is P. Erdős, “Problems and results on number theoretic properties of consecutive integers and related questions,” *Proceedings of the Fifth Manitoba Conference on Numerical Mathematics* (1976), pp. 25–44. On printed p. 31, Erdős writes the sequence of r-powerful numbers as $u_k^{(r)}$, notes that $x^2-8y^2=1$ supplies infinitely many consecutive powerful pairs, and says it is very probable that the distance between the third-powerful neighbours has no solutions. For the 2-powerful sequence he expects a positive-power gap of the form $u_{k+2}^{(2)}-u_k^{(2)}>(u_k^{(2)})^c$ for some $c>0$, but explicitly says that he cannot even prove that distance $3$ occurs only finitely often. This is primary evidence for the historical conjectural boundary, not a proof of the no-triple assertion.

The maintained entry currently states the direct question and labels it “verifiable” and open. It reports that the abc conjecture implies only finitely many such triples, not that there are none. It also records two restricted nonexistence results for triples centred at a cube, one with neighbouring shapes $p^3x^2$ and one with shapes $p^2x^3$, and reports an OEIS-based search bound below approximately $7.38\times10^{28}$. Those are narrower results or finite evidence; none is the universal theorem.

The live discussion contains six comments and one proof claim. The proof claim says that two mixed cube-centred shapes are excluded, while expressly acknowledging that other shapes remain and that Problem 364 is not settled. It is therefore an unverified external report of a partial result, not an accepted proof. The live page was last edited 13 April 2026, so its state is newer than the frozen catalogue packet.

## What remains

The clean next step is a source-and-interface package, not an attempt to settle the conjecture. First, capture the positive-integer statement and the definition of powerfulness in a notation table. Second, compare each quantifier in the pinned Lean theorem with the primary and maintained statements, including the treatment of zero, indexing of `Nat.nth`, and whether the gap assertion is intended for all sufficiently large indices or all indices. Third, attach a bounded exact-arithmetic certificate for a declared bound, with factorisation witnesses for every rejected candidate and a reproducible generator-completeness argument.

The result should separate four layers: published mathematics, unverified literature or discussion reports, finite computation, and the formal theorem source with `sorry`. A successful package may show that the formal statement is a faithful interface and that a finite range is clear. It must not turn either fact into a universal proof. If the mixed cube-centred claims are reused, their exact hypotheses and source versions must be recorded rather than generalised to arbitrary powerful neighbours.
