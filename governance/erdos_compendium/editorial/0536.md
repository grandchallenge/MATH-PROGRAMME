**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #536 is **OPEN**. It asks whether every sufficiently large subset of the integers with positive relative density contains three **distinct** members whose three pairwise least common multiples are equal. This is a multiplicative analogue of finding certain unavoidable patterns in dense sets. Its stronger companion problem, #537, was disproved, but that does not answer #536.

## The question in plain language

The least common multiple of two positive integers $a,b$, written $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$, is the smallest positive multiple of both.

Consider three **different** integers $a,b,c$. Call them an **LCM triangle** if

$$
\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=
\operatorname{lcm}(a,c)=
\operatorname{lcm}(b,c).
$$

Let $f(N)$ be the largest possible number of integers in a subset $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ containing **no** LCM triangle. Erdős asks whether

$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}N=0.}
$$

Equivalently, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, must every sufficiently large set $A\subseteq[1,N]$ with $|A|\ge\varepsilon N$ contain three distinct elements with all pairwise LCMs equal?

The three elements must be distinct. If repeated elements were allowed, trivial repetitions would change the problem drastically, and allowing exactly two equal elements would introduce the quite different theory of **primitive sets**. This is not a technicality: the original 1964 and 1973 papers specify three elements drawn from a strictly increasing sequence.

## A small example

The integers $6,10,15$ form an LCM triangle:

$$
\operatorname{lcm}(6,10)=
\operatorname{lcm}(6,15)=
\operatorname{lcm}(10,15)=30.
$$

No one of these integers is a multiple of either other. All three pairwise combinations nevertheless recover the same product $30$.

Compare $6,10,14$. Their pairwise least common multiples are $30$, $42$ and $70$, so this triple is **allowed**. This example is especially useful because the same triple is *forbidden* in Erdős #535, where the condition is equal pairwise **greatest common divisors** rather than equal least common multiples.

There is an exact prime-by-prime test. For a prime $p$, let $v_p(a)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in $a$. Since

$$
v_p(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\max(v_p(a),v_p(b)),
$$

the three pairwise LCMs coincide precisely when, for **every prime** $p$, the largest of the three valuations

$$
v_p(a),\quad v_p(b),\quad v_p(c)
$$

occurs **at least twice**. If the largest valuation appears only once, the LCM omitting that number has a smaller power of $p$.

This equivalence gives an exact integer-arithmetic predicate for short examples and finite tests. It does not imply that dense sets must contain such a triple.

## What is known

### Erdős's original formulation

In *On a Problem in Elementary Number Theory and a Combinatorial Problem*, *Mathematics of Computation* **18** (1964), 644–646, [original scanned paper](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1964-10.pdf), Erdős asks on printed p.646 whether any fixed positive proportion of the integers up to $N$ forces three elements with equal pairwise LCM. The complete primary three-page paper was retrieved, and the final page examined directly.

Erdős repeats the question in *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), Chapter 12, [printed p.124](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=8). The original page was also read. He contrasts the three-element question with a **four-element** version: a linear-size set may avoid four integers with all six pairwise LCMs equal. This does **not** resolve the three-element problem.

The same 1973 passage asks a stronger question about three representations of one number as $pa$, with $p$ prime and $a$ drawn from a dense set. Imre Ruzsa disproved that stronger question (#537). A counterexample to a **stronger** property does not show that the weaker equal-LCM configuration is absent. It would be incorrect to label #536 DISPROVED because of Ruzsa's construction.

### Known upper and lower bounds

Abbott and Gardner, in a 1967 investigation cited by the [maintained #536 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/536), established a lower construction of the form

$$
f(N)\ge(1-o(1))\frac{N\log\log N}{\log N}.
$$

This gives relatively large sets with **no** LCM triangle, yet their proportion of $[1,N]$ still tends to zero. It therefore does not contradict the conjecture.

A discussion by Desmond Weisenberg, posted December 21, 2025, supplies further improvements recorded in the maintained source, including an upper bound

$$
\boxed{
f(N)\le\left(\frac{221}{225}+o(1)\right)N
}
$$

and a lower bound involving a factor $(\log\log N)^{\omega(N)}$, for some function $\omega(N)\to\infty$, over $N/\log N$. These are **attributed community mathematical arguments**, not independently reconstructed or confirmed as peer-reviewed published theorems in this editorial tranche.

Even the stated upper bound still permits a positive fraction of all integers to avoid LCM triangles. The desired conclusion $f(N)=o(N)$ would require driving that proportion all the way to zero.

### A reusable prime-bin construction

Divide a collection of distinct primes into $k$ disjoint **bins**. Choose numbers having exactly **one prime factor from each bin**, each with exponent one.

Suppose three such numbers had equal pairwise LCMs. Consider one particular bin. The three selected primes from that bin must all be identical: if any selected prime occurred only once, it would divide just two of the pairwise LCMs, not the third. This is true in every bin. Hence the three numbers would be identical, contradicting distinctness.

This is a valid finite family construction. Obtaining **positive density in the integers** from a suitable sequence of such constructions requires additional number-theoretic counting and is not supplied by the elementary bin argument.

## What remains

The mathematical research problem is to prove $f(N)=o(N)$ or exhibit a genuine positive-density counterexample. A promising bounded subproblem is to characterize LCM triangles within specified prime-divisibility layers and derive an inequality on permissible overlap patterns. Such a lemma would require an explicit density-sensitive argument to extend beyond finite enumeration.

The community-reported quantitative bounds merit separate full source review before being used as independently certified mathematical results. No new Solve campaign is launched by this editorial work.
