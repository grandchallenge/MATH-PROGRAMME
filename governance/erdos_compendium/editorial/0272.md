## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $N$, write $[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. We seek the largest size $t(N)$ of a family of distinct subsets $A_1,\ldots,A_{t(N)}$ of $[N]$ such that every two different members meet in a nonempty arithmetic progression:

$$
t(N)=\max\left\{t:\ \exists\text{ distinct }A_1,\ldots,A_t\subseteq[N],\ \forall i\ne j,\ A_i\cap A_j\text{ is a nonempty arithmetic progression}\right\}.
$$

Here a one- or two-element set counts as an arithmetic progression. A larger one has the form $\{a,a+d,\ldots,a+(\ell-1)d\}$ with $d\geq1$. The word “nonempty” is essential: the empty intersection is forbidden, but there is no requirement that all intersections have the same length or common difference. The quantifier $\forall i\ne j$ ranges over pairs of members, and each pair may have its own progression.

The problem is to determine $t(N)$, ideally by an exact formula valid for every $N$. The original Erdős–Graham source, p.20, is less explicit about modern distinct-family conventions; this article uses the normalization stated explicitly in Szabó’s Definition 1 and represented by the pinned formal model. The maintained [Erdős entry for #272](https://www.erdosproblems.com/272) records the same question and currently labels it open.

## A small example

An exact finite witness can be checked at $N=7$. Take the common element $m=4$. Include $\{4\}$ and all six pairs $\{4,x\}$ with $x\in[7]\setminus\{4\}$. Include every three-element set containing $4$ except

$$
\{2,4,5\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{3,4,6\},
$$

and add the three arithmetic progressions

$$
\{2,3,4,5,6\},\qquad \{2,3,4,5\},\qquad \{3,4,5,6\}.
$$

There are $1+6+(\binom{6}{2}-2)+3=23$ distinct sets. Every member contains $4$, so intersections are nonempty. Intersections involving a singleton or pair have at most two elements. The only less immediate check is a triple against one of the added progressions. Inside the five-term window, the six triples through $4$ include four progressions and exactly the two excluded triples above; hence a three-element intersection is also a progression.

The following short exact-integer verifier checks all $\binom{23}{2}$ intersections and prints the claimed count; it uses no floating-point arithmetic.

```python
from itertools import combinations

N, m = 7, 4
def ap(s):
    s = sorted(s)
    if not s: return False
    if len(s) <= 2: return True
    d = s[1] - s[0]
    return d > 0 and all(s[i] - s[i-1] == d for i in range(2, len(s)))

family = [{m}] + [{m, x} for x in range(1, N+1) if x != m]
blocked = [{2, 4, 5}, {3, 4, 6}]
family += [{m, u, v} for u, v in combinations(
    [x for x in range(1, N+1) if x != m], 2)
    if {m, u, v} not in blocked]
family += [{2,3,4,5,6}, {2,3,4,5}, {3,4,5,6}]
bad = [(a, b) for a, b in combinations(family, 2) if not ap(a & b)]
print(len(family), len(bad))
```

The reproducible output is `23 0`, and the formula below also gives $\binom{7}{2}+1+\lfloor6/4\rfloor=23$. This is a lower-bound example, not a proof of optimality for $N=7$ or a solution for all $N$.

## What is known

Graham, Simonovits, and Sós proved in Proposition 4 of their 1980 paper, pp.108–109, that if empty intersections are allowed then the maximum is

$$
\binom{N}{3}+\binom{N}{2}+N+1,
$$

with the extremal family consisting of all sets of size at most three. That result is a useful boundary case, not a solution here, because #272 requires every pairwise intersection to be nonempty. Simonovits and Sós, *Intersection Properties of Subsets of Integers*, Theorem 3 p.365 and Problem 1 pp.371–372, gave an upper-bound framework and left the sharp nonempty case unresolved.

For the nonempty problem, their natural conjecture $t(N)=\binom{N}{2}+1$ was disproved by Szabó. His explicit construction and Theorem 2.1 give the unconditional bounds

$$
\binom{N}{2}+1+\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{4}\right\rfloor
\leq t(N)
=\frac{N^2}{2}+O\!\left(N^{5/3}(\log N)^3\right).
$$

The asymptotic statement determines the leading term but not the exact integer value. Yang’s arXiv v1 preprint (submitted 25 July 2026) reports, as Theorem 1.1, $t(3),\ldots,t(12)=4,7,12,17,23,30,39,48,58,69$. It also proves an exact upper bound matching Szabó’s construction for every *starred* family, meaning a family whose members all contain one common element (Theorem 1.4). The unrestricted upper bound still depends on the kernel question: must every extremal family be starred?

## What remains

The central residual question is whether a largest unrestricted family must have a common element. If so, Yang’s starred-family theorem would give the exact formula

$$
t(N)=\binom{N}{2}+1+\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{4}\right\rfloor.
$$

Without that kernel statement, a non-starred family could in principle exceed the bound. The reported values through $N=12$ are important finite evidence, but they do not settle arbitrary $N$ and were not independently replayed in this audit. A bounded next step is an independently verifiable exact upper-bound certificate for $N=13$, where Szabó’s construction has size $\binom{13}{2}+1+\lfloor12/4\rfloor=82$. Such a certificate would resolve only $t(13)$, not the infinite problem.

Any formal successor must first reconcile one-based indexing, the exact arithmetic-progression predicate, distinctness, and the $N\geq1$ boundary. It must compile without `sorry` and be checked independently; a formalized statement or an external proof artifact alone is insufficient. The unresolved source and semantic exceptions in the `PARTIAL` return should remain attached to any such work.
