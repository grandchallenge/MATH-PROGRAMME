## The question in plain language

Two collections of subsets of the natural numbers can be compared by ignoring *negligible* sets. But there is more than one notion of negligible.

[Erdős Problem #1123](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1123), attributed to Erdős and Ulam, considers two Boolean algebras: subsets of the integers **modulo sets of natural density zero**, and subsets **modulo sets of logarithmic density zero**.

Call the corresponding quotients $B_1$ and $B_2$. Erdős asked whether these Boolean algebras must be **nonisomorphic**—that is, whether no bijection can preserve all their Boolean operations.

This has a set-theoretic answer: **the isomorphism question is independent of ZFC** under the reported consistency results.

## A small example

Take the perfect squares up to $10,000$. There are $100$, forming just $1\%$ of the first $10,000$ integers. Their logarithmic weight also represents only a portion of the harmonic weight of the full interval.

In the limit, squares have both natural and logarithmic density zero. Thus $B_1$ and $B_2$ both identify the set of squares with the empty set.

Agreement on one sparse subset—or on all finite subsets—does **not** show that these infinite quotient algebras are isomorphic. Their differences depend on much subtler global structure.

## What is known

**Just and Krawczyk (1984)** proved that $B_1$ and $B_2$ are **isomorphic under the continuum hypothesis (CH)**.

**Farah (2000, Corollary 3.4.4)** proved they are **not isomorphic** under the **Open Coloring Axiom together with Martin's Axiom**, not merely OCA alone. Abraham, Rubin and Shelah established the consistency of the requisite combined axioms relative to ZFC.

The opposing models establish an independence result, assuming the published relative-consistency conditions. The problem is not resolved by choosing an unconditional “yes” or “no” inside ZFC.

An additional historical subtlety is whether the original Erdős–Ulam formulation implicitly assumed CH. Some later accounts explicitly do; the 1981 version is less clear.

## What remains
