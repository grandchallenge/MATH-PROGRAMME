## The question in plain language

Does there exist ABSOLUTE C>0 such that every n with sigma(n)>Cn is a sum of DISTINCT proper positive divisors? This is stronger than abundance sigma(n)>2n: such exceptional abundant nonsemiperfect numbers are weird. No requirement to use all divisors.

## A small example

n=70 has sigma(70)=144>2*70 but its proper positive divisors {1,2,5,7,10,14,35} have no subset sum 70. In contrast n=12 has proper divisors {1,2,3,4,6}, and 12=6+4+2 using distinct proper divisors. Explicit finite subset-sum algorithms can confirm both. Necessary universal C must exceed 2, not merely be positive.

The example can be checked exactly. A finite witness does not replace the universal, asymptotic, or infinitely-often quantifiers in the original question.

## What is known

Maintained https://www.erdosproblems.com/825 updated 2026-02-01 labels PROVED by Daniel Larsen, source proof https://github.com/Larsen-Daniel/Erdos-825/blob/main/825.pdf. It states for each epsilon>0 there is L so any n with least prime factor >L and sigma(n)>(2+epsilon)n is semiperfect. This least-prime-factor theorem is not logically identical by itself to an unconditional absolute C; the missing reduction to handle small prime factors needs checking in Larsen's actual proof, not assumption. Conjectural sharp C=3 is not declared proved. The PDF full proof has not been independently reviewed or formalized by GCL.

**Finite obstruction to $C=2$.** The proper positive divisors of 70 are

$$

\{1,2,5,7,10,14,35\},\qquad \sigma(70)=144>140.

$$

No subset sums to 70. Thus any universally valid $C$ must satisfy $C>2$. Larsen's reported theorem with a restriction on the least prime factor is a separate, stronger-looking assertion with a restricted input class: obtaining the original unconditional absolute $C$ requires a proof of the associated reduction. Its existence is reported by the catalogue; a particular numerical $C$, such as 3, is **not** established in this audit.
