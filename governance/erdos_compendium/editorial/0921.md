## The question in plain language

A graph has **chromatic number** $k$ when it can be properly colored using $k$ colors but cannot be colored using fewer. An **odd cycle** is a simple cycle containing an odd number of edges, such as a triangle or a five-cycle.

Fix an integer $k\ge4$. Among all graphs with $n$ vertices whose chromatic number is exactly $k$, how large can their **shortest odd cycle** be? Define $f_k(n)$ to be the largest integer $m$ for which some such graph contains no odd cycle of length at most $m$.

Erdős and Gallai asked whether

$$
f_k(n)\asymp n^{1/(k-2)}
$$

for each fixed integer $k$ at least four. This is [Erdős Problem #921](https://www.erdosproblems.com/921), proved affirmatively by Kierstead, Szemerédi and Trotter in 1984, building on earlier lower constructions. Both directions are needed: the **upper bound** forces a reasonably short odd cycle in any graph with chromatic number $k$, and the **lower bound** constructs graphs still requiring $k$ colors despite having no short odd cycles.

This is a question about **odd** cycles, not the usual girth excluding both odd and even cycles. A graph with no odd cycles at all is bipartite and therefore cannot have chromatic number four or more.

## A small example

The complete graph $K_4$ has chromatic number four and contains a triangle. Thus it has no odd cycle of length less than three, but its shortest odd cycle has length three. The largest integer $m$ below that length is two.

There are also four-chromatic graphs with **no triangles**; the Mycielski construction supplies familiar examples. Those examples demonstrate that short triangles are not an unavoidable feature of high chromatic number. The theorem instead controls how large the **shortest possible odd cycle** can grow with the total number of vertices.

Our small finite search shows that for every graph on at most six vertices with chromatic number at least four, there is a triangle. This is consistent with the fact that the smallest triangle-free four-chromatic graph has eleven vertices. Finite searches through six vertices do **not** determine the exponent $1/(k-2)$, which concerns arbitrarily large graph orders.

## What is known

The question appears in Erdős, [*Problems and results in chromatic graph theory*](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1969-13.pdf) (1969), and was attributed to Erdős and Gallai. Gallai, [*Kritische Graphen I*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/921), *Magyar Tudományos Akadémia Matematikai Kutató Intézetének Közleményei* (1963), pp.165–192, established a lower bound $f_4(n)\gg n^{1/2}$. Erdős stated an unpublished matching upper bound for $k=4$.

The complete general theorem appears in H. A. Kierstead, E. Szemerédi and W. T. Trotter, [*On coloring graphs with locally small chromatic number*](https://trotter.math.gatech.edu/papers/45.pdf), *Combinatorica* **4** (1984), pp.183–185, DOI [10.1007/BF02579219](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579219). GCL inspected the **authors' original three-page PDF**, including the theorem statement and the proof page, rather than relying on the catalogue label alone.

Their **Theorem 1** is stronger than an odd-cycle statement. For positive integers $c$ and $\ell$, if every subgraph $H$ whose radius in the ambient graph $G$ is at most

$$
2\ell n^{1/\ell}
$$

has chromatic number at most $c$, then

$$
\chi(G)\le\ell(c-1)+1.
$$

With $c=2$, sufficiently short-radius subgraphs are bipartite when the graph contains **no sufficiently short odd cycles**, yielding the upper bound sought by Erdős. For a target chromatic number $k$, set $\ell=k-2$. A graph lacking odd cycles shorter than a suitable constant multiple of $n^{1/(k-2)}$ would then be colorable with at most $k-1$ colors, contradicting $\chi(G)=k$.

On the lower side, the authors' **Example 3**, printed p.184, credits constructions due to Lovász (and independently Schrijver), showing that the exponent is essentially best possible when $c=2$. The paper's abstract explicitly describes the Lovász–Schrijver examples as the sharpness obstruction. Those construction details are not independently reconstructed here; the published result and the catalogue jointly support the matching lower exponent.

The maintained [#921 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/921), with [revision history](https://www.erdosproblems.com/history/921), labels the conjecture **PROVED**, citing [KST84]. That classification agrees with the actual 1984 paper. No protected Formal Conjectures statement file was provided in the admitted GCL snapshot for #921, and there is no source-matched Lean kernel proof being claimed.

## GCL's current state

The crucial parameter substitution in the published coloring theorem is elementary. Let the target graph have chromatic number $k\ge4$ and define $\ell=k-2$. With $c=2$, the bound of Kierstead–Szemerédi–Trotter is $\ell+1=k-1$. A graph of chromatic number $k$ therefore **cannot** satisfy the relevant no-small-radius-nonbipartite-subgraph condition. Tracing an odd cycle inside such a subgraph gives the asserted upper control of order $n^{1/(k-2)}$.

It is important not to replace the authors' notion of **radius of a subgraph inside the ambient graph** with diameter, or with an induced graph's intrinsic radius, without proving equivalence. Their actual Theorem 1 and Lemma 2 use ambient graph distances. GCL checked the publisher version of Theorem 1 and its proof page, but did **not** independently reconstruct every inductive inequality in Lemma 2.

The exact executable fixture at governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_920_921.py enumerates all 33,866 labelled simple graphs with two through six vertices. It computes the exact chromatic number of each and checks that every graph in this range with chromatic number at least four has a triangle. Of these, **5,281** have chromatic number at least four. This verifies the limited small-order instance of an upper odd-cycle restriction, not the full growth law.

The finite calculation also confirms the underlying coloring bound $\chi(G)\alpha(G)\ge|V(G)|$ used in the neighboring Ramsey problem #920. That combinatorial inequality is independent of the 1984 small-radius theorem; one does not derive #921 merely by appealing to chromatic Ramsey bounds.

## What remains

The first bounded verification task is the exact **odd-cycle-to-radius implication** in the 1984 theorem. Inputs: a finite graph $G$, a subgraph $H$, ambient distance $d_G$ and the radius $R_G(H)$. Establish that every nonbipartite subgraph of suitably small ambient radius yields an odd cycle whose length is bounded by an explicit multiple of the radius, with integer-rounding checked. Then substitute $c=2$ and $\ell=k-2$ into the published Theorem 1 to get a short odd cycle in any graph with chromatic number exactly $k$. Acceptance requires a complete proof or a pinned no-placeholder Lean theorem with a literal axiom report, and all small finite graph regressions; an unaddressed discrepancy between ambient and intrinsic distance invalidates the source equivalence.

The deeper independent proof-review task is to check the **KST84 Lemma 2 inductive obstruction construction** on the original printed p.184 and the **Lovász–Schrijver lower construction** described in Example 3. Matching both directions, with all constants dependent only on fixed $k$ and arbitrary sufficiently large $n$, is required for a source-independent derivation of the asymptotic equivalence.

There is no remaining conjectural yes-or-no question in the historical statement: its published status is **PROVED**. A smaller optimal constant, a separate odd-cycle-free local-coloring theorem, or a fully formal source-matched development would be successor work, not an unresolved original conjecture.
