## The question in plain language

Let $P(r)$ mean the largest prime factor of an integer $r$. Erdős Problem 370 asks whether there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ for which both consecutive numbers are unusually smooth:

$$
P(n)<\sqrt n \quad\text{and}\quad P(n+1)<\sqrt{n+1}.
$$

Here “smooth” means that no prime factor is too large. The strict inequality matters. A number such as $12=2^2\cdot3$ has $P(12)=3<\sqrt{12}$, whereas a prime number $q$ has $P(q)=q$, so it cannot pass the test. The two conditions must hold for the same $n$, not merely for infinitely many unrelated integers.

The original source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (L’Enseignement Mathématique, Monographie 28, 1980), printed page 69. Their wording is the question above. The source discusses a density observation of Pomerance and says that very little is known. The maintained problem page later records an elementary construction attributed to Stefan Steinerberger, which changes how the literal wording should be read: as written, the problem has a sparse infinite family.

## A small example

Take $m=9$ and $n=m^2-1=80$. Direct factorization gives

$$
80=2^4\cdot5,\qquad 81=3^4.
$$

Thus $P(80)=5$ and $P(81)=3$. Exact integer arithmetic verifies

$$
5^2=25<80,
\qquad
3^2=9<81.
$$

Since both sides are nonnegative, these squared inequalities are exactly the desired comparisons with the square roots. So $n=80$ is a genuine finite witness. It does not, by itself, establish infinitude, and a finite search cannot decide a statement quantified over all integers.

The example also shows the mechanism. The identities $80=(9-1)(9+1)$ and $81=9^2$ place the prime factors of $n$ and $n+1$ below the relevant square-root scales. The choice $m=9$ is especially convenient because $8$, $9$, and $10$ are all composite.

## What is known

The maintained entry at the frozen catalogue commit `536408716724a2402bdfa7c1d764bf83d64a8c89` marks Problem 370 “proved” and reports a Lean formalization. Its exact YAML entry is Git blob `4e60263a80af6207aca494d2e1388e44fa372889`, last updated there on 2025-11-24. The status agrees with the frozen packet, but catalogue status is evidence about the record, not GCL mathematical certification.

The live problem page gives the construction $n=m^2-1$. If $m$ and $m+1$ are composite, then the largest prime factor of $m^2$ is strictly less than $m=\sqrt{m^2}$, while every prime factor of $(m-1)(m+1)$ is at most $m+1$ and, with the composite choice, below $\sqrt{m^2-1}$ after the elementary factor bounds are applied. A uniform choice is $m=k!+3$ for every $k\ge3$: $m-1$ and $m+1$ are even and composite, and $m$ is divisible by $3$ and larger than $3$. This supplies distinct values $n=m^2-1$ without relying on a search.

The original Erdős–Graham document remains important because it fixes the historical statement and its intended context. The later explanation may indicate that the printed problem was mistyped or that a stronger density version was intended, but that speculation does not alter the registered literal question.

## What remains

For the literal statement, the useful remaining work is provenance and semantic maintenance rather than an open search for examples. A bounded successor could compare the elementary construction line by line with the formal theorem, prove the composite-number lemmas in a pinned Lean environment, and record whether the imported `Nat.maxPrimeFac` convention is harmless. It should also check whether the intended historical problem was a density or longer-block variant, since the original page links Problems 369 and 928 as related material.

No such variant should be silently substituted for Problem 370. In particular, the original source’s density remark and the live page’s warning about a possible typographical error are context, not a replacement theorem.
