**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #537 is **DISPROVED**. It proposed that a sufficiently dense set of integers must contain three members that become equal when multiplied by **three different primes**. Imre Ruzsa found a positive-density counterexample, described by Erdős in his 1973 survey. The precise construction uses squarefree integers whose successive prime factors grow by more than a factor of two.

## The question in plain language

Suppose $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ contains at least $\varepsilon N$ integers for some fixed $\varepsilon>0$. Must there exist $a_1,a_2,a_3\in A$ and **distinct primes** $p_1,p_2,p_3$ such that

$$
\boxed{p_1a_1=p_2a_2=p_3a_3?}
$$

The three selected elements are automatically different: if $p_i\ne p_j$ and $p_ia_i=p_ja_j$ for positive integers, then $a_i\ne a_j$.

The answer is **no**. Ruzsa constructed sets occupying a positive proportion of large integer intervals with no three such representations of any common integer.

This is distinct from #536's open equal-pairwise-LCM problem. If one could always find three suitably normalized prime-multiple representations then one could force an LCM triangle, but **failure of this stronger construction does not imply failure of the weaker LCM condition**.

## A small example

Take a positive squarefree integer

$$
a=q_1q_2\cdots q_r,\qquad q_1<q_2<\cdots<q_r,
$$

whose successive prime factors satisfy

$$
\boxed{q_{j+1}>2q_j\qquad(1\le j<r).}
$$

Such numbers have *widely separated prime factors*. Every prime is allowed as a one-prime product. For example, $2\cdot5=10$ qualifies, while $2\cdot3=6$ does not because $3$ is not greater than twice $2$.

Let $B$ denote the set of all such positive squarefree integers. For each $N$, restrict it to an upper half-interval,

$$
A_N=B\cap(N/2,N].
$$

The crucial fact is that **no number can have three different prime representations $m=pa$ with $a\in A_N$**.

Suppose otherwise:

$$
m=p_1a_1=p_2a_2=p_3a_3
$$

for three distinct primes. Reorder so that $a_2>a_3$. Then $p_2<p_3$ and

$$
1<\frac{p_3}{p_2}
=\frac{a_2}{a_3}<2,
$$

because both $a_2,a_3$ lie between $N/2$ and $N$. On the other hand, $p_2$ and $p_3$ both divide $a_1$, since they divide $m=p_1a_1$ and neither equals $p_1$.

The prime factors of $a_1$ are separated by a factor greater than two. Thus

$$
\frac{p_3}{p_2}>2,
$$

a direct contradiction. This is the decisive finite arithmetic obstruction.

## What is known

### Ruzsa's counterexample is already in Erdős's 1973 survey

The primary source is Erdős, *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, Chapter 12 of *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), [printed p.124](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=8). The original complete scanned chapter was downloaded and page 124 was read directly.

Erdős describes the proposed property, explaining that a positive answer would imply his equal-pairwise-LCM question (#536). Immediately afterward, he credits **Imre Ruzsa, then a sixteen-year-old Hungarian mathematician**, with the negative example.

Ruzsa's set consists of squarefree integers whose distinct prime factors satisfy the multiplicative separation rule in the example. Erdős asserts that this set has **positive asymptotic density**, and hence that its intersection with $(N/2,N]$ contains at least $cN$ integers for some $c>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$.

The argument that **at most two** prime-multiple representations can have a common product is exactly the ratio contradiction shown above. It applies separately for each $N$ and does not rely on statistical experiments or a limit theorem.

### Why there are enough admissible integers

The positive-density assertion is a second, genuinely infinitary component of the counterexample. A finite list of admissible numbers does not establish positive density.

An outline of an independent density argument begins by fixing a sufficiently large prime cutoff $P$. Restrict attention to positive integers with no prime factor below $P$ and with no squared prime factor. Standard sieve and Euler-product estimates show this restricted class has a **positive natural density** for fixed $P$.

Remove numbers divisible by two distinct primes $p<q\le2p$ with $p\ge P$. By the union bound, the upper density removed is at most

$$
\sum_{\substack{p\ge P\\p\ \mathrm{prime}}}
\ \sum_{\substack{p<q\le2p\\q\ \mathrm{prime}}}
\frac1{pq}.
$$

Classical prime-counting bounds, for example Chebyshev's estimates, make this double series converge and its tail tend to zero as $P$ grows. With a suitable cutoff, some positive density of the restricted squarefree class remains. Every surviving integer has the required separation between consecutive prime factors.

This explains the arithmetic mechanism behind the positive-density statement. The historical Erdős passage itself gives no complete detailed density proof, and this outline is **not represented as a separately peer-reviewed GCL proof certificate**.

### The distinction from the open LCM problem

It is tempting to conclude that Ruzsa also disproves #536. This would be logically invalid.

The existence of a threefold common prime-multiple representation, with the appropriate coprimality normalization, can yield three elements with equal pairwise least common multiples. But a triple with equal pairwise LCMs does not generally give three prime-multiple representations.

For instance, $6,10,15$ have all pairwise LCMs equal to $30$. Yet $30/6=5$, $30/10=3$, $30/15=2$ happen to be distinct primes in this example; more complicated LCM triangles can have composite quotients instead. Ruzsa forbids the stronger *prime quotient* configuration, not every possible LCM triangle.

Consequently #537 is DISPROVED while #536 remains OPEN. Both classifications must be preserved in the same editorial cohort.
