## The question in plain language

Let $A$ be an infinite set of positive integers. An ordinary sum may reuse an element: $hA$ consists of sums of $h$ members of $A$, repetitions allowed. A restricted sum uses distinct members, written $h\times A$. The ordinary order of $A$ is the least $h$ for which every sufficiently large integer lies in one of $A,2A,\ldots,hA$. The restricted order is the least $t$, if one exists, for which every sufficiently large integer lies in one of $A,2\times A,\ldots,t\times A$.

Erdős Problem 338 asks four related questions. When does a basis have a finite restricted order? If its ordinary order is $r$, is there a bound depending only on $r$? When are the two orders equal? It also asks whether a basis remains a basis after deleting any finite set, and whether that stronger deletion property forces a restricted order. “Sufficiently large” is essential: finitely many exceptional integers do not matter.

The original issue is [MATH-PROGRAMME #1608](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1608), and the maintained entry is [Erdős Problem 338](https://www.erdosproblems.com/338). The current page calls the problem open. That is a status report about the general questions, not a claim that no special cases are known.

## A small example

The maintained page gives Bateman’s useful obstruction. For $h\geq3$, take

$$
A_h=\{1\}\cup\{x>0:h\mid x\}.
$$

For $h=3$, every sufficiently large integer has an ordinary representation using at most three terms: a multiple of $3$ uses one term; a number congruent to $1$ uses $1+(n-1)$; and a number congruent to $2$ uses $1+1+(n-2)$. The last representation is allowed to repeat $1$, so it is not restricted. Thus the ordinary order is $3$.

The obstruction to restricted order is exact modular arithmetic. A distinct sum from $A_3$ contains either zero or one copy of $1$; all other summands are divisible by $3$. Therefore every restricted sum is congruent to $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$. Integers congruent to $2$ modulo $3$ are never represented, no matter how large they are.

I checked the finite prefix $A_3\cap[1,60]$ independently with an unbounded dynamic program for ordinary sums and a descending 0/1 subset-sum computation for distinct sums. The exact output was:

```text
A={1} union {3,6,...,60}
ordinary_max_for_5_to_60= 3
restricted_missing_residue_2= [5, 8, 11, 14]
14=1+1+12 (ordinary), 14 not in distinct subset sums= True
```

This verifies the finite calculation and illustrates the mechanism; it does not prove a statement about every possible basis, nor does a finite search establish an asymptotic order. The source packet also contains a material transcription issue: the scanned Erdős–Graham page appears to print the congruence class $\{x>0:x\equiv1\pmod h\}$ where the maintained page and later summaries give $\{1\}\cup\{x>0:h\mid x\}$. Those sets have different restricted-order behavior, so the formula must not be silently normalized.

## What is known

The primary source inspected here is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, “On bases with an exact order,” *Acta Arithmetica* 37 (1980), 201–207. Its p. 201 introduction defines ordinary and exact order; p. 206 defines restricted order and prints the four questions that became Problem 338. The same page records the Bateman example, Kelly’s order-two bound, and the examples of squares and triangular numbers. Because of the displayed-set discrepancy just noted, those p. 206 lines are evidence for the question and historical context, but not yet a clean source lock for the obstruction formula.

Several special results are recorded by the maintained page and have identifiable primary references. Kelly, “Restricted bases,” *American Journal of Mathematics* 79 (1957), 258–264, proves restricted order at most $4$ for an order-two basis and at most $3$ under positive lower density. Hennecart, “On the Restricted Order of Asymptotic Bases of Order Two,” *The Ramanujan Journal* 9 (2005), 123–130, gives the order-two upper bound and an example attaining restricted order $4$. Hegyvári, Hennecart, and Plagne, “A proof of two Erdős’ conjectures on restricted addition and further results,” *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* 560 (2003), 199–220, is the primary reference behind the later lower-bound literature. The maintained entry reports that for every $k\geq2$ there is a basis of order $k$ with restricted order at least $2^{k-2}+k-1$.

These results leave the general necessary-and-sufficient conditions and the best order-only bound open. In particular, an explicit family with no restricted order is not a solution of the whole problem; it only shows that ordinary basishood does not force restricted basishood.

## What remains

The smallest immediate research task is source reconciliation. Compare the p. 206 scan with the original book or a reliable contemporaneous reprint, determine whether the congruence-class display is a typographical error, and record the exact set, convention for positive integers, and quantifier “sufficiently large.” Then independently verify the Bateman modular argument for the corrected set.

A second bounded task can formalize only the elementary interface: define ordinary and restricted sumsets, prove that $A_3$ has ordinary order $3$, and prove that its restricted sums omit every integer congruent to $2$ modulo $3$. That would be a useful test case, not a formal solution of the open classification questions. A larger formalization would need the primary proofs of Kelly, Hennecart, and Hegyvári–Hennecart–Plagne, plus a semantic review of their hypotheses.
