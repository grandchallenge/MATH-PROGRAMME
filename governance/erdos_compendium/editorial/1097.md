## The question in plain language

A three-term arithmetic progression is a pattern

$$
a,\quad a+d,\quad a+2d,
$$

where $d>0$. Given $n$ distinct integers, how many **different positive common differences** can occur in such progressions?

[Erdős Problem #1097](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1097) asks for the maximum possible count. Erdős speculated that the answer might always be at most a constant times $n^{3/2}$. That particular estimate has since been **disproved**, although the correct growth exponent remains open.

## A small example

For the set $A=\{0,1,\ldots,8\}$, a progression of difference $d$ requires $2d\le8$. Exactly four positive differences occur:

$$
d\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$

For the sparse set $\{0,1,3,4,9,10\}$ there are no three-term arithmetic progressions at all. The size of a set alone does not determine how many differences it supports.

These direct checks illustrate the counting convention; they do not address the maximal asymptotic exponent.

## What is known

The maintained catalogue attributes the original question to Erdős at a 1989 number-theory meeting. It describes a connection observed by Chan to Bourgain's restricted sum-and-difference question arising in approaches to the Kakeya problem.

The catalogue reports that the exponent for the **maximum** number of differences lies between a lower construction of about $1.77898$ and the Katz–Tao upper exponent $11/6\approx1.8333$:

$$
n^{1.77898\ldots}\lesssim
\max_{|A|=n}|\{d:a,a+d,a+2d\in A\}|
\lesssim n^{11/6}
$$

up to the technical losses and quantifiers in the cited results. The lower constructions trace through Lemm (2015) and a reported later AlphaEvolve improvement. Since even the lower exponent exceeds $3/2$, the proposed $O(n^{3/2})$ bound cannot hold.

The exact equivalence of the two extremal formulations, and the precise exponent losses in those works, still require an independent primary-paper audit.
