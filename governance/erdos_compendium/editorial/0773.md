## The question in plain language

How many perfect squares can we select from the first $N$ squares while ensuring that every sum of two selected squares uniquely identifies the pair? Such a collection is a **Sidon set**. Erdős Problem #773 asks whether we can select almost $N$ squares on the scale of powers: at least $N^{1-o(1)}$ for large $N$. This is weaker than selecting a positive fraction of the squares, but much stronger than the presently established lower exponent $2/3$.

The question remains **OPEN** as of this source audit. A strong June 2026 upper bound improves what we know about how large such a set can be, but it does not settle the existence question.

## A small example

The first four squares form the set $S_4=\{1,4,9,16\}$. The ten unordered pair sums when repetitions are allowed are

$$
2,\ 5,\ 10,\ 17,\ 8,\ 13,\ 20,\ 18,\ 25,\ 32.
$$

All ten are different. Therefore the full set of four squares is Sidon. The order of a pair does not matter: $1+9$ and $9+1$ are the same representation, not two violations.

This property need not persist as more squares are included. At $N=7$ we find

$$
1^2+7^2=1+49=50=25+25=5^2+5^2.
$$

The unordered pairs $\{1,49\}$ and $\{25,25\}$ are distinct, so the entire set $S_7$ is not Sidon under the usual diagonal-inclusive definition. This finite collision does **not** estimate the asymptotic maximum: a large Sidon subset might still exist after deleting some squares.

## What is known

Write $S_N=\{1^2,\ldots,N^2\}$, and let $F(N)$ denote the maximum size of a Sidon subset of $S_N$. The problem asks whether

$$
F(N)=N^{1-o(1)}.
$$

The notation means that the exponent of the largest achievable set tends to one. In particular, a construction of only $N^{2/3}$ elements would not answer the conjecture.

Alon and Erdős discussed the question in [*An Application of Graph Theory to Additive Number Theory*](https://www.cs.tau.ac.il/~nogaa/PDFS/Publications2/An%20application%20of%20graph%20theory%20to%20additive%20number%20theory.pdf), *European Journal of Combinatorics* **6** (1985), pp. 201–203. On p. 203 they give, for each fixed positive $\varepsilon$, a probabilistic construction with at least $c(\varepsilon)N^{2/3-\varepsilon}$ elements, and ask whether the exponent can approach one. The maintained catalogue attributes a subsequent $\Omega(N^{2/3})$ lower bound to Lefmann and Thiele (1995); the latter's original proof still requires a separate source check in this tranche.

An elementary route to an upper bound counts the available sums of two squares. A Sidon subset of size $m$ determines $m(m+1)/2$ distinct sums, all at most $2N^2$. The classical Landau–Ramanujan estimate bounds the number of integers up to $2N^2$ representable as sums of two squares by $O(N^2/\sqrt{\log N})$. Hence

$$
m=O\left(\frac{N}{(\log N)^{1/4}}\right).
$$

This is an upper bound, not a construction.

A stronger modern result was proved in the five-author June 2026 preprint by Croot, Mao, Pohoata, Sheffer and Yip, [*A combinatorial large sieve for Sidon sets, distances, and norm forms*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17487), arXiv:2606.17487v2, Corollary 1.2:

$$
F(N)\leq N\exp\left(-\left(\frac{\log 2}{2}-o(1)\right)\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right).
$$

The authors' Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2 appear on pp. 2–3. Their method exploits the fact that the squares use only $(p+1)/2$ residue classes modulo each odd prime $p$, then combines local modular restrictions with the scarcity of repeated differences in a Sidon set. The paper itself describes this as the first super-polylogarithmic saving for #773. Its contribution is a much stronger **upper** bound.

Even this upper bound is still of the form $N^{1-o(1)}$. It therefore neither confirms nor rules out a construction of size $N^{1-o(1)}$. A bound $F(N)\leq N^{1-\delta}$ for some fixed $\delta>0$ would have a different logical consequence, but no such bound is claimed here.
