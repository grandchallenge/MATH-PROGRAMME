## The question in plain language

Start with a finite collection $Q_0$ of prime numbers. At each step, examine all triples of **three different primes already present**. If a triple adds up to a prime, include that new prime in the collection. Repeat.

Does some finite choice of starting primes generate an arbitrarily large collection?

For example, from $3,5,7,11$ we may form $3+5+11=19$ and $5+7+11=23$, both prime. We are allowed to add $19$ and $23$, but not a composite sum. The distinction between using distinct primes and allowing a prime twice is crucial.

Write

$$
Q_{k+1}
=
Q_k\cup
\bigl\{p\text{ prime}:p=a+b+c,\ 
a,b,c\in Q_k\text{ pairwise distinct}\bigr\}.
$$

The question is whether some finite $Q_0$ gives

$$
\lvert Q_k\rvert\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

**Answer: yes.** Indeed, one can choose a sufficiently large initial finite set that eventually generates **every prime**.

Erdős and Graham also specifically asked what happens if we start with just $\{3,5,7,11\}$. This smaller-seed question is **not** answered by the general existence argument.

## A small example

Using the proposed four-prime seed gives the following exact finite computation:

| Generation | Number of primes | Some new members |
|---|---:|---|
| $Q_0$ | $4$ | $3,5,7,11$ |
| $Q_1$ | $6$ | $19,23$ |
| $Q_2$ | $12$ | $29,31,37,41,47,53$ |
| $Q_3$ | $29$ | $43,59,61,67,71,\ldots$ |

For the first step, enumerate the four triples:

$$
3+5+7=15,\quad
3+5+11=19,\quad
3+7+11=21,\quad
5+7+11=23.
$$

Only $19$ and $23$ are prime. At the next step, for example, $3+7+19=29$ and $7+19+23=49$; the former can be added, while the latter cannot.

This shows how the rule works, and the finite values are independently reproducible. However, **three generations, or even a million generations, cannot prove** that the particular four-prime seed will grow forever.

## What is known

The question is due to Stanisław Ulam and was recorded by Paul Erdős and Ronald Graham in *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed page 94.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #471](https://www.erdosproblems.com/471) reports an affirmative solution, credited to **Rudi Mrazović and Vjekoslav Kovač**, and independently to **Noga Alon**. The key is **Vinogradov's three-primes theorem**.

The version of Vinogradov's theorem needed here is more than the statement that every sufficiently large odd number is a sum of three primes. We need the summands to be **pairwise distinct**. This follows from the quantitative theorem: for every sufficiently large odd integer $N$, the number of ordered prime triples with

$$
N=p_1+p_2+p_3
$$

is at least a positive constant times $N^2/(\log N)^3$. By comparison, at most $O(N)$ ordered triples have any repeated prime, because once we choose the repeated prime, the third summand is determined.

For large $N$,

$$
\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^3}\gg N.
$$

Thus there must be a representation of $N$ as a sum of **three distinct primes**. Because $N$ is odd and sufficiently large, all three summands can be taken odd.

There is a deep analytic-number-theory input here. Vinogradov proved his theorem using the circle method, and later authors substantially developed this subject. As one modern primary reference, Kaisa Matomäki, James Maynard and Xuancheng Shao proved in 2017 that all sufficiently large odd integers can be represented as sums of three primes lying very close to one third of the target integer. Their result illustrates the strength of ternary prime representation theory. Neither their proof nor Vinogradov's original circle-method proof has been reproduced by GCL in this article.

### How the theorem solves the problem

Choose a threshold $N_0$ so that **every odd integer greater than $N_0$** is a sum of three distinct primes, necessarily smaller than that integer.

Let $Q_0$ contain **all primes at most $N_0$**. This is a finite set.

We show by strong induction on primes $p$ that every prime eventually belongs to one of the generations $Q_k$.

For $p\leq N_0$ this is immediate: $p\in Q_0$.

If $p>N_0$, then $p$ is odd and the theorem gives

$$
p=a+b+c,
$$

where $a,b,c$ are **distinct primes** satisfying $a,b,c<p$. By induction, each already appears in some finite generation, say in $Q_{i_a}$, $Q_{i_b}$ and $Q_{i_c}$. Set

$$
j=\max\{i_a,i_b,i_c\}.
$$

Because the generations are increasing sets, all three summands belong to $Q_j$. The rule therefore adds $p$ by $Q_{j+1}$.

Hence the union of the generations contains every prime:

$$
\bigcup_{k\geq0}Q_k=\{\text{all prime numbers}\}.
$$

There are infinitely many primes, so the cardinalities $\lvert Q_k\rvert$ are unbounded.

This is a complete reduction of Erdős #471 to an established theorem, not a new solution.

### What is not established

Nothing in the proof produces an explicit small $N_0$, because a concrete numerical threshold for the precise distinct-summand version was not supplied.

Nor does it establish that the particular starting set $\{3,5,7,11\}$ generates all primes, or even infinitely many. A theorem giving **some** successful finite seed cannot automatically be applied to a **specified** seed.

## GCL's current state

GCL's [authenticated #471 audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1741#issuecomment-6166958720) compares the exact original 1980 statement with the established ternary Goldbach theorem and reconstructs the induction step.

The audit explicitly checks the three distinctions most likely to cause an incorrect explanation: three **distinct** summands, generation-by-generation additions rather than arbitrary repeated closure, and **existence of a finite successful seed** rather than proof about the original four-prime seed.

The values $\lvert Q_0\rvert=4$, $\lvert Q_1\rvert=6$, $\lvert Q_2\rvert=12$, $\lvert Q_3\rvert=29$ were obtained using exact prime testing and unordered combinations of three distinct elements. These are mathematical regression examples, not an infinity argument.

No independently compiled Lean proof of Vinogradov's theorem or of the formal reduction, and no GCL mathematical certification, is claimed.

## What remains

The mathematical status of the **existential** question is settled. Useful further work is limited and precise.

A bounded source-fidelity task can identify a primary quantitative version of Vinogradov's theorem with the exact asymptotic representation count, verify that repeated-prime solutions contribute only $O(N)$, and check the induction in a formal proof assistant. Acceptance would require precise theorem and parameter provenance, no unproved existential quantifier exchange, and a Lean compilation with recorded axioms.

Separately, the concrete four-prime seed $\{3,5,7,11\}$ remains an interesting computational and potentially mathematical research task. Any assertion about its infinite growth would require a proof that is not supplied by this article. More finite iterations may be useful evidence, but are not a substitute.
