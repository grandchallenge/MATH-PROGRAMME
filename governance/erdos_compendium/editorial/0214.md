## The question in plain language

Suppose $S\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ contains **no pair of points at Euclidean distance one**. Must its complement contain the vertices of a **unit square**? The complement consists of every planar point not in $S$, regardless of whether $S$ is measurable or finite.

Equivalently, colour the plane red and blue so that there is no blue unit-distance pair. The conjecture asks whether a **red** unit square is unavoidable.

## A small example

Take $S=\{(0,0)\}$. It contains no unit-distance pair, and the complement contains innumerable unit squares away from the origin. This verifies the conclusion in one easy case but says nothing about adversarial infinite sets $S$.

A unit square has four points with side lengths one, perpendicular adjacent sides and diagonals of length $\sqrt2$. A rotated square still counts. Requiring only four points that form **some** square would weaken the statement.

## What is known

**PROVED.** András Juhász (1979) showed the much stronger result: for **every configuration of four planar points**, the complement of a unit-distance-free set contains a **congruent copy** of that configuration. A unit square is a special case, so its existence follows immediately.

The maintained [Erdős #214](https://www.erdosproblems.com/214) also records a separate extremal question: the largest number $k$ such that every unit-distance-avoiding two-colouring has a red congruent copy of **every** $k$ point set. The current bounds are

$$
4\le k\le7.
$$

Juhász's universal four-point theorem gives the lower bound; Csizmadia and Tóth (1994) supplied the upper bound seven, improving earlier bounds. The exact value of this **stronger** Ramsey parameter is not known. Proving the original square existence does not settle it.

## What remains

A bounded finite fixture enumerates placements and orientations of unit squares in a small rational-coordinate grid, checks that no pair in the forbidden blue set has squared distance one, and tests red-square witnesses. Acceptance: all four side lengths equal one, the diagonal lengths equal $\sqrt2$, and restrictions of a whole-plane problem to a finite grid are labelled exploratory only. Replaying Juhász's theorem is a separate proof task.
