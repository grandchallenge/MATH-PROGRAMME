**Editorial status:** This is an unaccepted mathematical explanation of an **open** growth problem. The cited lower bounds and small Schur numbers are established results; the proposed exponential upper bound remains unproved. The GCL finite checks described below are not a resolution.

## The question in plain language

Color each of the positive integers $1,2,\ldots,N$ using one of $k$ colors. We are looking for three integers of the **same color** satisfying

$$
a+b=c.
$$

For example, if $1$ and $2$ have the same color, then $1+1=2$ is a monochromatic solution. The two summands are allowed to be **equal**. That detail separates the ordinary Schur problem from the related *weak Schur number* problem.

Schur's theorem guarantees that however we color the positive integers with finitely many colors, a monochromatic equation $a+b=c$ must eventually appear. But **how large must $N$ be before it is unavoidable?**

Define

$$
f(k)=\min\Bigl\{N\ge1:\text{every }k\text{-coloring of }
\{1,\ldots,N\}\text{ contains a monochromatic }a+b=c\Bigr\}.
$$

Erdős Problem #483 asks whether there is **one absolute constant** $C>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle f(k)<C^k\quad\text{for every }k\ge1.}
$$

This is an exponential bound in the **number of colors**. The current general upper bound is of factorial size, much larger than a fixed-base exponential for large $k$. The conjectured improvement remains **open**.

Some authors instead write $S(k)$ for the largest $N$ that *can still be colored without* a monochromatic triple. These two conventions differ by exactly one:

$$
\boxed{f(k)=S(k)+1.}
$$

Confusing the two makes even small examples seem contradictory.

## A small example

With two colors, we can color $\{1,2,3,4\}$ without any monochromatic solution:

| Integer | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Color | Red | Blue | Blue | Red |

The red set $\{1,4\}$ is sum-free inside the interval, and so is the blue set $\{2,3\}$.

However, a fifth integer cannot be colored without creating a monochromatic triple. To see this, call the color of $1$ red. Then $2$ must be blue, because $1+1=2$. Next $4$ must be red, because $2+2=4$. Since $1+3=4$, the integer $3$ must be blue. Finally $5$ cannot be red, because $1+4=5$, and it cannot be blue, because $2+3=5$.

Thus

$$
S(2)=4,\qquad f(2)=5.
$$

For three colors, an explicit assignment to $\{1,\ldots,13\}$ is

$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
\text{Color 0} & 1,4,7,10,13\\
\text{Color 1} & 2,3,11,12\\
\text{Color 2} & 5,6,8,9
\end{array}
$$

Each color class avoids $a+b=c$. Exhaustive backtracking, with color labels identified up to permutation, proves that no three-coloring of $\{1,\ldots,14\}$ avoids a monochromatic triple. Hence

$$
S(3)=13,\qquad f(3)=14.
$$

These finite checks are helpful examples and useful regression tests for a mathematical formalization. But even an enormous list of exact values of $f(k)$ would not establish one exponential bound valid for **all** $k$.

## What is known

### Erdős's original question

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), Paul Erdős stated this question as Problem 20, printed pages 232–233. He recalled Schur's theorem and noted that a general bound of approximately factorial size was known. He suggested that the minimal forcing threshold might satisfy

$$
f(k)<C^k.
$$

He also mentioned the stronger possibility that the sequence of $k$‑th roots,

$$
f(k)^{1/k},
$$

might converge to a constant. Convergence of these roots is **not the same assertion** as existence of a finite exponential upper bound: convergence would give more detailed information about the growth rate.

His following discussion of an interval-coloring problem due to Turán adds the condition $a\ne b$. That condition belongs to a **different question**. Ordinary Schur numbers do **not** prohibit repeated summands.

### Exact small values

The first five forcing thresholds are

| Colors $k$ | Last avoidable $S(k)$ | First forced $f(k)$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 | 5 |
| 3 | 13 | 14 |
| 4 | 44 | 45 |
| 5 | 160 | 161 |

The most challenging finite result was established by **Marijn Heule**, in *Schur Number Five* (2018). The construction shows that five colors suffice through $160$, while a computer-assisted proof shows that **every** five-coloring of the integers through $161$ contains a monochromatic solution.

The published proof uses propositional satisfiability (SAT) methods and a very large proof certificate, approximately **two petabytes**, checked through a formally verified proof checker. This is substantial published evidence for **one finite value**. GCL has not independently replayed that entire certificate.

### The strongest source-checked exponential lower bound

There is an important distinction between **constructing many avoiding colorings**, which gives a lower bound for $f(k)$, and **proving every coloring must fail**, which gives an upper bound.

A 2022 paper by **Romain Ageron, Paul Casteras, Thibaut Pellerin, Yann Portella, Arpad Rimmel and Joanna Tomasik**, *New lower bounds for Schur and weak Schur numbers*, builds special patterned colorings called *Schur templates*. Its central reusable inequality is

$$
S(k+5)\ge380\,S(k)+148,
$$

using the usual integer reading of the authors' strict inequality. Iterating this recurrence produces an exponential lower bound of the form

$$
f(k)\ge 380^{k/5}-O(1)
$$

in the asymptotic sense, giving a growth base of approximately

$$
380^{1/5}\approx3.2806.
$$

Thus, if Erdős's conjecture is true, the exponential constant $C$ cannot be arbitrarily small.

The construction is source-grounded. A *Schur template* is not merely any coloring with no monochromatic solutions: most of its color classes must satisfy extra **wraparound** restrictions, which allow two smaller colorings to be combined into a larger one. The paper's Definition 2.1 and Theorem 2.3 specify those restrictions, and its inequalities on printed pages 6–7 establish the improvement.

The very existence of exponential **lower** growth is perfectly compatible with an exponential **upper** bound. It does not answer the question.

### The available upper bound and Ramsey theory

The maintained Erdős Problems entry reports the general upper bound

$$
f(k)\le\left(e-\frac16\right)k!,
$$

attributed to **Xu, Xie and Chen** (2002). Their full original paper was not independently inspected in this audit, so the exact leading constant is presented as a catalogue-reported published result, not a newly reproduced proof.

A clean mathematical reduction explains why bounds for multicolor triangle Ramsey numbers are relevant.

Let $R(3;k)$ be the least integer $V$ such that every coloring of the edges of the complete graph on $V$ vertices with $k$ colors contains a monochromatic triangle.

Suppose the integers $1,\ldots,N$ have been colored with $k$ colors, and form a complete graph on the vertices

$$
0,1,\ldots,N.
$$

For $i<j$, color the edge $\{i,j\}$ by the color assigned to the positive difference $j-i$.

A monochromatic triangle $i<j<\ell$ gives three same-colored positive differences

$$
a=j-i,\qquad b=\ell-j,\qquad c=\ell-i,
$$

satisfying $a+b=c$. Therefore, whenever the graph has enough vertices to force a monochromatic triangle, the original integer coloring must contain a Schur triple. In particular,

$$
\boxed{f(k)\le R(3;k)-1.}
$$

This reduction is rigorous and elementary. But the known general bounds for these multicolor Ramsey numbers are also factorial in $k$. Transferring them to $f(k)$ therefore does **not** establish Erdős's desired fixed-base exponential upper bound.

## GCL's current state

The [authenticated issue-bound source audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1753#issuecomment-6179196927) directly inspected the original 1961 Erdős paper, printed pages 232–233, the published-source version of Ageron and colleagues' template construction, and Heule's primary 2018 paper and certificate documentation.

An independent exhaustive Python backtracker reproduced the first three thresholds, checking:

- $f(1)=2$, with the repeated-summand triple $1+1=2$;
- $f(2)=5$, including the explicit four-integer avoiding coloring;
- $f(3)=14$, including an explicit thirteen-integer avoiding coloring and exhaustive rejection at fourteen.

The backtracker eliminates duplicate branches caused only by renaming colors; it does **not** omit legitimate colorings. New color names are introduced in a canonical order. Each time a new integer receives a color, the program checks all possible decompositions of that integer as $a+b$, including $a=b$.

The external Heule five-color proof is vastly larger. GCL does not claim its reproduction merely because the small backtracking tests pass.

The protected GCL Formal Conjectures snapshot contains no Erdős #483 statement file. A newer unpinned upstream version defines the coloring as a function on a finite interval and correctly allows repeated summands. Its two trivial one-color tests have direct finite proofs, but its **general exponential theorem is still `by sorry`**. No GCL MathCert follows from its presence.

This manuscript remains **UNACCEPTED_DRAFT**. Problem #483 belongs to the cohort's required deterministic critical sample, and the final review must not be confused with the present source and mathematical checks.

## What remains

The genuine unresolved problem is to replace factorial upper growth with a fixed-base exponential bound. That requires a structural mathematical theorem, not simply more finite enumeration.

A productive bounded task is to **formalize and independently verify the reusable Schur-template construction**. Starting from a precisely described sum-free coloring and an admissible template, implement the paper's Theorem 2.3 and prove that the resulting larger coloring has no monochromatic $a+b=c$. The proof must cover all cases of the special color and all wraparound constraints.

A second bounded task is to certify the exact small cases with two independent algorithms: an exhaustive color-symmetry-broken search and a SAT encoding with proof-producing unsatisfiability checks. This builds a reliable regression foundation without suggesting that it solves the asymptotic question.

A third elementary formalization target is the difference-coloring reduction

$$
f(k)\le R(3;k)-1.
$$

It should be proved with the exact vertices $\{0,\ldots,N\}$ and the correct difference $j-i$, making the off-by-one and repeated-summand semantics explicit.

These are suitable bounded mathematical verification tasks. They provide useful infrastructure for further research, but no one should claim that an independently checked finite table or a single template gives an all‑$k$ exponential upper bound.
