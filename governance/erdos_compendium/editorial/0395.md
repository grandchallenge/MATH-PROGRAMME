## The question in plain language

Choose $n$ complex numbers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ on the unit circle. Independently choose each sign $\varepsilon_i$ to be $+1$ or $-1$, and add the signed vectors:

$$
S=\varepsilon_1z_1+\cdots+\varepsilon_nz_n.
$$

Problem 395 asks whether there is an absolute constant $c>0$ such that, for every $n>0$ and every unit choice of the $z_i$, at least a fraction $c/n$ of the $2^n$ sign choices satisfy

$$
|S|\leq\sqrt{2}.
$$

The probability is uniform over all $2^n$ sign patterns. Thus the claim is a lower bound that must work simultaneously for every configuration of unit complex numbers; it is not a statement about a typical configuration. In geometric language, signed walks made from arbitrary unit steps must return to the disk of radius $\sqrt{2}$ often enough. This is called a reverse Littlewood–Offord problem because ordinary Littlewood–Offord results usually give upper bounds on concentration.

The historical version used radius $1$. The present registered statement uses $\sqrt{2}$, a material normalization rather than a cosmetic change.

## A small example

Take four unit steps, two equal to $1$ and two equal to $i$. An exact enumeration can be done with integer coordinates: each sign pattern produces $(x,y)$, where $x$ is the signed sum of the two real steps and $y$ is the signed sum of the two imaginary steps. The condition $|S|\leq\sqrt{2}$ is exactly $x^2+y^2\leq2$.

The real coordinate is zero in exactly two of the four sign choices for the real steps, and the imaginary coordinate is zero in exactly two choices for the imaginary steps. Therefore exactly $2\cdot2=4$ of the $2^4=16$ patterns give $(x,y)=(0,0)$, so the probability is

$$
\frac{4}{16}=\frac14.
$$

This was independently checked by enumerating the sixteen sign tuples with integer arithmetic; the accepted squared norm among the hits was exactly $0$. It illustrates the counting convention and avoids numerical approximations. It does not prove a uniform bound for arbitrary vectors or any asymptotic statement.

The radius-one form already fails in the smallest two-step example $z_1=1$, $z_2=i$. Every signed sum is one of $\pm1\pm i$, whose squared norm is $2$, so none lies in the unit disk. This is the counterexample attributed to Carnielli and Carolino on the live entry. Replacing $1$ by $\sqrt{2}$ is therefore essential.

## What is known

Paul Erdős posed the original question in “On a lemma of Littlewood and Offord,” *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* 51 (1945), 898–902, [DOI 10.1090/S0002-9904-1945-08454-7](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9904-1945-08454-7). Carnielli and Carolino, “Adjusting a conjecture of Erdős,” *Contributions to Discrete Mathematics* 6 (2011), 154–159, [DOI 10.55016/ojs/cdm.v6i1.62011](https://doi.org/10.55016/ojs/cdm.v6i1.62011), give the planar radius-one counterexample and explain the adjusted radius.

The primary resolving source checked for this audit is Xiaoyu He, Tomas Juškevičius, Bhargav Narayanan, and Sam Spiro, “The Reverse Littlewood–Offord problem of Erdős,” [arXiv:2408.11034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11034), version 3 PDF, SHA-256 `834e4098f8411787bf87e4e5c71f9333e1d391ddb7dd67693565125c6afa7cc1`. Theorem 1.1 on p. 2 states that an absolute $c>0$ exists for unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with threshold $\sqrt{2}$ and probability at least $c/n$; the proof is completed on p. 13. Complex unit numbers and unit vectors in the plane are the same data under the usual identification.

That paper also records a stronger earlier result by József Beck, “On a Geometric Problem of Erdős, Sárközy, and Szermerédi Concerning Vector Sums,” *European Journal of Combinatorics* 4 (1983), 1–10, [DOI 10.1016/S0195-6698(83)80002-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0195-6698(83)80002-X). Beck’s result is cited by the later paper as covering the relevant higher-dimensional form. This article relies on the directly inspected HJNS theorem for the exact registered planar statement, without claiming an independent replay of either proof.
