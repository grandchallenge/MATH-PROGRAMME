## The question in plain language

Let $F_1=F_2=1$ and $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$, so the positive-index Fibonacci numbers begin $1,1,2,3,5,8,\ldots$. Erdős and Graham ask about an infinite increasing sequence of positive integers

$$
n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots
$$

whose indices are separated at a fixed multiplicative rate: there is a real number $c>1$ such that

$$
\frac{n_{k+1}}{n_k}\ge c
$$

for every $k$. Must the reciprocal Fibonacci sum

$$
\sum_{k\ge 1}\frac{1}{F_{n_k}}
$$

be irrational? The original wording appears on p. 65 of Erdős and Graham’s [1980 scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf), and the maintained [Erdős Problem 267 entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/267) reproduces it.

The quantifiers matter. The question concerns every admissible pair consisting of a sequence and a fixed rate $c>1$; it is not asking whether one can choose a convenient rate after seeing the sequence. Equivalently, a disproof would need one $c>1$ and one sequence satisfying the displayed inequality whose sum is rational. The known boundary is $c=2$: the case $c\ge 2$ is proved, while the interval $1<c<2$ is the unresolved part. The sequence is positive-indexed in the original statement, so the quotients are ordinary real quotients of positive integers.

## A small example

Take the especially sparse indices $n_k=2^k$, starting at $k=0$. The relevant Fibonacci denominators are

$$
F_1,F_2,F_4,F_8,F_{16},F_{32},F_{64},F_{128}
=1,1,3,21,987,2178309,10610209857723,251728825683549488150424261.
$$

Using exact integer arithmetic, the first eight terms have sum

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{7}\frac{1}{F_{2^k}}
=
\frac{599609506830117398769623090}{251728825683549488150424261}.
$$

This is independently reproducible without floating-point arithmetic:

```python
from fractions import Fraction

F = [0, 1]
for _ in range(2, 129):
    F.append(F[-1] + F[-2])
S = sum((Fraction(1, F[2**k]) for k in range(8)), Fraction())
print(S)
```

The exact output is also recorded in the authenticated [RESULT/1 source audit](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1537#issuecomment-6137999998); the code above recomputes it independently.

The computation checks a finite rational identity, not the value or irrationality of the infinite sum. Good’s 1974 paper and Hoggatt–Bicknell’s 1976 paper establish the stronger special identity

$$
\sum_{k\ge 0}\frac{1}{F_{2^k}}=\frac{7-\sqrt{5}}{2},
$$

so this particular sum is irrational. It is a useful calibration example: the ratio is exactly $2$, and therefore it lies in the proved boundary case rather than in the difficult interval.

## What is known

The first relevant special case is the power-of-two series just displayed. Good gives the identity on p. 346; Hoggatt–Bicknell derive a wider family on pp. 453–455 and recover the same value when their parameter is $1$. Badea’s 1987 paper proves another special case, namely irrationality of $\sum_{k\ge 0}1/F_{2^k+1}$ in Corollary 4 on p. 226. These results settle particular index patterns, not the full quantifier over all rapidly separated sequences.

The decisive published interface for the general question is C. Badea, “A theorem on irrationality of infinite series and applications,” *Acta Arithmetica* 63 (1993), 313–323, [DOI 10.4064/aa-63-4-313-323](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-63-4-313-323). Badea considers the recurrence

$$
x_0=0,\qquad x_1=1,\qquad x_{m+2}=a x_{m+1}+b x_m
$$

with positive integers $a$ and $b$. Lemma 3.1(i), p. 317, gives

$$
x_{2m-1}=x_m^2+b x_{m-1}^2\ge x_m^2.
$$

Corollary 3.2, p. 318, says that if $n(k+1)\ge 2n(k)-1$ for all sufficiently large $k$, then $\sum_k1/x_{n(k)}$ is irrational. Setting $a=b=1$ identifies the positive terms of this recurrence with the Fibonacci numbers. If the Erdős condition holds with $c\ge2$, then $n_{k+1}\ge2n_k$, hence Badea’s weaker eventual inequality applies. This proves the whole $c\ge2$ subcase.

No inspected primary source resolves $1<c<2$. The maintained entry therefore reports the problem as open. Its attribution of irrationality for $\sum_{n\ge1}1/F_n$ to André-Jeannin is retained as a historical lead, but that cited source was not accessible in the source audit and is not promoted here as independently checked evidence.

## What remains

The mathematical residue is precise: determine whether every sequence with a fixed ratio in $1<c<2$ still gives an irrational sum, or produce one rational counterexample. A bounded next theorem would formalize only Badea’s $c\ge2$ interface with explicit real reciprocals, positive indices, convergence, and the index shift between $F_n$ and `Nat.fib`. Its acceptance test should include the recurrence bridge, the eventual inequality, and a kernel-checked proof with no `sorry`.

That task would not settle the original open interval. In particular, a proof for $n_k=2^k$, for $n_k=2^k+1$, or for the unreviewed external full-range claim would remain a special case or an unverified claim unless its exact semantics and proof were independently replayed.
