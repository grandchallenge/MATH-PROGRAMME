## The question in plain language

A family of sets is *2-intersecting* if every two distinct members share at least two elements. Erdős #83 asks how large such a family can be when each set has size $2n$ and the ground set has size $4n$.

The precise proposed bound is

$$
|\mathcal F|\le\frac12\left[\binom{4n}{2n}-\binom{2n}{n}^{2}\right].
$$

All members of $\mathcal F$ must have **exactly** $2n$ elements; using sets of any size, or requiring only a nonempty intersection, is a different extremal question.

## A small example

For $n=1$, the ground set is $[4]$ and each member has two elements. Distinct two-element sets intersect in at most one element, so a 2-intersecting family has at most one member. The formula gives $(\binom42-\binom21^2)/2=(6-4)/2=1$.

For $n=2$, divide $[8]$ into two blocks of four, and include precisely those four-element sets containing at least three elements of the first block. There are $\binom43\binom41+\binom44=17$ such sets, and every pair intersects in at least two elements. The conjectured upper bound equals $(\binom84-\binom42^2)/2=(70-36)/2=17$.

## What is known

**PROVED.** Rudolf Ahlswede and Levon Khachatrian solved the general complete intersection problem in [*The Complete Intersection Theorem for Systems of Finite Sets*](https://doi.org/10.1006/eujc.1995.0092), *European Journal of Combinatorics* 18 (1997), 125–136. Their theorem identifies maximum-size $t$ intersecting, $k$ uniform families on an $m$ element ground set, with a parameter controlling the optimal initial segment.

The historical Erdős case specializes to $m=4n$, $k=2n$, $t=2$. The canonical source states that the extremal family is formed by sets containing at least $n+1$ elements of one chosen $2n$ element block; that construction is precisely the one counted above. The primary publisher bibliographic record and its connection to the complete intersection theorem have been checked. The original 1997 proof and detailed boundary-parameter substitution are not independently replayed here.

## What remains

A bounded proof task is to establish the exact family-size identity using the complement involution on middle-layer sets: among the $\binom{4n}{2n}$ sets, those having exactly $n$ elements of the first block number $\binom{2n}{n}^2$; the remaining sets split into two equal classes by complement. Acceptance: exact half-count and 2-intersection of the strict-majority class, with a computational cross-check for $n=1,2$. The deep universal **upper** bound requires the 1997 theorem and must not be inferred from this example.
