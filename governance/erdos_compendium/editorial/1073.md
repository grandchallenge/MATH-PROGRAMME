## The question in plain language

For which **composite** numbers $u$ is there an integer $n\ge0$ such that

$$
u\mid n!+1?
$$

Let $A(x)$ count those composite $u$ with $u<x$. [Erdős Problem #1073](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1073) asks whether this counting function grows more slowly than every fixed positive power of $x$:

$$
A(x)\le x^{o(1)}.
$$

In plain language, are composite divisors of numbers of the form $n!+1$ exceptionally sparse when we require the divisor $u$ itself to be the modulus?

## A small example

The smallest two examples are immediate:

$$
4!+1=25,\qquad5!+1=121.
$$

Thus $u=25$ and $u=121$ both qualify, and neither is prime. The number $169$ qualifies because $169\mid12!+1$.

A direct modular scan of every composite $u\le1000$ finds exactly seven qualifying moduli:

$$
25,\ 121,\ 169,\ 437,\ 551,\ 667,\ 721.
$$

Their smallest witness indices are respectively $4,5,12,18,18,18,6$. Therefore $A(1001)=7$. These finite results agree with the catalogue's published initial terms.

## What is known

The source question was raised by Erdős in discussions with Hardy and Subbarao, and is associated with their 2002 paper and Guy's collection, problem A2. The maintained catalogue calls the proposed subpolynomial growth **open**.

A useful elementary observation makes bounded checking exact: if $n\ge u$, then $u$ is a factor of $n!$, so

$$
n!+1\equiv1\pmod u.
$$

Therefore a successful witness for $u$ must satisfy $n<u$. To test one composite modulus, it is enough to compute at most $u$ successive factorial residues; no unbounded search is needed. This exact finite search property says nothing by itself about how $A(x)$ grows for arbitrarily large $x$.

## GCL's current state

[Authenticated source audit #2344](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/2344#issuecomment-6157195353) checks the strict boundary $u<x$, the distinction from the neighboring prime question #1072, and the [frozen Lean definition](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/1073.lean). That formal statement contains an unproved general growth claim.

The seven-modulus finite table was generated by exact modular multiplication. The original Hardy–Subbarao source pages and the exact exponent conventions have not yet been independently reviewed.

## What remains

One would need a uniform mathematical upper bound on the count of qualifying composites, not merely a longer list. A bounded successor can reproduce the first thousand cases using both modular residues and independent arbitrary-precision factorial arithmetic, then check the original printed statement. The current manuscript records the finite evidence without treating it as an asymptotic proof.
