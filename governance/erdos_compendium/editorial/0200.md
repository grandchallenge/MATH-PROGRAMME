## The question in plain language

Let $L(N)$ be the largest integer $k$ for which some **nonconstant $k$ term arithmetic progression** of primes lies entirely within $\{1,\ldots,N\}$:

$$
p,\ p+d,\ldots,p+(k-1)d,\qquad d\ge1.
$$

Erdős and Graham asked whether this length is **sublogarithmic**:

$$
L(N)=o(\log N).
$$

The logarithm is natural. This is a precise asymptotic limit: $L(N)/\log N\to0$, not merely a statement that the ratio stays bounded.

## A small example

The primes $5,11,17,23$ form a four-term arithmetic progression with common difference six. Thus $L(23)\ge4$. For sufficiently long prime progressions, the common difference must be divisible by many small primes; otherwise one term in the progression is divisible by a small prime and must equal that prime. This elementary modular obstruction is the basis of the known logarithmic upper bound.

A finite computation finding a long progression does **not** refute the conjecture: the question concerns the ratio $L(N)/\log N$ at arbitrarily large scales, not unusually large finite values.

## What is known

**OPEN.** The maintained [Erdős #200](https://www.erdosproblems.com/200) records the classical prime-number-theorem consequence

$$
L(N)\le(1+o(1))\log N.
$$

This rules out growth faster than $\log N$ but leaves the central question whether the ratio tends to zero. The difference between the multiplicative constant one and a term that tends to zero is the entire remaining challenge.

Green and Tao (2008) proved that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. This guarantees that $L(N)\to\infty$, but it gives no matching growth rate as a function of the largest prime $N$ and hence does not contradict the proposed $o(\log N)$ bound. Results on the longest progression inside **arbitrary dense subsets** of integers are not directly estimates for the sparse set of primes.

The source cites Erdős and Graham's original 1979 and 1980 questions. No inspected primary source proves or disproves the desired little-oh statement.

## What remains

A bounded task enumerates primes through $N=100000$, finds $L(N)$ at selected cutoffs using exact modular arithmetic, and returns one maximal progression witness and a separate exhaustive maximality certificate for each tested cutoff. Acceptance: reproduce $5,11,17,23$, handle short progressions whose first prime divides the step separately, and report both $L(N)$ and $L(N)/\log N$ without extrapolating a limit from finite points.

A subsequent analytic task should isolate the exact small-prime divisibility lemma used in the PNT upper bound, including the exceptional small initial prime, and state which additional uniform estimate would be needed to achieve little-oh.
