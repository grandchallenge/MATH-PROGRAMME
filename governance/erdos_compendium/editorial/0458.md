## The question in plain language

Let $L(n)$ be the least common multiple of the integers from $1$ through $n$. It is the smallest positive integer divisible by every one of those integers. Let $p_k$ be the $k$‑th prime, starting with $p_1=2$. Erdős and Graham asked in 1980 whether the inequality

$$
L(p_{k+1}-1)<p_kL(p_k)
$$

holds for **every** $k\geq1$. The right side allows the least common multiple to grow by a factor smaller than the previous prime while one moves from one prime to just before the next.

The original authors considered the statement highly plausible but warned that a proof was difficult. The difficulty comes from composite numbers between consecutive primes that are **prime powers**, such as $8=2^3$ or $9=3^2$. Each time the interval crosses a new prime power, the least common multiple acquires an additional factor. Two or more such factors in one prime gap could make the increase unexpectedly large.

## A small example

The consecutive primes $7$ and $11$ enclose two proper prime powers, $8=2^3$ and $9=3^2$. The least common multiple changes by a factor $2\cdot3=6$:

$$
L(7)=420,\qquad L(10)=2520,\qquad\frac{L(10)}{L(7)}=6<7.
$$

So the conjectured inequality holds at this unusually crowded gap, but only narrowly. Other finite gaps containing multiple prime powers include $(23,29)$, where the product is $15$, and $(113,127)$, where it is $55$. Each is smaller than its preceding prime. These examples show why prime powers matter, but they cannot prove a universal statement.

## What is known

The key reduction is an **exact identity**, not an approximation. For a prime $q$, the exponent of $q$ in $L(n)$ is the largest integer $a$ for which $q^a\leq n$. Hence

$$
L(n)=\prod_{q\text{ prime}}q^{\max\{a\geq0:q^a\leq n\}}.
$$

For consecutive primes $p<r$, there are no primes strictly between them. Only **proper** prime powers $q^a$ with $a\geq2$ can change the least common multiple between $p$ and $r-1$. Thus

$$
\frac{L(r-1)}{L(p)}=\prod_{\substack{p<q^a<r\\q\text{ prime},\ a\geq2}}q.
$$

The product counts every prime power occurrence. In fact, one prime base cannot occur twice in a single prime gap: by Bertrand's theorem $r<2p$, whereas two successive powers of the same base $q$ differ multiplicatively by at least $2$. The desired inequality is therefore **equivalent** to saying that the product of the distinct prime bases appearing in the gap is smaller than $p$.

This also clarifies the word **falsifiable**. The universal conjecture has the form

$$
\forall k\geq1,\quad R(k),
$$

where $R(k)$ is an effectively decidable integer inequality. A single explicit $k$ for which $R(k)$ fails would refute the whole conjecture. In the standard arithmetical hierarchy, this is a $\Pi^0_1$ assertion: a universally quantified computable condition. Each instance can be decided by finite arithmetic, but checking any finite number of instances does **not** decide the universal assertion. No independence result from Peano arithmetic, nor any proof or disproof of the universal assertion, is established by this classification.

The primary 1980 text identifies two potential obstructions: several prime squares in one gap and complications caused by small primes. A bound on prime gaps strong enough to rule out the former would be related to difficult questions of Legendre type, but would not automatically dispose of the latter. The maintained catalogue therefore reports **FALSIFIABLE**, meaning open with a finite-counterexample route to disproof, not false or proved.

## GCL's current state

GCL read the original Erdős–Graham statement on printed page 91 of the 1980 volume and independently reconstructed the exact prime-power product identity. A deterministic sieve and enumeration checked all **9,591 consecutive-prime gaps ending at or below 99,991**, finding no failure. Exactly five gaps below $100,000$ contain multiple proper prime powers: $(7,11)$, $(23,29)$, $(113,127)$, $(2179,2203)$ and $(32749,32771)$, with respective products $6,15,55,39,362$.
