## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $n$, look at the number $n! = 1\cdot2\cdot\ldots\cdot n$. We may split it into a product of positive integer factors, all no larger than $n^2$. Let $A(n)$ be the smallest possible number of factors. Reordering factors never changes a product, so we may write them in nondecreasing order:

$$
n! = a_1a_2\cdots a_t,\qquad 1\leq a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_t\leq n^2.
$$

Erdős and Graham ask whether, as $n$ grows, the optimal number of factors is

$$
A(n)=\frac n2-\frac{n}{2\log n}+o\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right).
$$

The leading term has a simple explanation: a factor is at most $n^2$, while $n!$ has logarithm approximately $n\log n-n$. Thus one needs about half as many factors as the analogous problem with factors bounded by $n$. The negative correction measures the saving obtained by packing factors efficiently rather than using $n$ repeatedly.

The original source uses a slightly broader parameterization: it first allows $a_k\leq n f(n)$ and then asks for the case $f(n)=n$. That is exactly the $n^2$ bound above. The source passage does not impose an order on the factors; the displayed monotone version is an equivalent normalization.

## A small example

Take $n=5$. Then $5!=120$ and the permitted factor bound is $5^2=25$. The exact factorization

$$
120=5\cdot24
$$

has two ordered factors, both at most $25$. One factor cannot work, because the only one-factor representation is $120$ itself, which exceeds $25$. Therefore the finite value is exactly $A(5)=2$.

This calculation was checked with integer arithmetic: $5!=120$, $5\cdot24=120$, $5\leq24\leq25$, and $120>25$. It illustrates the bounded-factor optimization and verifies one instance of the definition. It says nothing about the asymptotic formula: a statement involving $o(n/\log n)$ concerns the behavior of all sufficiently large $n$, not a short table of values.

## What is known

The primary historical source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory*, L’Enseignement Mathématique, Monographie 28 (1980). In the scanned copy, the relevant discussion is on printed pages 75–76: page 75 records the already-known $a_k\leq n$ estimate, and page 76 asks whether relaxing the limit to $n^2$ gives the displayed half-sized asymptotic. The source is available as a [scanned copy](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf); the retrieved PDF has SHA-256 `0cbf0c32f0ab1e1c71db5121a88bac905bf976c4a6ab6bb6d7d9cf9ddd184ed3`.

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/392), accessed 2026-10-13, reproduces the normalized question, cites `[ErGr80,p.75]`, and reports `PROVED (LEAN)`. Its linked discussion records a constructive route: first build a balanced factorization with factors at most $n$ and about $n-n/\log n$ terms, then pair consecutive factors. Pairing preserves the product and changes a factor bound of $n$ into one of $n^2$, while halving the count up to a negligible rounding error. The matching lower scale follows from the size bound and Stirling’s estimate. These are source-reported mathematical claims here, not a new certification by this article.

A relevant modern source is [Alexeev, Conway, Rosenfeld, Sutherland, Tao, Uhr, and Ventullo, “Decomposing a factorial into large factors,” arXiv:2503.20170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20170), revised version 4 dated 2026-04-03. Its abstract treats a closely related factorial factorization problem and states that its methods answer a question of Erdős and Graham. The current [PNT+ source file](https://github.com/AlexKontorovich/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd/blob/c39a751132c88b6e8080b74c74023fd95b3d8be0/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd/IEANTN/Erdos392.lean) contains `Solution_1` and `Solution_2`, including the pairing interface with bound $n^2$, at repository commit `c39a751132c88b6e8080b74c74023fd95b3d8be0` and Git blob `61ffe123a7c8e292532d60d89faa666ce6484d6d`. Text inspection found no `sorry` in that file; no Lean build or replay was performed.
