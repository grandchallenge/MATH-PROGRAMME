## The question in plain language

For a positive integer $N$, look at the numbers $1,2,\ldots,N$. We seek the largest set $A$ of these numbers with a strong avoidance property: no selection of distinct members of $A$ has reciprocals adding to $1$. Write this maximum size as $A(N)$. The original Erdős–Graham question asks how large $A(N)$ can be compared with $N$.

The quantifiers matter. The forbidden selection is any finite subset $S$ of $A$, including a one-element subset, and the equation is an exact rational equation, not an approximation. Thus a set containing $1$ is immediately disallowed, while a set may have a large total reciprocal sum provided no sub-selection sums exactly to $1$.

The early guess was that almost every integer could be retained: $A(N)=(1+o(1))N$. Croot showed that this is false. The later theorem of Liu and Sawhney identifies the correct density:

$$
A(N)=(1-1/e+o(1))N.
$$

Here $e$ is the base of the natural logarithm, so the limiting proportion is about $0.632$. This is an asymptotic statement: it describes the ratio as $N$ grows, not an exact formula for any particular small $N$.

## A small example

An exact finite check illustrates the definition without pretending to address the asymptotic theorem. For $N=5$, the set $\{2,3,4,5\}$ is admissible. Exhaustively checking its nonempty subcollections gives the reciprocal sums

$$
1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, 5/6, 3/4, 7/10, 13/20, 7/12, 8/15, 9/20, 47/60, 19/20, 23/30, 77/60,
$$

none of which equals $1$. The only five-element subset of $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ contains $1$, so it is not admissible. An exact enumeration of all subsets therefore gives $A(5)=4$, with $\{2,3,4,5\}$ a maximizer. A reproducible check using rational arithmetic is:

    from fractions import Fraction
    from itertools import combinations

    def good(B):
        return all(sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in S), Fraction(0)) != 1
                   for k in range(1, len(B) + 1)
                   for S in combinations(B, k))

    valid = [B for k in range(6)
             for B in combinations(range(1, 6), k) if good(B)]
    print(max(map(len, valid)), [B for B in valid if len(B) == 4])

The actual output is 4 [(2, 3, 4, 5)]. This verifies one small value by finite exact calculation. It does not prove the limiting density, establish the analytic estimates in the research paper, or validate any formal proof.

## What is known

The primary modern source is Yang P. Liu and Mehtaab Sawhney, “On further questions regarding unit fractions,” arXiv:2404.07113v1, submitted 10 April 2024. Their Theorem 1.3 states that for every fixed $ε$ with $0<ε<1/2$, and for all sufficiently large $N$ depending on $ε$, every $A⊆[1,N]$ satisfying $|A|≥(1−1/e+ε)N$ contains a subset $A'$ whose reciprocal sum is $1$. This is the upper bound on the avoidance maximum. Their discussion immediately gives the matching obstruction: for fixed $γ>0$, the tail beginning around $(1/e+γ)N$ has reciprocal sum below $1$, so it itself contains no forbidden sub-sum. Together these bounds yield the stated asymptotic.

The paper’s proof is not a numerical search. It uses Fourier analysis on reciprocal sums, smooth-number decompositions, and an inverse argument controlling modular residues. The paper explains that Theorem 1.3 is obtained from its Proposition 5.2 after restricting to a suitable range and removing numbers with unsuitable smoothness or too many prime factors. This is the external mathematical proof evidence used here; it is not a GCL certification.

The historical source is Erdős and Graham, “Old and new problems and results in combinatorial number theory” (1980), where the question appears in the unit-fraction problems. Croot’s “On a coloring conjecture about unit fractions,” Annals of Mathematics 157 (2003), 545–556, supplies the earlier obstruction to density tending to $1$. These references explain the provenance of the question and the first qualitative disproof, while Liu–Sawhney supplies the sharp asymptotic resolution.
