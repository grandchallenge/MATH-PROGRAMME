## The question in plain language

Colour the integers with finitely many colours. Erdős Problem #303 asks whether one colour must contain three different nonzero integers $a$, $b$, and $c$ satisfying

$$
\frac{1}{a}=\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c}.
$$

“Monochromatic” means that the same colour is assigned to all three numbers. The maintained Erdős Problems page uses the word “integers”, while the original Brown–Rödl paper states its result for the positive integers. The positive-integer theorem is the important primary formulation: a colouring of all integers restricts to a finite colouring of the positive integers, so a positive witness is also an integer witness. Distinctness matters: allowing $b=c$, or allowing a zero denominator, would describe a different question.

The equation is a small unit-fraction equation. The problem is not asking for one especially convenient triple. It asks for a triple in every finite colouring, no matter how the colours have been assigned.

## A small example

Take the two-colouring of the integers given by parity. The three distinct denominators $4$, $6$, and $12$ all have the same colour, and exact fraction arithmetic gives

$$
\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{12}=\frac{2}{12}+\frac{1}{12}=\frac{3}{12}=\frac{1}{4}.
$$

Thus $(a,b,c)=(4,6,12)$ is a monochromatic solution for this particular colouring. The calculation was checked with rational arithmetic, so it does not rely on decimal approximations. It establishes only one example for one colouring; it does not establish the universal assertion over all finite colourings. In particular, a finite search or a convenient colouring cannot replace the theorem’s quantifiers.

## What is known

The primary source is T. C. Brown and V. Rödl, “Monochromatic solutions to equations with unit fractions”, *Bulletin of the Australian Mathematical Society* 43 (1991), 387–392. In the introduction on printed page 387, the authors describe the unit-fraction colouring question and then state a stronger result. Their Theorem 2.1, on printed page 389, converts a finite-colouring result for homogeneous integer equations into a finite-colouring result after replacing variables by reciprocals. Corollary 2.2, on printed page 390, says that for every finite colouring, every $n\ge 2$, and $1\le a\le n$, there are distinct monochromatic positive integers solving $a/x_0=1/x_1+\cdots+1/x_n$. Taking $a=1$ and $n=2$ gives exactly the three-variable equation in #303.

This is an external published proof, not a conjectural report. The paper’s proof uses a compactness step and a least-common-multiple substitution; the source audit checked the five-page article, including the theorem and corollary pages. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/303) also records the colouring version as true and cites Brown–Rödl. The live page was last edited 28 December 2025. The exact source semantics should still be kept visible: the paper says positive integers, whereas the website’s short wording says integers.

The quantifier “finite colouring” means that the number of colours is fixed and finite, but the colouring itself is arbitrary. Brown–Rödl’s statement is correspondingly stronger than checking a few named colourings: for every fixed number of colours, every assignment of those colours to the positive integers has a monochromatic witness. Their general reciprocal-substitution theorem explains why a homogeneous partition-regular equation can yield a unit-fraction equation. For #303, only the specialization $n=2$ is needed; the paper actually supplies the whole family with $n\ge2$.
