## The question in plain language

Stand at each point of a finite planar configuration and count how many **different distances** that point has to the other points. Some points may have the same count, even when their actual distances differ.

For a set \(X\) of \(n\) distinct points define

$$
R_X(p)=\#\{|p-q|:q\in X,\ q\ne p\},\qquad
v(X)=\#\{R_X(p):p\in X\},
$$

and let

$$
g(n)=\max_{X\subset\mathbb R^2,\ |X|=n}v(X).
$$

[Erdős Problem #653](https://www.erdosproblems.com/653) asks whether \(g(n)\geq(1-o(1))n\). In plain language: for every small positive proportion \(\varepsilon\), and every sufficiently large \(n\), can some \(n\)-point arrangement exhibit at least \((1-\varepsilon)n\) **different counts of distinct distances**? This is a question about diversity among local counts, not about the total number of pairwise distances.

**Status discrepancy, observed 13 October 2026:** the maintained Erdős catalogue still says **OPEN**, while an external service, Conjectures.io, reports an affirmative Lean proof certified in September 2026. No GCL proof replay has occurred, and neither description can be silently substituted for the other.

## A small example

Let

$$
X=\{(-1,0),(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,0)\}.
$$

Comparing squared distances suffices: two nonnegative distances agree if and only if their squares agree. We obtain:

| Centre | Squared distances to other points | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \((-1,0)\) | \(\{1,2,4,9\}\) | 4 |
| \((0,0)\) | \(\{1,4\}\) | 2 |
| \((1,0)\) | \(\{1,2,4\}\) | 3 |
| \((0,1)\) | \(\{1,2,5\}\) | 3 |
| \((2,0)\) | \(\{1,4,5,9\}\) | 4 |

Thus the pinned-distance count vector is \((4,2,3,3,4)\); its value spectrum is \(\{2,3,4\}\). Hence

$$
v(X)=3,\qquad g(5)\geq 3.
$$

The calculation was independently recomputed using exact integer arithmetic. A finite example illustrates the definition but does not resolve the asymptotic question.

## What is known

The [maintained problem page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/653) attributes historical lower estimates of \(g(n)>3n/8\) to Erdős–Fishburn and \(g(n)>7n/10\) to Csizmadia–Ismailescu; it also reports an upper bound \(g(n)<n-cn^{2/3}\) for an absolute \(c>0\). These are **catalogue-reported results**, not independently reproduced proofs: the original papers' relevant theorem pages have not yet been inspected here. Note that a sublinear-defect upper bound is logically compatible with the proposed lower bound.

A [Conjectures.io result for #653](https://conjectures.io/results/257ce6dd-e3e5-4169-9d7e-2811092977ca) describes an affirmative proof submitted by gus, checked in Lean on 15 September 2026, reviewed on 16 September and externally **certified on 17 September**. Its [public source viewer](https://conjectures.io/results/257ce6dd-e3e5-4169-9d7e-2811092977ca/solution) displays the beginning of a reported 28,608-line Lean proof. The external report says its target matches the task and its permitted axioms. This is substantial third-party evidence, but GCL has **not** independently compiled the 28,608-line file, examined its transitive dependencies or mathematically adjudicated the result.

The formal semantics require special attention. GCL's [pinned Formal Conjectures file](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/85f863718beeec7b58a3a1926ee92e3472bc2020/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/653.lean) has SHA-1 c46288f94af438eec9d8b977bfbd8343c597937f and states an eventual asymptotic inequality for maximalDistinctDistancesFrom. It is marked research/open and still contains a sorry proof hole. It is a conjectural source interface, not an accepted proof.

A helper definition counts the zero distance from a point to itself. The original problem excludes that distance. On a set of *distinct* points, this shifts every individual count by exactly one:

$$
R_X^{\mathrm{with\ zero}}(p)=R_X(p)+1.
$$

Such a uniform shift preserves how many different count values occur. This removes one elementary concern about the formalization but **does not establish complete semantic equivalence** of the 2026 submitted theorem and the GCL-pinned file.
