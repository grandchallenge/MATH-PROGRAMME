## The question in plain language

If a positive integer $N$ factors as $N=ab$, the two complementary factors differ by $|a-b|$. Collect the **distinct** possible differences in the *factor-difference set*

$$
D(N)=\{|a-b|:a,b\in\mathbb N_{>0},\ ab=N\}.
$$

Erdős and Rosenfeld asked: for every positive integer $k$, can one find **$k$ distinct increasing integers** $N_1<\cdots<N_k$ whose factor-difference sets all share at least $k$ distinct values?

This is a question about *simultaneously shared factor differences*, not the simpler number of divisors of one integer. Having three integers with four common differences does not prove the case $k=4$, because that case requires **four** integers.

## A small example

An exact integer computation gives

$$
D(79200)\cap D(227205)\cap D(1258560)
=\{36,468,692,1028\}.
$$

The three distinct integers therefore share four factor differences and in particular give a finite witness for the already proved case $k=3$. For each displayed difference $d$ and each of the three numbers $N$, the verification program records a pair of positive factors $a,b$ satisfying $ab=N$ and $b-a=d$.

A public April 2026 reply to the example accidentally displays $1029$ instead of $1028$. Direct factorization verifies **1028**. The same forum note also supplies five positive integers $z=330,870,2445,4155,10482$ for which each of $z^2+756000$, $z^2+15971200$ and $z^2+45130176$ is a square. This is an *anchored three-offset* construction, not a proof that five different $N_i$ have five common factor differences.

## What is known

The original primary article is Paul Erdős and Moshe Rosenfeld, [*The factor-difference set of integers*](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/acta-arithmetica/all/79/4/109554/the-factor-difference-set-of-integers), *Acta Arithmetica* **79** (1997), pp.353–359, DOI 10.4064/aa-79-4-353-359. Its introduction states the exact intersection problem and the authors prove the case $k=2$.

Jorge Jiménez-Urroz, [*A Note on a Conjecture of Erdős and Rosenfeld*](https://doi.org/10.1006/jnth.1999.2407), *Journal of Number Theory* **78** (1999), pp.140–143, proved $k=3$. Andrew Bremner, [*On a problem of Erdős related to common factor differences*](https://doi.org/10.1142/S1793042119500581), *International Journal of Number Theory* **15** (2019), pp.1059–1068, proved $k=4$ using elliptic-curve methods.

The maintained [Erdős Problem #885](https://www.erdosproblems.com/885) remains **OPEN** for general $k$; an April 2026 forum computation concerns special finite configurations only. The existence of a protected *Formal Conjectures statement* for #885 is not a formal proof.

## GCL's current state

There is an exact and useful reformulation. For a proposed nonnegative factor difference $d$, positive integers $a,b$ satisfying $ab=N$ and $b-a=d$ exist if and only if

$$
d^2+4N=s^2
$$

for a positive integer $s$ with $s\equiv d\pmod2$, in which case

$$
a=\frac{s-d}{2},\qquad b=\frac{s+d}{2}.
$$

Conversely, every factor pair gives $s=a+b$ and the identity above. The parity requirement prevents a false positive when translating a square test into integer factors.

The reproducible fixture governance/erdos_compendium/research/EC-BULK-006/finite_885_886.py independently enumerates all factor pairs of the three example integers, checks every common difference against the square-and-parity certificate, and verifies the equivalence for every $1\le N\le2000$. It also verifies all fifteen square identities in the five-point, three-offset example.

These computations establish the stated finite facts but not an unbounded family for arbitrary $k$. A July 2026 independent exploratory report explicitly records failed or saturated attempts at $k=5$ rather than a solution; no unsolved case is promoted on the basis of finite exploration.

## What remains

The immediate bounded successor is a source-locked **factor-difference equivalence lemma**: for all positive integers $N$ and nonnegative integers $d$, prove membership in $D(N)$ equivalent to the integer square, parity and positive-factor conditions above. Acceptance requires a complete paper proof or a pinned Lean theorem with no placeholders, an actual axiom report, and replay of the exact three-integer four-difference certificate.

A second bounded research task is to reconstruct Bremner's $k=4$ construction with all four factor-difference sets verified and then seek genuinely new mechanisms for $k=5$. A purported witness must contain **five distinct integers and five common differences**; a five-point anchored three-offset example is not enough.
