## The question in plain language

Can one choose an infinite set of positive integers $A$ that is quite large on the **scale of the primes**, yet has infinitely many translations that turn every selected smaller element into a prime? Erdős Problem 428 asks for an $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ such that, for infinitely many positive integers $n$,

$$
n-a\ \text{is prime for every }a\in A\text{ with }0<a<n,
$$

and at the same time

$$
\liminf_{x\to\infty}
\frac{|A\cap[1,x]|}{\pi(x)}>0,
$$

where $\pi(x)$ is the number of primes not exceeding $x$.

The two conditions are jointly demanding. Each chosen $n$ must work simultaneously for **all** the previously selected elements, not only for finitely many convenient ones. And the set $A$ cannot be arbitrarily sparse: it must contain at least a fixed positive multiple of $\pi(x)$ elements up to **every sufficiently large** $x$. This is density relative to the prime-counting function, not ordinary natural density relative to $x$.

The use of a lower limit is also essential. A positive $\limsup$ permits the ratio to be large on occasional subsequences and tiny in between. A positive $\liminf$ prohibits those persistent troughs.

## A small example

Let $A=\{1,3,5\}$ and take $n=8$. Then

$$
8-A=\{7,5,3\},
$$

and every displayed integer is prime. This is a genuine finite witness of the simultaneous-primality condition at one starting point. It can be checked exactly by trial division: $3,5,7$ have no divisors other than $1$ and themselves.

However, $A$ is finite, and therefore

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,x]|}{\pi(x)}
=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac3{\pi(x)}=0.
$$

So this example **cannot** solve the problem. The same issue affects any finite collection of impressive primality witnesses: a finite set can never satisfy the required positive relative lower density.

For a finite candidate set, one can enumerate integer translates $n$ and test every required $n-a$ for primality, but the problem asks for one fixed infinite $A$ and infinitely many suitable $n$. An experiment confined to $n\leq N$ cannot establish either infinite quantifier. The exact placement of $a<n$ matters too: when $a\geq n$, the corresponding difference is not required to be prime, and including it would change the problem.

## What is known

The question comes from Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), the original source denoted [ErGr80] by the maintained catalogue. It links the theory of prime constellations with an additional, difficult requirement of consistent positive relative density across all large scales.

The maintained [Erdős Problems #428](https://www.erdosproblems.com/428) page reports the exact problem as **OPEN**. It credits Erdős and Graham with a conditional partial result: assuming the **prime $k$-tuple conjecture**, they could obtain such a construction if the density requirement were weakened by replacing $\liminf$ with $\limsup$. This is an important distinction in two directions. The prime $k$-tuple conjecture is not an established general theorem, and the weaker positive-upper-limit assertion does not imply the desired positive-lower-limit condition.

To see why the limit distinction is logical rather than cosmetic, imagine a nonnegative sequence of ratios that repeatedly takes values near $1$ and $0$. Such a sequence has positive limsup but zero liminf. Establishing good density at selected scales leaves open whether the set becomes too sparse in the gaps between those scales. The Erdős problem demands that these gaps remain controlled while infinitely many prime-compatible translates are preserved.

Other research on admissible prime tuples or prime-rich patterns can offer possible methods, but no named external theorem was verified here as settling the full combination of requirements. A finite admissible pattern, even when it has arbitrarily many prime translations conditionally or unconditionally, is not yet a globally dense infinite set with the required synchronization.
