## The question in plain language

Let $A$ be a set of nonnegative integers that becomes rarer and rarer: its natural density is zero. We ask whether there must always be a set $B$ whose pairwise sums cover $A$, while $B$ is exceptionally sparse. Writing

$$
B(N)=\lvert B\cap\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\rvert,
$$

the proposed sparsity is $B(N)=o(\sqrt N)$. In words, after dividing by $\sqrt N$, the ratio should tend to zero. The maintained problem page writes the counting interval as $\{1,\ldots,N\}$; the 1977 source and the formal statement use the nonnegative-integer convention. This endpoint choice changes at most one counted element and does not change a little $o$ assertion, but it should be stated.

The sumset $B+B$ consists of all $b+b'$ with $b,b'\in B$. Problem 333 asks whether every density-zero $A$ can be covered by such sums from a basis $B$ that is much thinner than the square-root scale. The answer reported by the source record is no: there is a density-zero set that forces every covering set $B$ to have square-root-sized initial segments infinitely often.

## A small example

The finite version makes the issue concrete. Take $A=\{1,3,5,7\}$. An exhaustive exact check of all subsets $B\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,7\}$ of size at most three finds that none has $A\subseteq B+B$. The four-element set

$$
B=\{0,1,2,5\}
$$

does work: its pairwise sums include $1=0+1$, $3=1+2$, $5=0+5$, and $7=2+5$. Thus this finite example has minimum basis size four. The enumeration is finite and exact: any summand of a number at most seven is itself at most seven, so no larger element of a nonnegative $B$ can help.

This calculation illustrates, but does not prove, the Erdős question. A finite set can have a minimum basis, whereas the problem asks for one infinite $A$, density zero, for which every possible $B$ fails the asymptotic condition. The obstruction must therefore be repeated at unbounded scales, not merely observed once.

## What is known

The primary source is P. Erdős and D. J. Newman, “Bases for Sets of Integers,” *Journal of Number Theory* 9 (1977), 420–425, received 13 October 1976. They define a basis for a finite set $A$ as a set $B$ with every $a\in A$ expressible as $b+b'$, and write $m_A$ for the minimum size of such a basis. Their Theorem 2 states that most sets of type $(n,N)$—having $n$ elements and largest element $N$—satisfy

$$
m_A>\min\left(\frac{n}{\log N},\frac{\sqrt N}{2}\right).
$$

This is a finite, probabilistic counting theorem. It does not, on the printed page, state the infinite density-zero problem verbatim. The maintained Erdős Problems entry explains that Theorem 2 already supplies a negative answer by choosing increasingly large hard finite sets and gluing them into one sparse sequence. That block-gluing inference is mathematically plausible and is also sketched in the site's discussion, but this audit does not present it as a new proof or as a checked GCL theorem.

The same paper studies squares and proves that they behave differently: it gives nontrivial finite upper and lower bounds for bases of the first $n$ squares. That positive special case must not be confused with the general density-zero question. Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), is the source associated with the problem's later formulation.

## What remains

The remaining bounded mathematical task is to check the bridge from Theorem 2 to one infinite density-zero counterexample. It must specify the finite sets, their locations, their sizes, and the growth of successive scales. It must then prove both properties: the union has density zero, and any $B$ covering the union has $B(N)\geq c\sqrt N$ along an unbounded sequence of $N$. The nonnegative convention also needs an explicit observation that a representation of $a\leq N$ only uses summands at most $N$.

A formalization would additionally need to choose the exact density predicate, the interval endpoint convention, and the meaning of `answer(False)`.
