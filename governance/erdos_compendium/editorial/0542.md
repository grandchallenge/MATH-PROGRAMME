**Editorial status: UNACCEPTED_DRAFT.** Erdős Problem #542 is **SOLVED**, but its two questions have different answers. Schinzel and Szekeres proved the sharp reciprocal-sum bound $31/30$ in 1959 and disproved Erdős's proposed positive proportion of integers missed by certain divisibility coverings. **Source correction:** the modern catalogue reverses the divisibility direction in that second question. The original and modern statements are not equivalent.

## The question in plain language

Fix an integer $N\ge2$. Choose distinct integers $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ such that every two different members have a least common multiple greater than $N$:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)>N\qquad(a,b\in A,\ a\ne b).}
$$

This means that **no integer at most $N$ can be a multiple of two different members of $A$**. Erdős asked whether every such family satisfies the sharp reciprocal estimate

$$
\boxed{\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a\le\frac{31}{30}.}
$$

He also asked whether there must be at least $cN$ integers $m\le N$ which are **not divisible by any member of $A$**, where $c>0$ is an absolute constant. That is, he asked for at least $cN$ values of $m$ such that

$$
\forall a\in A,\quad a\nmid m.
$$

The first conjecture is **true**. The second is **false**.

### A source-fidelity warning

The [modern catalogue for #542](https://www.erdosproblems.com/542) instead describes integers $m$ that **do not divide any member of $A$**:

$$
\forall a\in A,\quad m\nmid a.
$$

The original [1973 Erdős article, printed p.135](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=19), clearly says that the integers $m$ are *not divisible by any of the $a$'s*. The original 1959 counterexample likewise concerns integers **not divisible by a member of $A$**. These statements reverse which number is the divisor. This article uses Erdős's **original formulation** and records the modern reversal as a source error; it does not silently treat the two as equivalent.

## A small example

Let $N=5$ and choose

$$
A=\{2,3,5\}.
$$

Its pairwise least common multiples are $6$, $10$ and $15$, all greater than five, so the family is admissible. The reciprocal sum is

$$
\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\boxed{\frac{31}{30}}.
$$

The sharp upper bound is therefore attained.

A second informative family is $A=\{3,4,5,7,11\}$ with $N=11$. Its smallest pairwise least common multiple is $12$, so it is admissible, and

$$
\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac17+\frac1{11}
=\frac{4699}{4620}>1,
$$

but still below $31/30$. The 1959 paper identifies this as another unusually large reciprocal sum, about $1.017099$.

There is a simple exact relationship to disjoint coverings. Write

$$
D_N(A)=\{m\in[1,N]: a\mid m\text{ for at least one }a\in A\}.
$$

For each $a$, there are precisely $\lfloor N/a\rfloor$ multiples of $a$ in $[1,N]$. Because the pairwise least common multiples exceed $N$, **these lists of multiples are disjoint**. Hence

$$
\boxed{|D_N(A)|=\sum_{a\in A}\left\lfloor\frac Na\right\rfloor\le N.}
$$

The uncovered set in the original second question is $[1,N]\setminus D_N(A)$. This identity supplies a bounded computational check, but does not by itself prove the sharp $31/30$ reciprocal inequality.

## What is known

### Schinzel and Szekeres's complete 1959 solution

Andrzej Schinzel and György Szekeres, *Sur un problème de M. Paul Erdős*, *Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum (Szeged)* **20** (1959), 221–229, proved the sharp result. Their [complete original nine-page article](https://acta.bibl.u-szeged.hu/13886/1/math_020_221-229.pdf) was retrieved and inspected directly.

**Theorem 1, printed p.222**, asserts

$$
\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a\le\frac{31}{30}
$$

for all admissible $A$, **with equality only** for $N=5$, $A=\{2,3,5\}$.

The proof uses carefully weighted counting of multiples rather than only the elementary inequality $|D_N(A)|\le N$. The authors construct auxiliary nonnegative weights for intervals according to how many multiples lie below $N$, and then verify the resulting inequalities. That construction is responsible for the sharp finite constant. Exhaustively checking small $N$ is informative but does not replace their proof for every $N$.

The same paper contains two further results. **Theorem 2** proves that for every $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$, admissible sets satisfy a reciprocal upper bound of approximately

$$
\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a<1.017262\ldots+\varepsilon.
$$

This numerical constant is **above one**, so it is not a proof of Erdős's stronger speculation that every sufficiently large family has sum at most $1+o(1)$.

**Theorem 3** supplies admissible families for arbitrarily large $N$ with reciprocal sum exceeding $1-\varepsilon$, for each fixed $\varepsilon>0$. Its proof, on the final pages of the original paper, constructs families $A_N$ whose multiples cover **all but $o(N)$** of the integers up to $N$:

$$
\boxed{
\left|\{m\le N:\ \forall a\in A_N,\ a\nmid m\}\right|=o(N).
}
$$

This is precisely why Erdős's original positive-proportion uncovered claim is false. The authors' construction is significantly subtler than the simple upper-half-interval example below.

### Why the modern reversed question is trivially false

Consider for any $N\ge2$ the set

$$
A_{\mathrm{high}}=\{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1,\ldots,N\}.
$$

Any two distinct elements exceed $N/2$. If they had a common multiple at most $N$, that multiple would have to equal each of them, an impossibility. Therefore $A_{\mathrm{high}}$ satisfies the original pairwise LCM condition.

Now take **any** $m\le N$ and choose its largest multiple at most $N$,

$$
a=m\left\lfloor\frac Nm\right\rfloor.
$$

Because $N-m<a\le N$, and because the case $m>N/2$ gives $a=m$, we have $a>N/2$ in every case. Hence $a\in A_{\mathrm{high}}$ and **$m\mid a$**.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\{m\le N:\ \forall a\in A_{\mathrm{high}},\ m\nmid a\}
=\varnothing.
}
$$

The modern reversed claim fails with **zero** uncovered numbers for every $N$, not merely with $o(N)$ of them. This elementary observation must not be attributed to Schinzel and Szekeres as their 1959 construction: their work answers the **opposite, original** divisibility question.

### Subsequent refinements and unresolved stronger statements

Yong-Gao Chen, *On a problem of P. Erdős*, *Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum (Szeged)* **62** (1996), 101–114, obtained the later bound recorded in the [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/542): for $N>172509$,

$$
\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a<
\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac17+\frac1{11}
=1.017099\ldots.
$$

The complete original Chen paper was not independently retrieved in this tranche, so its exact hypotheses beyond those recorded in the catalogue remain a primary-source follow-up.

Erdős also conjectured that $\{2,3,5\}$ and $\{3,4,5,7,11\}$ might be the only admissible sets with reciprocal sum strictly larger than one. This is a **separate stronger classification conjecture** in later literature, not a consequence of the 1959 solution. The cohort classification SOLVED refers to the two original yes/no questions, not an independent solution of every related strengthening.

## What remains

The principal original questions are solved. The immediate editorial correction is to amend the catalogue's second question from *integers $m$ that divide none of the $a$'s* to *integers $m$ divisible by none of the $a$'s*, preserving the actual original source. This tranche records the discrepancy but does **not** silently overwrite the protected source packet or externally maintained catalogue.

Independent examination of Chen's full 1996 refinement and the later classification conjecture would be separate undertakings, not reasons to mark the original two questions open.
