## The question in plain language

An **exact covering system** is a finite collection of congruence classes $a_i\bmod n_i$ such that each integer belongs to *exactly one* class. Erdős asked whether such a covering can have all moduli $n_i$ distinct. The answer is **no**, as proved independently by Mirsky and Newman and by Davenport and Rado. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/947) labels the result PROVED and reports a Lean formalization. The existence of such a report must be distinguished from a locally replayed GCL proof.

## A small example

*A valid covering illustrating the obstruction*

Consider the classes $0\bmod2$, $1\bmod4$, and $3\bmod4$. Every integer belongs to exactly one of them: an integer is even or odd, and every odd integer is either $1$ or $3$ modulo $4$. The system is exact, but its moduli are $2,4,4$, so they are **not distinct**.

The main theorem does not prohibit exact coverings. It prohibits an exact covering whose moduli are **all different**. The distinction is essential.

## Necessary density equation

A residue class modulo $n$ has natural density $1/n$. If finitely many classes partition the integers without overlaps or omissions, then

$$
\sum_{i=1}^k\frac1{n_i}=1.
$$

For the example above, $1/2+1/4+1/4=1$.

This reciprocal identity is necessary but **not sufficient** for the congruence classes to cover each integer exactly once: densities alone do not control overlaps. Nor does the identity by itself rule out distinct moduli. A complete proof of the Mirsky–Newman theorem needs additional structure.

## The generating-function mechanism

If the classes $a_i\bmod n_i$ cover every nonnegative integer exactly once, choose representatives $0\le a_i<n_i$. Summing their geometric series yields the identity of formal power series

$$
\sum_{i=1}^k \frac{z^{a_i}}{1-z^{n_i}}=\frac1{1-z}.
$$

This also holds as a rational-function identity. Suppose the moduli have a unique largest value $N>1$. Take $z$ to be a primitive $N$‐th root of unity. Only the denominator $1-z^N$ from the largest modulus vanishes: no smaller positive $n_i<N$ is divisible by $N$. Its numerator is nonzero. The left side therefore has a genuine pole, whereas the right side is finite because $z\ne1$, a contradiction.

Hence the **largest modulus must occur at least twice**, which is strictly stronger than merely saying some two moduli coincide. This argument proves the desired impossibility when all moduli are distinct. It applies to finite positive moduli and requires the exact-once hypothesis. Modulus $1$ is a trivial singleton covering; if other classes are present, exactness fails.

## What is known

The Mirsky–Newman and Davenport–Rado obstruction is classical. The discussion of #947 identifies stronger conclusions: Stein characterized a special nearly distinct case; Znám and Newman established multiplicity restrictions governed by the smallest prime factor of the largest modulus. These strengthen the theorem but are not needed for the elementary root-of-unity proof supplied above. A commenter reported a roughly 200-line Lean formalization of the power-series proof. Its source, pinned dependencies, theorem statement and kernel replay have **not** been independently verified in this GCL manuscript. [Discussion](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/947).
