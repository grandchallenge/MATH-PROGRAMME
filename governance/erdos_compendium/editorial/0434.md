## The question in plain language

A numerical semigroup is the collection of all nonnegative sums formed from specified positive integer denominations, with repetitions allowed. If the denominations have greatest common divisor $1$, every sufficiently large integer is attainable, but finitely many smaller positive integers may not be attainable. Such unattainable numbers are called **gaps**.

Erdős Problem 434 asks how to make the **number of gaps** as large as possible when we may choose exactly $k$ distinct denominations from $\{1,\dots,n\}$. The natural candidate is simply to use the $k$ largest allowable numbers:

$$
A_*=\{n-k+1,n-k+2,\ldots,n\}.
$$

The conjecture is that $A_*$ maximizes the number of nonrepresentable positive integers among all sets with $k$ elements $A\subseteq[1,n]$ with $\gcd(A)=1$. The assertion is now a **published theorem**, due to Géza Kiss.

This problem is closely related to #433, but the optimization target is **different**. Problem #433 maximizes the largest nonrepresentable integer, the Frobenius number. Problem #434 maximizes the **total count** of nonrepresentable integers, sometimes called the genus. Two sets may have the same largest gap but different numbers of gaps.

## A small example

Take $n=10$ and $k=3$. The proposed extremal set is $A_*=\{8,9,10\}$. Every representable positive integer is a sum of some number of copies of $8$, $9$ and $10$. The first reachable positive values are $8,9,10,16,17,18,19,20,\ldots$.

Exact enumeration finds precisely sixteen positive gaps:

$$
\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,11,12,13,14,15,21,22,23,31\}.
$$

The largest gap is $31$. Every integer from $32$ through $39$ can be formed: for example, $32=4\cdot8$, $33=3\cdot8+9$, $34=2\cdot8+2\cdot9$, $35=8+3\cdot9$, $36=4\cdot9$, $37=8+9+10+10$, $38=8+3\cdot10$ and $39=9+3\cdot10$. Adding copies of $8$ then constructs all higher integers, so the gap enumeration is complete.

An independent exact-integer search through all three-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,10\}$ whose gcd is one finds that **sixteen is indeed the maximum**. The set $\{5,9,10\}$ also has sixteen gaps, showing that the interval construction need **not be the unique maximizer**. The claim is that the indicated interval is *a* maximizer, not that no other set can attain the maximum.

The set $\{4,7\}$ provides a contrasting smaller fixture. Its Frobenius number is $17$, but its gaps are $1,2,3,5,6,9,10,13,17$, only nine in total. These examples reinforce the distinction between largest gap and gap count.

## What is known

The original problem is posed in P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), printed p. 86, available in the [historical scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf). It concerns extremal representations with repeated summands and an allowed set of denominations of fixed cardinality.

Géza Kiss proved the conjecture in [*On the extremal Frobenius problem in a new aspect*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/434), *Annales Universitatis Scientiarum Budapestinensis, Sectio Mathematica* 45 (2002), pp. 139–142. The maintained [Erdős Problems #434 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/434) attributes the result to Kiss and labels the problem **PROVED (LEAN)**. The mathematical answer is affirmative: for $2\leq k\leq n$, the interval of $k$ largest integers maximizes the number of gaps under the gcd-one condition.

The restriction $k\geq2$ is important. If $k=1$ and $n>1$, the proposed singleton $\{n\}$ has gcd $n\neq1$, making it ineligible; the admissible singleton $\{1\}$ has no gaps. Thus the theorem cannot be stated without specifying the valid cardinality range.

The maintained discussion also describes later work formalizing the theorem in Lean with input from the related Dixmier Frobenius bounds. An earlier formal proof route assumed a version of Kneser's addition theorem as an axiom; a May 2026 comment claims a later fully unconditional Lean development. These are meaningfully different trust assumptions. A readable theorem statement and a purported machine-checked proof must be compared at exact source revisions before claiming formal verification.
