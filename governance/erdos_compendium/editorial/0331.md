## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 331 asks whether two sufficiently large sets of natural numbers must have a repeated nonzero difference. Write

$$
A(x)=|A\cap\{1,\ldots,x\}|,\qquad B(x)=|B\cap\{1,\ldots,x\}|.
$$

The question assumes that, for some fixed positive constant, both $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ are at least a constant multiple of $x^{1/2}$ for all sufficiently large $x$. It then asks whether there must be infinitely many solutions of

$$
a_1-a_2=b_1-b_2\ne0,
$$

with $a_1,a_2\in A$ and $b_1,b_2\in B$. Equivalently, after rearranging, it asks whether the equation $a_1+b_2=a_2+b_1$ must have infinitely many nontrivial solutions.

The answer supplied by the primary source is no. The key idea is to reserve alternating binary digit positions for the two sets. The two sets can each have square-root-scale counting functions, while every nonnegative integer has a unique decomposition as one element from each set. Unique decomposition prevents the required nonzero difference from occurring.

## A small example

Let $A$ contain numbers whose binary expansion has nonzero digits only in even positions, and let $B$ contain numbers whose nonzero digits occur only in odd positions. Restricting attention to numbers below $32$ gives

$$
A\cap[0,31]=\{0,1,4,5,16,17,20,21\},
$$

and

$$
B\cap[0,31]=\{0,2,8,10\}.
$$

There are $8\cdot4=32$ ordered pairs $(a,b)$, and exact integer enumeration gives the set of sums

$$
\{a+b:a\in A\cap[0,31],\ b\in B\cap[0,31],\ a+b<32\}=\{0,1,\ldots,31\}.
$$

Each sum occurs once. For example, $13=5+8$, and no other listed pair produces $13$. The verification is finite and uses ordinary integer arithmetic; it is a check of the initial segment, not a proof about all natural numbers. The reason the pattern continues is structural: the two summands never use the same binary position, so their addition creates no carry, and the binary expansion of the sum recovers both summands uniquely.

The inclusion of $0$ in this illustration follows the primary paper's normalization. Removing $0$, or shifting the sets to positive integers, changes only finitely many values and does not change the asymptotic lower bounds or the nonzero-difference conclusion.

## What is known

The direct primary evidence is P. Erdős and Robert Freud, “On disjoint sets of differences,” *Journal of Number Theory* 18 (1984), 99–109. Its introduction quotes the original Erdős–Graham formulation from [ErGr80, p. 50] and then gives the binary construction. On the paper's printed pp. 100–101, it explains that the binary representations using even powers of two for $A$ and odd powers for $B$ have only trivial solutions to the difference equation. It also records the quantitative fact

$$
\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\min\{A(x),B(x)\}}{\sqrt{x}}=\frac1{\sqrt2},
$$

so the example meets the square-root growth requirement with room to spare. The paper therefore resolves the original question negatively, rather than merely giving a computation or heuristic.

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/331) reports the same construction, labels the problem “DISPROVED (LEAN),” and records a stronger-looking variant suggested by Ruzsa: replace lower bounds of square-root order by asymptotic relations $A(x)\sim c_Ax^{1/2}$ and $B(x)\sim c_Bx^{1/2}$ with positive constants. The original counterexample does not automatically settle that variant, and this article does not call the variant solved.

## What remains

The original lower-bound question has a primary counterexample. What remains is careful interface work: check the imported counting convention against the source's interval convention, document the harmless treatment of zero, and compare every formal quantifier with the printed statement. The Ruzsa asymptotic variant remains a separate mathematical question in the pinned formal file and on the maintained page.

A bounded successor could formalize the finite binary lemma: for a fixed digit length, prove by exact induction that alternating-position digit sets map bijectively to the corresponding initial interval under addition, then derive the finite counting formulas. Acceptance would require a checked theorem whose hypotheses and indexing are recorded; failure would be any collision, missing sum, or mismatch with the source normalization. This would improve reuse without pretending to settle the asymptotic variant.
