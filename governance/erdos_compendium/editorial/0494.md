## The question in plain language

Suppose you are given an unknown finite set $A$ of $n$ complex numbers. You are allowed to see all sums formed by choosing **exactly $k$ distinct members** of $A$. Can you recover $A$ uniquely?

The information you receive is a **multiset**, not just a set: if several different choices produce the same sum, that value appears as many times as it occurs. Define

$$
\mathcal S_k(A)=
\Bigl[\sum_{a\in U}a:\ U\subseteq A,\ |U|=k\Bigr],
$$

where square brackets denote retention of multiplicity. You are also told $n=|A|$ and $k$. The source-faithful repaired theorem says that for **each fixed $k>2$** there is an $N(k)$ such that

$$
|A|=|B|=n\ge N(k),\quad
\mathcal S_k(A)=\mathcal S_k(B)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=B.
$$

This was proved by Gordon, Fraenkel and Straus in 1962. The number $N(k)$ may depend on $k$. Nothing in this theorem says that all cardinalities, including $n=k$, work.

The wording requires unusually careful source control. Erdős's 1961 description mistakenly substitutes **products** for sums, whereas the Selfridge–Straus paper he cites explicitly studies sums. The literal product version has counterexamples even with distinct **nonzero** complex numbers. Treating that variant as equivalent to the proved sum theorem would be a mathematical error.

## A small example

When $n=k=3$, the only sum available is the sum of *all* three elements. For example,

$$
A=\{0,1,4\},\qquad B=\{0,2,3\}.
$$

Both sets have three distinct elements and the single three-member sum $5$. Thus $\mathcal S_3(A)=\mathcal S_3(B)=[5]$ while $A\ne B$. This is an exact counterexample to unrestricted uniqueness, not to the large‑$n$ theorem.

A more instructive obstruction occurs when $n=2k$. For $k=3$, take

$$
A=\{-7,-3,-2,1,5,6\},\qquad B=-A.
$$

The total sum of $A$ is zero and $A\ne-A$. Every three-element subset $U$ has a complementary three-element subset $A\setminus U$ whose sum is the negative of the sum of $U$. Hence the multiset of three-sums is invariant under negation:

$$
\mathcal S_3(A)=\mathcal S_3(-A)=\mathcal S_3(B).
$$

More generally, for any zero-total set of $2k$ elements, taking the complement of a $k$‑element subset negates its sum; choosing $A\ne-A$ yields an ambiguity.

**A counterexample to the product wording:** let $\zeta$ be a primitive sixth root of unity. Form the distinct four-element sets

$$
A=\{\zeta^0,\zeta^1,\zeta^2,\zeta^4\},\qquad
B=\{\zeta^0,\zeta^2,\zeta^3,\zeta^4\}.
$$

For products of three distinct members, the exponents add modulo $6$. Both sets yield the multiset of residue exponents $[0,1,3,5]$, although $A\ne B$. No input is zero. This exact witness exposes the historical product-versus-sum discrepancy.

## What is known

### The original 1958 paper: exact scope

J. L. Selfridge and E. G. Straus, *On the determination of numbers by their sums of a fixed order*, *Pacific Journal of Mathematics* **8** (1958), 847–856, is the cited primary paper. Its [original publisher PDF](https://msp.org/pjm/1958/8-4/pjm-v8-n4-p17-p.pdf) was directly inspected.

For $k=2$, their Theorems 1 and 2 (pp.847–848) show that the two-sum multiset uniquely determines the original set when $n$ is **not a power of two**, and that ambiguities occur at powers of two. More generally, their Theorem 4 and corollary (pp.852–853) prove uniqueness under nonvanishing conditions on coefficients in recursive power-sum identities. In particular, uniqueness holds if $n$ has a **prime divisor larger than $k$**.

The method is algebraic. Write $p_j(A)=\sum_{a\in A}a^j$ for power sums of the unknown numbers, and let $q_j$ be the sum of the $j$‑th powers of all $k$‑element subset sums. Expanding the $q_j$ expresses them in terms of $p_1,\ldots,p_j$. Whenever the coefficient of $p_j$ is nonzero, that power sum can be recovered from previous ones. Recovering $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ determines the monic polynomial whose roots are the original elements, via Newton's identities.

A source-detail matters: the 1958 paper's **Example 1**, printed p.853, leaves $n=27$ and $n=486$ *in doubt* when $k=3$ under its nonvanishing method. The current catalogue's more sweeping attribution that Selfridge and Straus already proved uniqueness for *all* $k=3$, $n>6$ is therefore **not verified by that 1958 passage alone**. This does not challenge the later general 1962 eventual-uniqueness theorem, but requires a citation-specific editorial distinction.

### The 1962 resolution

B. Gordon, A. S. Fraenkel and E. G. Straus, *On the determination of sets by the sets of sums of a certain order*, *Pacific Journal of Mathematics* **12** (1962), 187–196, proves the required eventual uniqueness. Their [original publisher PDF](https://msp.org/pjm/1962/12-1/pjm-v12-n1-p17-s.pdf), including the introductory theorem statement on p.187 and the Section 4 proof on pp.189–191, was directly inspected.

The authors define $F_k(n)$ as the largest number of distinct original $n$‑element multisets having identical $k$‑sum multisets. Their Section 4 theorem states

$$
\text{For each fixed }k>2,\quad F_k(n)=1
\quad\text{for all but finitely many }n.
$$

This yields the corrected statement for sufficiently large $n$. The proof studies when the power-sum recovery coefficients vanish. An exponential-polynomial equation imposes strong restrictions on possible exceptional integer sizes, and a Diophantine approximation theorem of Ridout rules out infinitely many such exceptions for fixed $k>2$.

This is a **published proof inspected at the original pages**, not an independently rederived proof of the imported Ridout theorem or a uniform explicit bound on $N(k)$. In particular, the authors work more generally with torsion-free Abelian groups and explain how to transfer the ambiguity count to positive integers. The complex numbers under addition are a compatible ambient group.
