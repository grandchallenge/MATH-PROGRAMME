**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #503 is **OPEN in general dimensions**. The largest isosceles-only sets in the plane and in three-dimensional space have known sizes, respectively **6** and **8**, but no exact formula for arbitrary dimension is established. The problem is not equivalent to Erdős #502, which restricts the total number of distances to two.

## The question in plain language

A triangle is **isosceles** if at least two of its sides have the same length. Given a dimension $d$, how many distinct points can we put in Euclidean space $\mathbb R^d$ if **every** choice of three distinct points must make an isosceles triangle?

Define

$$
I(d)=\max\bigl\{|A|:A\subseteq\mathbb R^d,\;
\text{every three distinct points of }A\text{ form an isosceles triangle}\bigr\}.
$$

Equivalently, among the three distances $|x-y|,|y-z|,|z-x|$ for any triple, at least two must be equal. The requirement is local to **each triangle**; different triangles may use different pairs of equal lengths. It does not mean that the entire point set has only two globally occurring distances.

The original Erdős 1961 question asks for the least number of points that *forces* a scalene triangle, which is $I(d)+1$. Erdős explicitly records that seven planar points always force such a triangle but that six can avoid it. In three dimensions the question was unresolved at the time. The maintained [Erdős #503 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/503) continues to classify the **general** dimension problem as **OPEN**.

## A small example

**Six points in the plane**

Draw a **regular pentagon and its centre**. Any three outer vertices form an isosceles triangle: among all chords joining pentagon vertices, there are only **two** possible lengths, so by the pigeonhole principle three edges must include two of equal length. Any triangle containing the centre has two equal radii.

Thus the six-point configuration satisfies Erdős's condition. L. M. Kelly established that **seven** planar points cannot all avoid scalene triples, so

$$
I(2)=6.
$$

A modern source by Zoltán Kovács explains the stronger classification that the regular pentagon plus centre is, up to similarity, the only six-point configuration with this property.

**A general high-dimensional example**

For $d\ge3$, take all vectors

$$
v_{ij}=e_i+e_j,\qquad 1\le i<j\le d+1,
$$

in $\mathbb R^{d+1}$. Each has two coordinates equal to one and the others zero. The $\binom{d+1}{2}$ vectors all lie in the affine hyperplane whose coordinate sum is $2$, an ordinary Euclidean space of dimension $d$.

Their squared pairwise distances are **$2$ or $4$**. We can add the centroid

$$
c=\left(\frac2{d+1},\ldots,\frac2{d+1}\right).
$$

Every original vector has the *same* squared distance to $c$:

$$
\|v_{ij}-c\|^2=2-\frac4{d+1}.
$$

A triangle consisting entirely of original vectors is isosceles because its three squared distances come from only two values. A triangle with the centroid is isosceles because its two sides from $c$ are equal. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{I(d)\ge\binom{d+1}{2}+1\qquad(d\ge3).}
$$

This extra-centroid improvement was already observed by Desmond Weisenberg and recorded in the problem catalogue; it is not a new GCL result. Importantly, the entire construction has **three distinct global squared distances**, so it illustrates why the isosceles-only problem is different from the two-distance-set problem #502.

## What is known

### Original Erdős statement and the low-dimensional answers

In *Some unsolved problems* (1961), printed p.244 in Section III.1, Erdős asks for the minimum number of points in $d$‑space needed to force a scalene triangle. His original page has been [visually inspected](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=24). He states the planar threshold seven and says that the three-dimensional case was not then known.

The planar result was proved by L. M. Kelly in the published solution to Erdős's problem E735, *American Mathematical Monthly* (1947), pp.227–229. Its original proof pages were not retrieved for this audit. Zoltán Kovács, *A note on Erdős's mysterious remark*, [arXiv:2412.05190](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05190), presents an alternative algebraic approach to the planar upper bound and characterizes the regular-pentagon-plus-centre equality case. Its first page, abstract, problem statement and historical account were directly inspected; the full computer-algebra elimination was not independently replayed.

H. T. Croft, *9-point and 7-point configurations in 3-space*, *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society* (3) **12** (1962), 400–424, established the three-dimensional value

$$
I(3)=8.
$$

That full historical proof has not been independently obtained in this source audit. Both exact values appear in the current catalogue and the pinned Formal Conjectures statement as separately classified results.

### General upper and lower bounds

A. Blokhuis, *Few-distance sets* (1984), is cited for the general inequality

$$
I(d)\le\binom{d+2}{2}.
$$

The source is an original thesis that was **not** retrieved in full here. The inequality is a published historical result as reported in the problem catalogue, not a GCL proof or a bound derived merely from the two-distance theorem in #502.

Ryan Alweiss observed the weight-two coordinate-vector lower construction of size $\binom{d+1}{2}$. Desmond Weisenberg pointed out that its centroid can be added while maintaining the all-triangles-isosceles property, giving the strengthened lower bound proved elementarily above. Combining the published estimates, for $d\ge3$,

$$
\binom{d+1}{2}+1
\le I(d)\le\binom{d+2}{2}.
$$

The bounds are of the same quadratic order but have a gap of $d$. In particular, for $d=3$ the elementary centroid construction gives **seven**, whereas Croft's exact value is **eight**. This shows that the convenient vector construction is not generally extremal.

## GCL's current state

The authenticated [original #503 source return](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1774#issuecomment-6189285240) preserves the **OPEN** general status and records the original printed question, the low-dimensional history, the known general bounds and an exact finite construction.

The cohort's bounded regression test checks weight-two vertices and their centroid using rational squared distances, rather than numerical approximations to square roots. It tests the **all-triples** property, not just the global set of distinct distances, and it confirms that every vector and the centroid lie in the same $d$‑dimensional affine hyperplane.

These finite calculations verify the construction, which also has a universal elementary proof. They do not establish the upper bound or determine $I(d)$ in every dimension.

## What remains

The immediate reusable task is to verify that the centroid construction works for every dimension at least three and to produce exact tests for its first few instances. Separate source work should obtain and inspect Croft's 1962 proof of $I(3)=8$ and Blokhuis's 1984 upper-bound argument.

The genuine open challenge is an exact extremal theorem for $I(d)$ in arbitrary dimensions, not another numerical search that merely reproduces known lower witnesses. Any new candidate must be checked against the **local isosceles condition for every triple** and must not be mistaken for a theorem about configurations having only two possible distances.
