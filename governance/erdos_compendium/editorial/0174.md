## The question in plain language

A finite configuration $A\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is a **Euclidean Ramsey set** when, for every finite number $k$ of colours, some dimension $d=d(A,k)$ has this property: every colouring with $k$ colours of **all points** of $\mathbb R^d$ contains a monochromatic congruent copy of $A$.

Erdős #174 asks for a **classification** of such finite point sets. The embedding dimension may grow with the number of colours, but the distances within $A$ must remain unchanged. The property concerns colours of **points**, not graph edges.

## A small example

Every pair of distinct points is spherical: its two points lie on a circle with centre their midpoint. A singleton is trivially Ramsey. A set of three collinear distinct points, however, cannot lie on the surface of a sphere in Euclidean space with its original collinear distances, because a straight line meets a sphere in at most two points. Thus it fails the necessary condition discussed below.

The vertices of a regular simplex are a known Ramsey configuration. Mere visual symmetry in two dimensions is not the definition; the assertion must hold for every finite colouring after moving to some ambient $\mathbb R^d$.

## What is known

The exact classification is **OPEN**. Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Straus, [*Euclidean Ramsey theorems I*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/latex/174), *Journal of Combinatorial Theory A* (1973), proved the necessary **sphericity** condition: every Ramsey finite set lies on some sphere.

Graham conjectured that sphericity is also sufficient. Leader, Russell and Walters, [*Transitive sets in Euclidean Ramsey theory*](https://www.erdosproblems.com/174), proposed an alternative characterization: Ramsey sets are exactly the **subtransitive** ones, which embed in a higher-dimensional finite set on which the rotation group acts transitively.

Known examples include the vertices of rectangular boxes, nondegenerate simplices, trapezoids and regular polygons or polyhedra, with separate sources and proofs. Neither the necessary sphericity theorem nor these families settle **all** spherical configurations.

## What remains

A bounded task tests **sphericity** of finite rational-coordinate configurations by solving the linear equations for a sphere centre and checking the resulting squared radius exactly. Acceptance: a noncollinear triangle is spherical, a collinear triple of distinct points is not, and the test does not confuse **necessary** and **sufficient** conditions for Ramsey-ness. Stop before claiming that every spherical set is Ramsey.
