## The question in plain language

Take $n\ge2$ distinct points $P=\{p_1,\ldots,p_n\}\subset\mathbb R^2$. List the distinct positive distances between point pairs as

$$
d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_k,
$$

and let $f(d)$ be the number of **unordered pairs** $\{p_i,p_j\}$ with $\|p_i-p_j\|=d$. Erdős and Pach asked whether

$$
f(d_1)f(d_k)\le\left(\frac98+o(1)\right)n^2.
$$

**This problem is proved:** Adrian Dumitrescu established the stronger, quantitative upper bound

$$
f(d_1)f(d_k)\le\frac98n^2+O(n).
$$

The coefficient $9/8$ is asymptotically optimal. [Maintained Erdős Problem #957](https://www.erdosproblems.com/957); [published conference theorem](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2019.30).

## A small example

*An exact small example*

Let $P$ consist of the four corners of a unit square. The smallest interpoint distance is $d_1=1$, attained by the four sides; the largest is $d_k=\sqrt2$, attained by the two diagonals. Thus

$$
f(d_1)=4,\qquad f(d_k)=2,\qquad f(d_1)f(d_k)=8.
$$

The example makes the counting convention unambiguous. Ordered point pairs would double both multiplicities and quadruple the product, so they must not be substituted without adjusting the theorem. The finite example does not show sharpness of $9/8$.

## What is known: the proved sharp theorem

Dumitrescu's Theorem 1 in *A Product Inequality for Extreme Distances* (SoCG 2019; journal version in *Computational Geometry* **85**, 2019, article 101577) gives the exact asymptotic upper order above. Its abstract explicitly says it settles the Erdős–Pach conjecture of 1990. Both the [refereed conference publication](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2019.30) and the [journal publication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comgeo.2019.101577) identify the result.

For sharpness, the paper describes Makai's construction using points on a circular arc of $60^\circ$, a triangular-lattice cluster inside the arc, and a suitable center. On an asymptotic family it gives

$$
f(d_1)=\frac32n-O(\sqrt n),\qquad
f(d_k)=\frac34n,
$$

so

$$
f(d_1)f(d_k)=\frac98n^2-O(n^{3/2}).
$$

The lower-order term here is *different* from the theorem's upper error $O(n)$. The construction establishes only the optimality of the **leading constant**, not matching second-order errors.

A simple independent check of the leading arithmetic is

$$
\left(\frac32n\right)\left(\frac34n\right)=\frac98n^2.
$$

The geometry that realizes those multiplicities is part of the cited publication, not inferred from this multiplication.

## A proof-shape intuition and distinct remaining questions

A classical separate bound says the diameter occurs at most $n$ times, while the minimum distance occurs at most approximately $3n$ times. Multiplying these worst-case bounds gives roughly $3n^2$, far above $9n^2/8$. The hard theorem uses the geometry of planar configurations to show that having many *shortest* edges constrains how many *diameter* pairs are possible, and conversely. A proof must couple the two extremal edge graphs rather than estimate them independently.

The original Erdős–Pach paper also considered a different inequality of the form

$$
f(d_1)+f(d_k)\le 3n-c\sqrt n+o(\sqrt n)
$$

and asked for the best coefficient $c>0$, plus a hull-sensitive inequality involving $m$, the number of extreme points of the convex hull. **These are not the statement of #957**, and Dumitrescu's proved product theorem should not be misrepresented as a solution of every related sum or hull inequality.

For an odd regular $n$‐gon, each of its distinct chord lengths occurs exactly $n$ times. This demonstrates that multiple distance classes can simultaneously have multiplicity of linear order, although it does not approach the product-optimal leading constant $9/8$.
