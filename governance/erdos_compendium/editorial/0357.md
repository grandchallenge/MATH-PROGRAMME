## The question in plain language

Choose a strictly increasing list of positive integers

$$
1 \leq a_1 < a_2 < \cdots < a_k \leq n.
$$

Look at every nonempty consecutive block, such as $a_2+a_3$ or $a_1+a_2+a_3$. The requirement is that no two different blocks have the same sum. Let $f(n)$ be the largest possible length $k$. Erdős and Harzheim asked how quickly $f(n)$ can grow, especially whether

$$
f(n)=o(n).
$$

In plain terms, the question asks whether the proportion of the interval $[1,n]$ used by such a collision-free increasing list must tend to zero. It is important that the terms are strictly increasing and that the sums are over contiguous blocks. Dropping monotonicity produces a different extremal function, and merely asking for many distinct sums is weaker than asking for every block sum to be distinct.

The original GCL assignment is [issue #1627](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1627). Its source packet pins the catalogue snapshot and the formal declaration, but those pins are evidence for this audit, not a mathematical acceptance decision.

## A small example

The list $(1,2,4,8)$ lies in $[1,8]$. Enumerating its ten nonempty consecutive blocks gives

$$
1,3,7,15,2,6,14,4,12,8.
$$

These are pairwise distinct, so this exact calculation proves $f(8)\geq4$. It is easy to reproduce with integer addition: start a running total at each position, extend the endpoint one term at a time, and insert each total into a set; the set has size $10$, equal to the number $4\cdot5/2$ of blocks. The same exhaustive checker gives, for $n=1,\ldots,10$, the lower-and-upper maxima $1,2,2,3,4,4,5,5,5,6$.

Neither calculation proves the asymptotic question. A finite list only supplies a witness, and exhaustive search only settles the tested values of $n$. It cannot show that a construction exists for all large $n$, or that every longer construction eventually fails.

## What is known

The maintained [Erdős Problems entry for #357](https://www.erdosproblems.com/357) records the main question as open. It attributes to Erdős's 1977 survey the observation that an averaging argument forces $a_k\gg k\log k$ for infinitely many $k$ in an infinite analogue, hence zero lower density, and records the question about convergence of $\sum 1/a_k$. The page also reports Desmond Weisenberg's lower bound $f(n)\geq(2+o(1))\sqrt n$ via a related problem. Those are meaningful constraints, but neither answers the order of growth.

The page separately defines $g(n)$ by allowing an arbitrary sequence in $[1,n]$, without strict monotonicity, and reports Hegyvári's 1986 bounds between $(1/3+o(1))n$ and $(2/3+o(1))n$. Hegyvári's primary paper is [On consecutive sums in sequences](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01949064), *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* 48 (1986), 193–200. The journal record was available, but the complete article was not available in this bounded source pass, so the exact theorem has been retained as a reported auxiliary result rather than independently certified here.

A useful adjacent primary source is Beker's [2023 paper on consecutive sums in strictly increasing sequences](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10087). Its Theorem 1.2 proves the existence of a strictly increasing sequence with at least $c n^2$ distinct consecutive sums, for some constant $c>0$. That does not solve #357: it permits repeated sums and therefore does not imply that all consecutive sums are distinct. The paper itself distinguishes this “many values” problem from the all-distinct extremal question.

The live page reports a further upper-bound attribution to Coppersmith and Phillips for an auxiliary formulation, while its discussion records a scope objection about whether that bound applies to $f$ or $g$. This audit does not use that disputed attribution as a theorem about the main problem.

## What remains

The smallest useful next step is a source-and-semantics reconciliation. First, obtain and read Hegyvári's full paper, recording the exact hypotheses and theorem location for the non-monotone $g(n)$ result. Second, compare the website's “all sums” wording with the formal predicate, including whether the empty order-connected set is present and why positivity makes its zero sum harmless. Third, generate a compact exact certificate for $f(n)$ on a fixed finite range, with a witness at the claimed maximum and a collision certificate for every longer candidate.

That work can clarify interfaces and expose transcription errors, but it will not settle $f(n)=o(n)$. Any claimed asymptotic proof, counterexample, or Lean theorem replacement requires separate mathematical review and replay under the relevant authority.
