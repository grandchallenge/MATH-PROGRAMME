## The question in plain language

Choose an infinite sequence of independent unbiased signs, each either $+1$ or $-1$, and form the successive random polynomials

$$
P_n(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\varepsilon_j z^j.
$$

Let $R_n$ count their complex roots, including multiplicities, in the **closed unit disk**. [Erdős Problem #522](https://www.erdosproblems.com/522) asks whether

$$
\frac{R_n}{n/2}\longrightarrow1
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

*Almost surely* means that, with probability one, a single infinite realization of the signs gives the required limit. It is stronger than *convergence in probability*.

## A small example

For degree one, the polynomial is $P_1(z)=\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon_1z$. Its single root is $-\varepsilon_0/\varepsilon_1$, which always lies **on** the unit circle. Therefore a closed-disk count gives $R_1=1$, while an open-disk count gives zero.

This demonstrates why root-counting conventions matter even at the smallest degree. Nothing about this one finite polynomial decides the large-degree almost-sure law.

## What is known

The maintained registry attributes a weaker theorem to Yakir (2021): the normalized count converges to one **in probability**. Its reported deviation estimate has the form

$$
\mathbb P\left(|R_n-n/2|\ge n^{9/10}\right)\longrightarrow0.
$$

The fact that these probabilities tend to zero does not by itself yield an almost-sure statement: a summable deviation bound or another dependence-sensitive argument would be required. Erdős and Offord's earlier work on **real** roots concerns a different observable.

A forum comment has presented a claimed solution, but the maintained entry still classifies the almost-sure question as OPEN. The claim needs its full mathematical proof and provenance checked before it can be treated as resolved.

## GCL's current state

[Source audit #1793](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1793#issuecomment-6121442891) records the almost-sure versus in-probability distinction, the common infinite probability-space requirement and the closed-disk degree-one boundary. It also identifies ambiguity in the older source between sign coefficients and zero-one coefficients.

## What remains

Either prove the almost-sure law under one coherent iid coefficient process or exhibit a counterexample to that assertion. A theorem only establishing convergence in probability, or an unadjudicated comment claiming a solution, does not complete this obligation.
