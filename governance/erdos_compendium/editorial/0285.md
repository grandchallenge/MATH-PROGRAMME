## The question in plain language

Write $k$ distinct positive integers in increasing order, $n_1<\cdots<n_k$, whose reciprocal sum is $1$. Define $f(k)$ to be the smallest possible largest denominator $n_k$:

$$
f(k)=\min\left\{n_k:1\leq n_1<\cdots<n_k,\ \sum_{i=1}^k\frac{1}{n_i}=1\right\}.
$$

Erdős Problem 285 asks whether

$$
f(k)=(1+o(1))\frac{e}{e-1}k
$$

as $k$ tends to infinity. The quantifier is asymptotic: it does not say that every finite value of $f(k)$ equals the displayed main term, but that the ratio tends to $1$ along the values of $k$ for which the representation exists. There is no two-term representation of $1$ by distinct positive unit fractions, while the splitting identity

$$
\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}
$$

produces longer representations once a suitable one exists. Thus the natural eventual domain starts at $k=3$.

The [live problem page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/285) attributes the resolution to Martin. The exact source packet and authenticated audit are [issue #1555](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1555) and its [RESULT/1 comment](https://github.com/grandchallenge/MATH-PROGRAMME/issues/1555#issuecomment-6135376878).

## A small example

Exact search gives

$$
f(3)=6\quad\text{from }\quad 1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac16,
$$

and

$$
f(4)=12\quad\text{from }\quad 1=\frac12+\frac14+\frac16+\frac1{12}.
$$

The following calculation checks these values by enumerating every increasing denominator tuple whose largest entry is at most the search bound. `Fraction` makes every sum exact; no floating-point approximation is involved.

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations

def first_by_max(k, bound):
    for largest in range(k, bound + 1):
        for earlier in combinations(range(1, largest), k - 1):
            ns = earlier + (largest,)
            if sum((Fraction(1, n) for n in ns), Fraction()) == 1:
                return ns
    return None

assert first_by_max(3, 20) == (2, 3, 6)
assert first_by_max(4, 100) == (2, 4, 6, 12)
print(first_by_max(3, 20), first_by_max(4, 100))
```

The output is `(2, 3, 6) (2, 4, 6, 12)`. Since the routine tests smaller possible maxima first, these are finite certificates for the two stated values. They illustrate the definition only; no finite search can establish an asymptotic theorem.

## What is known

Greg Martin’s *Denser Egyptian fractions*, *Acta Arithmetica* 95 (2000), 231–260, proves a stronger statement. In the paper’s Theorem 2, equation (5), on published p. 232, Martin defines $M_t(r)$ as the least largest denominator in a representation with $t$ terms for a positive rational $r$. He proves that, for every positive rational $r$ and every integer $t\geq t_0(r)$,

$$
M_t(r)=\frac{t}{1-e^{-r}}+O_r\left(\frac{t\log\log(3t)}{\log(3t)}\right),
$$

and states that this is best possible. He explicitly takes $t_0(1)=3$. Therefore $f(k)=M_k(1)$ for $k\geq3$, and Martin’s theorem implies

$$
f(k)=\frac{e}{e-1}k+O\left(\frac{k\log\log(3k)}{\log(3k)}\right),
$$

which yields the problem’s $(1+o(1))$ formulation. The proof is a published external mathematical result. This audit checked the theorem statement and definitions in the accessible source; it did not independently reproduce Martin’s analytic proof.

The original Erdős–Graham source is cited by the maintained page as [ErGr80, p. 33], and the linked scan was reachable. It is the historical problem source, not a substitute for Martin’s resolving theorem. Croot’s related interval result is discussed in Martin’s introduction, but related consequences must not be promoted to the exact theorem for all values of $t$ without checking their quantifiers.
