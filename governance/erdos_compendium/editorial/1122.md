## The question in plain language

An *additive arithmetic function* assigns a real number to each positive integer and obeys

$$
f(ab)=f(a)+f(b)\quad\text{whenever }\gcd(a,b)=1.
$$

Suppose such a function **almost never decreases** when moving from $n$ to $n+1$. [Erdős Problem #1122](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1122) asks whether it must have the simple form $f(n)=c\log n$.

Here “almost never” means that the set of descents

$$
A=\{n\ge1:f(n+1)<f(n)\}
$$

has natural density zero, or $|A\cap[1,X]|=o(X)$. The function need only be additive on *coprime* inputs, not on arbitrary products.

## A small example

The function $\omega(n)$ counting **distinct prime factors** is additive on coprime products: primes dividing coprime $a,b$ occur in disjoint sets. Yet it is not a constant multiple of $\log n$.

For instance, $\omega(6)=2$ and $\omega(7)=1$, so $n=6$ is a descent. A finite exact search finds 65 descents for $1\le n\le200$ and checks additivity on 2,500 ordered pairs $a,b\le50$, subject to coprimality.

This finite list does **not** establish the asymptotic density of descents for $\omega(n)$; it is not a counterexample to Erdős's conjecture.

Conversely, for $c\ge0$, $c\log n$ has no descents. Negative $c$ makes every step a descent and hence cannot meet the density-zero premise.

## What is known

Erdős proved that $f(n)=c\log n$ when **no descents occur**, and also under the different assumption that $f(n+1)-f(n)\to0$.

The maintained catalogue records **Mangerel (2022)** as proving a partial result when the descent set obeys a stronger logarithmic saving,

$$
|A\cap[1,X]|\ll\frac{X}{(\log X)^{2+\varepsilon}},
$$

*and* prime values $f(p)$ satisfy an additional technical growth restriction. Dropping that restriction would misstate the theorem.

The original weaker density-zero question remains **open**.
