## The question in plain language

Let $\omega(n)$ count the **distinct** prime factors of $n$. For instance, $\omega(12)=2$ because $12=2^2\cdot3$. Erdős asked whether

$$
S=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(n)}{2^n}
$$

can equal a fraction of two integers. The denominator makes the sum converge rapidly, but convergence alone says nothing about rationality.

## A small example

The first contributions come from $n=2,3,4,5,6$:

$$
\frac14+\frac18+\frac1{16}+\frac1{32}+\frac2{64}.
$$

There is a remarkable equivalent form:

$$
S=\frac1{2^2-1}+\frac1{2^3-1}+\frac1{2^5-1}+\cdots
=\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\frac1{2^p-1}.
$$

Indeed $\omega(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}1$. Exchanging nonnegative sums and evaluating the geometric series over multiples of each prime gives the identity. This proves the *identity*, not irrationality.

## What is known

**The irrationality question is solved.** Terence Tao and Joni Teräväinen proved unconditionally that $S$ is irrational. Their Theorem 1.3 appears in *Quantitative correlations and some problems on prime factors of consecutive integers*, arXiv:2512.01739v2 (2026), with proof in Section 5. Their argument relies on difficult estimates for multiplicative-function correlations and probabilistic reasoning. Earlier work by Kyle Pratt obtained a conditional result. The classical irrationality theorem for the distinct series involving $\tau(n)$, the number of divisors, does not settle this question by itself.

## What remains

A bounded exercise is to prove, for every integer $N\ge2$,

$$
\sum_{n=2}^N\frac{\omega(n)}{2^n}
=\sum_{p\le N}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/p\rfloor}\frac1{2^{pk}}.
$$

Exact rational checks for all $2\le N\le100$ passed on CHAD. The infinite identity follows by monotone convergence. Formal certification of the unconditional irrationality theorem would be a separate undertaking.
