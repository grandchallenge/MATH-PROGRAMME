## The question in plain language

Erdős Problem 389 asks whether every positive integer $n$ has a positive integer $k$ for which the product of $k$ consecutive integers beginning at $n$ divides the next block of $k$ consecutive integers:

$$
\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(n+i)\mid\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(n+k+i).
$$

The divisor and dividend have the same number of factors, but the second block is shifted to the right. The question is not whether one can find a $k$ for a particular small $n$; it asks for a witness for every $n$, with no proposed bound on how large that witness may be. Divisibility here means that the quotient of the two integer products is an integer.

There is a small indexing wrinkle. The 1980 source uses products beginning at $n+1$ and ending at $n+k$, whereas the maintained page and the formal statement use products beginning at $n$. Replacing the source variable by $n-1$ aligns the formulas. The live version also includes $n=1$, where $k=1$ is immediate, so this shift does not change the universal question.

## A small example

For $n=2$ and $k=5$, exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot5\cdot6=720,
$$

and

$$
7\cdot8\cdot9\cdot10\cdot11=55440=77\cdot720.
$$

Thus $(n,k)=(2,5)$ is a verified witness in the maintained indexing. The older source's example $(n,k)=(2,4)$ is the same calculation after the shift: it checks $3\cdot4\cdot5\cdot6\mid7\cdot8\cdot9\cdot10$, with quotient $14$, and therefore corresponds to $n=3$ on the live page.

An additional finite check is useful for scale. Direct products with arbitrary-precision integers give remainder zero for $n=4,k=207$ and no zero remainder for any $1\leq k<207$. This agrees with the formal variant recording $207$ as the least witness for $n=4$. Neither calculation says anything about an arbitrary later $n$: a finite search can confirm witnesses, but cannot establish the universal quantifier or prove that a search found every possible witness unless its search range is explicitly exhaustive for the stated target.

## What is known

The primary historical reference is Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 75. There the question is attributed to Erdős and Straus, and the authors record the shifted product formula, the small example, and the observation that no example was then known for source parameters $n\geq10$. That last observation is historical, not a current status statement.

Ulas and Schinzel's 2013 paper, “A note on Erdős–Straus and Erdős–Graham divisibility problems” (International Journal of Number Theory 9 (2013), 583–599), studies this exact problem. It rewrites the source-indexed divisibility as

$$
\binom{k+n}{n}\mid\binom{2k+n}{k},
$$

because both consecutive products contain a factor of $k!$. Their Theorem 2.1 gives a prime-by-prime test using base $p$ digit sums: for each prime dividing the first block, the relevant expression $2s_p(k+n)-s_p(2k+n)-s_p(n)$ must be nonnegative. This is a useful computational interface, not a proof that every $n$ has a witness.

The same paper proves infinitely many witnesses for its source-indexed $n=1$, and transfers witnesses from that case to source-indexed $n=2$. It reports computed least witnesses through $n=16$, and gives values for $n=17,18,19,20$ marked with asterisks because those values were inferred through a conjectural connection rather than fully checked as minima. Its Conjecture 2.10 is precisely the belief that every source-indexed $n$ has a witness. These results are substantial finite and structural evidence, but they leave the assertion for all values of $n$ open.

## What remains

A bounded successor task should first freeze the normalization: use the live statement, or explicitly use the source-indexed variable and document the shift. Its input can be one selected $n$ and a finite interval $1\leq k\leq K$. The target is an exact witness or an exact certificate that the interval contains none. A suitable method is the Ulas–Schinzel digit-sum criterion, independently cross-checked for any reported witness by direct arbitrary-precision products. Acceptance requires the stated products to have zero remainder and the tested range to be reproducible; falsification is either a nonzero remainder for the proposed witness or a missing witness in a claimed exhaustive interval. Stop when the chosen interval is exhausted, a source ambiguity reappears, or the arithmetic and criterion disagree. This gives useful evidence without pretending that finite coverage proves the universal claim.
