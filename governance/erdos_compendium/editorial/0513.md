**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #513 is **OPEN**. It asks for the largest possible limiting proportion of an entire function's maximum modulus contributed by any one term of its power series. A 2026 construction improved the lower estimate from the classical $4/7$ to a value above $0.58507$; a later documented, GPT-assisted refinement reports a lower estimate above $0.5850788$. The exact constant remains unknown.

## The question in plain language

A transcendental entire function has a power series converging everywhere,

$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n.
$$

At radius $r>0$, compare two numbers: the largest *individual* term, and the largest magnitude of the *complete sum* on the circle $|z|=r$.

$$
\mu(r,f)=\max_{n\ge0}|a_n|r^n,
\qquad
M(r,f)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.
$$

Cauchy's estimate for coefficients says $\mu(r,f)\le M(r,f)$ for every $r$. However, the complete sum can be much larger than its largest individual term. We ask how large the ratio $\mu(r,f)/M(r,f)$ can stay on its **worst sufficiently large radii**, if we are free to choose the function.

The problem is to determine

$$
\boxed{
B=\sup_{\substack{f\text{ transcendental}\\f\text{ entire}}}
\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\mu(r,f)}{M(r,f)}.
}
$$

The order of the supremum and limit inferior is fundamental. First select one function $f$ and examine arbitrarily large radii where the ratio is small; **only then** optimize over functions. Replacing $\liminf$ by $\limsup$, or optimizing over a different function at every radius, would answer another question.

The function must be **transcendental**, meaning that it is *not a polynomial*. The 1961 printed text says “entire,” but that unrestricted wording gives the trivial upper extremum one. For example, $f(z)=z+1$ has $\mu(r,f)=r$ for $r\ge1$ and $M(r,f)=r+1$, so its ratio tends to one. Excluding polynomials is essential, not a cosmetic technicality.

## A small example

Consider the entire function

$$
f(z)=e^z=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!}.
$$

Its maximum on the circle $|z|=r$ is $M(r,e^z)=e^r$, achieved on the positive real axis. The largest Taylor term is approximately $e^r/\sqrt{2\pi r}$ by Stirling's formula, so

$$
\frac{\mu(r,e^z)}{M(r,e^z)}
\sim\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi r}}\to0.
$$

Thus a familiar, rapidly growing entire function is **not** close to extremal for this problem. The quantity $B$ rewards carefully balanced cancellation among different Taylor terms on large circles.

A more promising family has coefficients that decay *quadratically* with the index. For $K>1$ and a phase $\varepsilon$ with $|\varepsilon|=1$, define

$$
f_{K,\varepsilon}(z)
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\varepsilon^{n(n-1)/2}}{K^{n(n+1)/2}}z^n.
$$

The series converges for every complex $z$, since the denominator grows like $K^{n^2/2}$. At a geometric radius $r=K^m$, the magnitudes of successive terms are related by a very simple exact ratio:

$$
\frac{|a_{n+1}|r^{n+1}}{|a_n|r^n}
=\frac{r}{K^{n+1}}.
$$

The maximum therefore occurs at **two adjacent indices**, $n=m-1$ and $n=m$, tied at the value

$$
\mu(K^m,f_{K,\varepsilon})=K^{m(m-1)/2}.
$$

This already provides an exact reusable lemma; it does **not** by itself establish the correct maximum modulus or the final limiting value of $B$.

## What is known

### The original question and the 1964 bounds

Erdős asked in *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.249](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=29), Problem IV.5. The original page was visually inspected. He noted $1/2\le B\le1$, and credited Kövári with an unpublished $B>1/2$ argument. The upper bound one is Cauchy's inequality; the polynomial counterexample explains why the class must be transcendental.

Gray and Shah, *A note on entire functions and a conjecture of Erdős*, *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* **69** (1963), 573–577, obtained an upper bound $2/\pi$. J. Clunie and W. K. Hayman, *The maximum term of a power series*, *Journal d'Analyse Mathématique* **12** (1964), 143–186, improved both sides:

$$
\boxed{\frac47<B\le\frac2\pi-c_0}
$$

for some constant $c_0>0$. The complete 1964 original proof was not obtained in this audit. The positive gap beneath $2/\pi$ is recorded in the maintained catalogue; accessible later references often state only the weaker $B<2/\pi$.

### He and Tang's February 2026 construction

Yixin He and Quanyu Tang, *Generalizing the Clunie–Hayman construction in an Erdős maximum-term problem*, [arXiv:2602.12217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.12217), establish $B>0.5850724$ through a two-parameter construction. Their original paper's first and fourth pages were visually inspected, including its exact scaling lemma.

Define the two-sided Laurent series

$$
k_{K,\varepsilon}(z)=
\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
\frac{\varepsilon^{n(n-1)/2}}{K^{n(n+1)/2}}z^n.
$$

It satisfies the identity

$$
\boxed{k_{K,\varepsilon}(Kz)=z\,k_{K,\varepsilon}(\varepsilon z).}
$$

At the coefficient level, multiplying the $z^n$ coefficient by $K^n$ equals the $z^{n-1}$ coefficient multiplied by $\varepsilon^{n-1}$. This is an exact equality of convergent Laurent series for all nonzero $z$.

Iteration reduces the circle maximum at geometric radii $K^m$ to that on the unit circle with a known scaling factor. The authors then compare the Laurent sum to the entire positive-index series and establish the required liminf reduction. Rigorous **interval arithmetic** supplies the final numerical lower bound. Finite coefficient checks cannot themselves prove the decimal estimate.

The maintained [Erdős #513 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/513), revised April 2026, also documents a later Sothanaphan-prompted GPT-assisted refinement to $B>0.5850788$. The underlying complete numerical certificate has not been independently run by GCL. These bounds are externally reported and not a new mathematical discovery here.

The current documented range is approximately $0.5850788<B<0.63662$, with the stronger Clunie–Hayman upper expression $2/\pi-c_0$. The exact extremum remains unknown.

## What remains

The bounded verification obligation is to check the Laurent identity coefficientwise, establish rapid coefficient decay, and verify precisely which indices maximize the term at radius $K^m$. The next independent mathematical work would be interval certification of the unit-circle maximum and proof of uniform tail and intermediate-radius estimates. That is separate from editorial draft admission.

The general determination of $B$ remains OPEN.
