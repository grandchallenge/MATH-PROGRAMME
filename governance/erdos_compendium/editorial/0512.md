**Editorial status:** UNACCEPTED_DRAFT. Erdős Problem #512 is **PROVED**: the $L^1$ norm of an exponential sum over any $N$ distinct integer frequencies is bounded below by a constant times $\log N$. Sergei Konyagin and McGehee–Pigno–Smith proved the result independently in 1981. The exponent and logarithmic order are sharp; consecutive frequencies give matching order. An external Lean formalization was reported in June 2026, but GCL's pinned statement still has a proof placeholder and GCL has not independently replayed the kernel proof.

## The question in plain language

Choose $N$ **distinct integers** $A=\{n_1,\ldots,n_N\}$. Form the periodic complex exponential sum

$$
P_A(\theta)=\sum_{n\in A}e^{2\pi i n\theta}.
$$

What can one guarantee about the average **absolute magnitude** of the sum, regardless of how far apart or irregularly the frequencies are spaced?

The statement is that there exists one universal $c>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{
\int_0^1|P_A(\theta)|\,d\theta\ge c\log N
}
$$

for every $N\ge1$ and every $N$-element set of distinct integer frequencies. The case $N=1$ is vacuous on the right because $\log1=0$. For larger $N$, the logarithmic growth is the substance of Littlewood's conjecture.

There are two essential operations: **first add the complex waves, then take the absolute value, then integrate**. Taking the absolute value of the integral would be different: for example, if $0\notin A$, then $\int_0^1P_A(\theta)\,d\theta=0$ by orthogonality.

Negative frequencies and zero are allowed in this complex formulation. Adding the same integer $m$ to every frequency gives

$$
P_{A+m}(\theta)=e^{2\pi i m\theta}P_A(\theta),
$$

and therefore preserves $|P_A(\theta)|$ pointwise. The relative frequency differences, not the absolute origin of frequency numbering, determine the norm.

Erdős's original 1961 paper displays a **real cosine** $L^1$ problem, $\int_0^{2\pi}|\sum_j\cos(n_j\theta)|\,d\theta\gg\log N$. The current [Erdős #512 catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/512) uses the complex exponential formulation. They are related Littlewood-type results, but their formulas must not be declared literally identical without an additional comparison argument.

## A small example

Take the **consecutive** set $A=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}$. The resulting polynomial is the classical Dirichlet kernel

$$
D_N(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi i j\theta}.
$$

By the geometric-series identity, for nonintegral $\theta$,

$$
D_N(\theta)=\frac{1-e^{2\pi iN\theta}}{1-e^{2\pi i\theta}},
\qquad
|D_N(\theta)|=\frac{|\sin(\pi N\theta)|}{|\sin(\pi\theta)|}.
$$

The kernel is large near integer $\theta$ and oscillatory elsewhere. A classical elementary calculation gives

$$
\int_0^1|D_N(\theta)|\,d\theta=\Theta(\log N).
$$

Thus the logarithm in Littlewood's conjecture **cannot generally be replaced by a faster-growing function**. The special case establishes *sharpness*, not the universal lower bound for arbitrary irregular $A$.

A second check follows from Fourier orthogonality:

$$
\int_0^1|P_A(\theta)|^2\,d\theta=N.
$$

This states that the $L^2$ norm is $\sqrt N$, but it does **not** directly establish a logarithmic lower bound for the **$L^1$ norm**. The distinction between $L^1$, $L^2$ and cancellation is why the 1981 solution required substantial harmonic analysis.

## What is known

### Erdős's 1961 original problem

Erdős, *Some unsolved problems* (1961), [printed p.247](https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1961-22.pdf#page=27), writes his problem IV.2.1 with real cosines and notes that consecutive frequencies show the proposed logarithmic bound would be best possible. The printed page was visually inspected. At that time even the assertion that the norm necessarily tends to infinity was difficult. Erdős credits P. J. Cohen with an earlier estimate and H. Davenport with an improvement, neither of which settled the final logarithmic bound.

### Two independent 1981 solutions

Sergei V. Konyagin, *On a problem of Littlewood*, *Izvestiya Akademii Nauk SSSR, Seriya Matematicheskaya* **45** (1981), no.2, 243–265, 463, [primary publisher record](https://m.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=im&option_lang=eng&paperid=1556&wshow=paper), directly states the complex exponential result: for distinct integers $m_j$,

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
\left|\sum_{j=1}^N e^{im_jx}\right|\,dx
\ge C\log N.
$$

The substitution $x=2\pi\theta$ makes this precisely the displayed complex $L^1$ theorem, up to multiplying the absolute constant by $1/(2\pi)$. The primary bibliographic record and mathematical abstract were checked; the entire original argument was not independently reconstructed.

Independently, O. Carruth McGehee, Louis Pigno and Brent Smith, *Hardy's inequality and the $L^1$-norm of exponential sums*, *Annals of Mathematics* (2) **113** (1981), 613–618, [original Annals record](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1981/113-3/p09), obtained the theorem using a strengthening of Hardy's inequality for Fourier coefficients of analytic-type measures. This approach relates weighted sequences of ordered Fourier coefficients to an $L^1$ lower bound, yielding the logarithm through the harmonic series. The official Annals site verifies its authors, year and pages; its complete proof body was not obtained here.

These are **historically established 1981 solutions**, not new GCL work. The [maintained catalogue](https://www.erdosproblems.com/512) reports the problem **PROVED (LEAN)** and names both author groups.

### Why the logarithm is the exact order

For the consecutive frequencies, the sine-ratio formula yields a direct upper bound. On $0<\theta\le1/2$,

$$
|\sin(\pi\theta)|\ge2\theta,
$$

while the trivial triangle inequality gives $|D_N|\le N$. By symmetry,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1|D_N(\theta)|\,d\theta
&\le2\int_0^{1/2}
\min\left(N,\frac{1}{2\theta}\right)\,d\theta\\
&\le 2+\log(N/2)\qquad(N\ge2).
\end{aligned}
$$

The matching **lower** logarithmic growth can be checked on disjoint intervals. For $N\ge16$ and

$$
1\le j\le\left\lfloor\frac N4\right\rfloor-1,
$$

consider

$$
I_j=\left[
\frac{j+1/6}{N},\frac{j+5/6}{N}
\right].
$$

On $I_j$, the numerator $|\sin(\pi N\theta)|$ is at least $1/2$ and the denominator $|\sin(\pi\theta)|$ is at most $\pi\theta$. The interval length is $2/(3N)$, and $\theta\le(j+5/6)/N$, so

$$
\int_{I_j}|D_N(\theta)|\,d\theta
\ge\frac{1}{3\pi(j+5/6)}.
$$

Summing over $j$ gives a positive multiple of $\log N$. Together with the upper bound, this independently proves that the **consecutive-frequency example** has $L^1$ norm of order $\log N$.

This elementary example should not be confused with the much harder universal statement for *every* frequency set, which is what Konyagin and McGehee–Pigno–Smith proved.
