## The question in plain language

Let $\tau(n)$ count the positive divisors of $n$. A factorial $n!$ is the product $1\cdot2\cdots n$, and it gains new prime factors and higher prime powers as $n$ increases. Erdős Problem 419 studies how quickly the **number of divisors of factorials** changes from one step to the next. Its central sequence is

$$
R(n)=\frac{\tau((n+1)!)}{\tau(n!)}.
$$

The question asks for the *limit points* of this sequence: the real numbers approached by $R(n)$ along some subsequence with $n$ tending to infinity. It is not the same as asking for a single ordinary limit. In particular, some subsequences may behave differently from others without contradicting the existence of an exact, countable set of limit points.

The problem was posed in Erdős and Graham's 1980 book. The final answer is unusually explicit:

$$
\{1\}\ \cup\
\left\{1+\frac1k:k=1,2,3,\ldots\right\}.
$$

This set is bounded between $1$ and $2$, contains $2$ and $3/2$, and has $1$ as its sole accumulation point. The mathematical obligation is to show both that no other limit points occur and that every number displayed really occurs as a subsequential limit.

## A small example

Divisor counts can be computed exactly from the prime factorization. If $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$, each divisor chooses an exponent between $0$ and $a_i$ independently, so

$$
\tau(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(a_i+1).
$$

For $n=5$, we have $5!=120=2^3\cdot3\cdot5$, so $\tau(5!)=4\cdot2\cdot2=16$. Meanwhile $6!=720=2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5$, giving $\tau(6!)=5\cdot3\cdot2=30$. Therefore

$$
R(5)=\frac{30}{16}=\frac{15}{8}.
$$

For $n=6$, the new factor $7$ is prime and $7!=6!\cdot7$. It doubles the number of divisors without affecting previous prime exponents, so

$$
R(6)=\frac{60}{30}=2.
$$

The exact arithmetic was independently checked using integer prime factorization and also yields $\tau(8!)=96$, so $R(7)=96/60=8/5$. Neither $15/8$ nor $8/5$ needs to be a limit point. A term of the sequence can lie outside its eventual limit-point set; the definition concerns infinitely far-out indices.

There is an important immediate source of the limit point $2$: whenever $n+1=p$ is prime, the new factor $p$ did not divide $n!$, and hence $R(n)=2$. Since there are infinitely many primes, $2$ occurs infinitely often. This is a genuine proof of one limit point, not merely a finite example.

## What is known

Erdős and Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), **printed page 83**, already noted the limit points $1+1/k$ and $1$, but asked whether others could occur. The historical source is available in the [monograph scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf).

The complete characterization appeared in P. Erdős, S. W. Graham, A. Ivić and C. Pomerance, *On the number of divisors of $n!$*, in *Analytic Number Theory*, Progress in Mathematics 138 (1996), pp. 337–355; [publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-4086-0_19). **Corollary 1** gives precisely the set displayed above. The maintained [Erdős Problems #419 page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/419) reproduces a concise argument communicated by Mehtaab Sawhney and notes the earlier 1996 result. It currently labels the problem solved.

Here is the mechanism. Writing $v_p(m)$ for the exponent of prime $p$ in $m$, the exact formula

$$
R(n)=\prod_{p\mid n+1}
\left(1+\frac{v_p(n+1)}{v_p(n!)+1}\right)
$$

follows immediately from $\tau$. For primes $p\leq n^{2/3}$, the contribution becomes negligible in aggregate as $n$ grows, because $v_p(n!)$ is large relative to $v_p(n+1)$. There can be at most one prime divisor $p>n^{2/3}$ of $n+1$ for large $n$. If one exists, write $n+1=kp$. Then $p$ appears exactly once in $n+1$, and $v_p(n!)=k-1$ for sufficiently large $p$. Its contribution is $1+1/k$. This is why the only possible limiting shapes are $1$ and $1+1/k$.

To realize $1+1/k$ for fixed $k$, choose primes $p$ tending to infinity and take $n=kp-1$. The large-prime factor contributes $1+1/k$ while the remaining finite collection of small prime factors contributes a factor tending to $1$. To realize $1$, choose $n+1$ as a power of $2$, for which the ratio approaches $1$. These statements explain the published characterization but are not presented as a newly certified replacement for the complete 1996 proof.
