## The question in plain language

For integers $n$ and $k$ with $1 \leq k < n$, consider the binomial coefficient

$$
\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}.
$$

Problem 387 asks whether there is one absolute constant $c>0$ that works for every such pair: must $\binom{n}{k}$ always have a divisor $d$ with $cn<d\leq n$? “Absolute” means that $c$ cannot depend on $n$ or $k$. Since $\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}$, it is equivalent to inspect $1\leq k\leq n/2$, although the registered question uses $k<n$.

The intuition is appealing. The numerator consists of $k$ consecutive integers close to $n$, so one might expect some uncancelled factor, or a product of factors, to remain close to $n$. The difficulty is that division by $k!$ can cancel precisely the factors that would have supplied a large divisor. The question is about the divisor structure of the resulting integer, not about the size of the binomial coefficient itself.

## A small example

Take $n=4$ and $k=2$. Exact integer arithmetic gives

$$
\binom{4}{2}=6,
$$

whose divisors at most $n$ are $1,2,3$. Thus $2$ and $3$ lie in $[n/k,n]=[2,4]$. This illustrates the elementary lower scale: a divisor somewhere near $n/k$ is easy to obtain, but that is not a fixed positive fraction of $n$ when $k$ grows.

The historical example is more revealing. I evaluated `math.comb(99215,15)` exactly and checked all fifteen integers $99215-i$ for $0\leq i<15$. Every remainder of the binomial coefficient on division by $99215-i$ was nonzero; the first three remainders were $39686$, $49607$, and $66142$, and the last was $33067$. Hence none of the numerator terms divides $\binom{99215}{15}$. This verifies a finite fact, associated with Schinzel’s 1958 counterexample, but it does not by itself disprove the question for a fixed constant $c$: a single coefficient could still have another divisor in $(cn,n]$.

## What is known

The live Erdős Problems page records the original question and the historical references [ErGr76b, p. 351], [Er78g, p. 315], and [ErGr80, p. 74]. It also records Schinzel’s counterexample, the elementary interval $[n/k,n]$, and Faulkner’s result about a sufficiently large prime divisor, each of which concerns a related but different assertion.

The relevant primary modern source is H. M. Bui, S. Naprienko, K. Pratt, and A. Zaharescu, “Binomial coefficients with divisors avoiding an interval,” arXiv:2605.21221v3, revised 30 September 2026. Theorem 1.2 proves a positive result in the regime of large $k$: for sufficiently small fixed $\epsilon>0$, sufficiently large $n$, and

$$
\exp((\log n)^{2/3+\epsilon})\leq k\leq n/2,
$$

there is a divisor in $\left(n-n/(\log n)^{1/4},n\right]$. Theorem 1.4 gives infinitely many exceptions in a regime of small $k$, and Corollary 1.7 states that for every fixed $c>0$ some binomial coefficient has no divisor in $(cn,n]$. The paper describes a restricted covering argument, sieve estimates, and exponential-sum estimates; this article does not independently certify those proofs.

There is a version boundary worth preserving. The source packet and earlier arXiv wording were associated with a GRH-qualified negative answer, whereas the current v3 abstract and theorem text contain no GRH hypothesis. The audit therefore attributes the unconditional wording only to v3, not to an undated summary or to the catalogue alone.
