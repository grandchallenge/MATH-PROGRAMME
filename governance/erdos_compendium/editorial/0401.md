## The question in plain language

Let $p_r$ be prime number $r$, and form the integer

$$
N_{n,r}=n!\,2^n3^n\cdots p_r^n.
$$

Erdős Problem 401 asks whether there is one function $f$ that grows without bound with $r$, while for every $r\geq1$ there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ for which one can find positive integers $a_1,a_2$ satisfying

$$
a_1+a_2>n+f(r)\log n
$$

and $a_1!a_2!$ divides $N_{n,r}$. In words, the two factorials must fit inside a number built from $n!$ and the first $r$ primes, but the two factorial indices together must exceed $n$ by a margin that becomes larger, on the logarithmic scale, as more primes are allowed.

The original source does not spell out all quantifiers. The maintained [Erdős Problems entry](https://www.erdosproblems.com/401) adopts the “infinitely many $n$” reading, and its discussion explains why. This article follows that registered formulation; it does not silently promote the stronger “all sufficiently large $n$” variant.

## A small example

A finite calculation illustrates the divisibility mechanism. Take $r=1$, so the extra prime factor is $2^n$, and choose $n=36$, $a_1=8$, and $a_2=34$. Then

$$
\frac{36!\,2^{36}}{8!\,34!}
=\frac{35\cdot36}{8!}\,2^{36}
=\frac{1260}{40320}\,2^{36}
=2^{31}.
$$

Thus $8!34!$ divides $36!2^{36}$ exactly, and $8+34=42>36$. This is reproducible integer arithmetic: the quotient is the displayed integer $2^{31}$, with no numerical approximation. It does not prove the problem. It treats only $r=1$ and one $n$, gives no function $f$, and does not show that suitable $n$ occur infinitely often. It also does not show that the six-unit margin is larger than the unknown quantity $f(1)\log 36$.

## What is known

The primary historical source is P. Erdős and R. L. Graham, *Old and New Problems and Results in Combinatorial Number Theory* (1980), p. 78. The scan is [available from Ronald Graham’s papers](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/80_11_number_theory.pdf); the relevant page literally asks for $a_1+a_2>n+d_r\log n$ with $d_r\to\infty$, and asks whether the displayed quotient is an integer. The downloaded PDF had SHA-256 `0cbf0c32f0ab1e1c71db5121a88bac905bf976c4a6ab6bb6d7d9cf9ddd184ed3`.

The maintained page records an affirmative resolution attributed to Barreto and Leeham, using an argument essentially parallel to their solution of Problem 729. Its discussion also records a disproof of the alternative “for all large $n$” interpretation: with $n=p_{r+1}^k-1$, the divisibility condition is reported to force $a_1+a_2\leq n+2p_{r+1}$. That is evidence about a distinct stronger variant, not a contradiction to the statement concerning infinitely many $n$.

The public discussion links an informal GPT-5.2 Pro proof and a long Lean development. The hosted source describes a construction with $n=2m$, $a_1=m+k$, $a_2=m$, a prime-by-prime valuation reduction, Kummer-style carry counts, and a probabilistic choice of $m$ in intervals $[M,2M]$. These are reported proof materials. This audit did not replay the Lean build or certify the mathematics independently.
