## The question in plain language

Colour every **positive integer** either red or blue. There is no regularity assumption: the colouring can be completely arbitrary.

For a finite or infinite set $A$ of distinct positive integers, define its **nonempty finite-sums set**

$$
\operatorname{FS}(A)=
\left\{\sum_{a\in S}a:
\varnothing\ne S\subseteq A,\quad S\text{ finite}\right\}.
$$

The elements of each $S$ are used **at most once**, and the empty sum is excluded. The original conjecture asks whether every colouring has an **infinite** set $A$ for which **all** of $\operatorname{FS}(A)$ receives just one colour:

$$
\boxed{
\forall c:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\{0,1\},\
\exists A\subseteq\mathbb Z_{>0}\text{ infinite},\
\exists i\in\{0,1\},\
\forall x\in\operatorname{FS}(A),\ c(x)=i.
}
$$

Crucially, the colour $i$ is the **same for every finite sum**. It cannot depend on how many terms were used. A colour class that contains the finite sums of some infinite sequence is called an **IP set** (an infinite finite-sums set).

The theorem actually holds for **any finite number of colours**, not just two.

This is different from the preceding finite Folkman problem, Erdős #531. Folkman's theorem says that any sufficiently long finite interval guarantees $k$ suitable generators for any fixed $k$. Problem #531 asks for the *smallest such interval*. Hindman's theorem asks for an **infinite** family of generators and is a qualitative existence theorem.

## A small example

Take a finite set $A=\{1,2,4\}$. Its seven nonempty finite subset sums are

$$
\operatorname{FS}(A)=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}.
$$

This happens because each integer from one to seven has a unique binary representation using the three generators. More generally,

$$
\left|\operatorname{FS}\bigl(\{1,2,4,\ldots,2^{m-1}\}\bigr)\right|
=2^m-1.
$$

That equality does **not** hold for every set of distinct positive integers. For example,

$$
\operatorname{FS}(\{1,2,3\})=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\},
$$

which has only six distinct values rather than seven, because the singleton sum $3$ coincides with $1+2$.

There is a simple **exact recursion** that is useful for verifying finite examples. If $A_m=\{a_0,\ldots,a_{m-1}\}$ and $a_m$ is another distinct positive integer, then

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{FS}(A_m\cup\{a_m\})=
\operatorname{FS}(A_m)\ \cup\ \{a_m\}\
\cup\bigl(a_m+\operatorname{FS}(A_m)\bigr).
}
$$

Each nonempty subset either omits $a_m$, consists solely of $a_m$, or contains $a_m$ together with another nonempty subset. This identity remains true even when some sums collide.

The recursion provides a finite mathematical check, but it is **not** a proof of Hindman's infinite colouring theorem. The deep content is finding generators that work for every finite subset simultaneously, despite a completely arbitrary colouring of the entire infinite number line.

## What is known

### The Graham–Rothschild conjecture in Erdős's original source

Erdős discussed the problem in *Problems and Results on Combinatorial Number Theory*, Chapter 12 of *A Survey of Combinatorial Theory* (1973), [printed p.122](https://www.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1973-21.pdf#page=6). The original scanned page was obtained and inspected. Erdős credits **Graham and Rothschild** with the infinite finite-sums question, then open, while the preceding paragraph states the established **finite** Sanders–Folkman theorem.

Erdős also describes a weaker challenge in which sums of exactly $k$ terms may receive a colour depending on $k$. This is not the registered theorem: Hindman's stronger result guarantees **one colour for every finite number of terms**.

### Hindman's 1974 theorem

Neil Hindman, *Finite sums from sequences within cells of a partition of* $\mathbb N$, *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A* **17** (July 1974), 1–11, [DOI 10.1016/0097-3165(74)90023-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(74)90023-5), proved the Graham–Rothschild conjecture. The original publisher's abstract explicitly confirms that one class of a two-colouring contains a sequence together with all finite sums of distinct members. The result extends to arbitrary finite colourings.

The complete original eleven-page proof was not independently reconstructed here; its mathematical status is based on the historical original, not a new GCL argument.

### Ultrafilters and the formalization mechanism

A standard proof uses an **idempotent ultrafilter** $U$ on the natural numbers, satisfying

$$
U+U=U.
$$

When an ultrafilter with this property contains a set, one can recursively choose positive generators whose entire nonempty finite-sums set remains inside that set. Given a finite partition, some colour class belongs to the ultrafilter, yielding the desired infinite monochromatic finite-sums sequence.

Mathlib's [Hindman module](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Combinatorics/Hindman.html) documents the partition-regularity theorem and its idempotent-ultrafilter construction. The source is not just a theorem name: it provides reusable general-semigroup results for finite sums.
