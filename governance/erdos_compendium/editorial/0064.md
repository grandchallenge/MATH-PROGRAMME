## The question in plain language

If every vertex of a finite graph has at least three neighbors, must the graph contain a cycle whose length is a power of two: $4,8,16,32,\ldots$? This is the Erdős–Gyárfás conjecture.

The problem is unresolved. Its catalogue label **FALSIFIABLE** does not mean it has been disproved. It means that a single verified finite counterexample would refute the universal claim.

## A small example

The complete graph $K_4$ has four vertices, each connected to the other three. Its minimum degree is three, and it contains a four-cycle. Thus it satisfies the conjecture.

The word *finite* is crucial. An infinite tree can have every vertex of degree three but no cycles at all. Such a tree is not a counterexample to the finite-graph conjecture.

## What is known

Liu and Montgomery proved that graphs of sufficiently large **average degree** contain cycles of power-of-two length. Their [Corollary 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2010.15802v2) covers sequences of even cycle lengths growing at most exponentially, including $4,8,16,\ldots$. The underlying Theorem 1.1 gives every even cycle length in a long interval.

If the minimum degree is at least the theorem's sufficiently large threshold, then the average degree is also at least that threshold, so the conjecture holds. But this does **not** establish the conjecture at the original minimum degree three.

Erdős and Gyárfás also entertained a stronger possibility: for every large minimum-degree threshold, some graph might avoid all power-of-two cycle lengths. The high-average-degree theorem rules out that stronger speculation. It does not prove the original statement.

## What remains

A bounded computational task is to enumerate finite cubic graphs and test every simple cycle whose length is a power of two and at most the number of vertices. A candidate counterexample must come with a complete edge list, a verified minimum-degree calculation and a reproducible absence-of-cycle certificate. Finding no counterexample up to some size is only finite evidence.

A separate formal task is to encode the implication from a sufficiently large minimum-degree threshold to the known average-degree theorem, without replacing the unknown threshold by three.
