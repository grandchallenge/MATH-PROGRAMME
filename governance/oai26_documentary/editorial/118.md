# OAI-26 family 118: unbounded configuration-LP gaps for bin packing

## The question in plain language

Bin packing asks how to place items of specified sizes into as few unit-capacity bins as possible. A mathematical relaxation allows fractional combinations of feasible bin contents, which can be easier to optimize than a genuine packing. The *integrality gap* measures the difference between the best actual packing and the fractional optimum.

Family 118 claims that this additive difference can become arbitrarily large for the standard configuration linear program, and that finding a packing within any fixed additive number of optimal bins is NP-hard.

## A small example

Suppose items have sizes $0.4,0.4,0.4$. A real packing needs two unit bins: two items fit together, while all three do not. A configuration represents a collection of items that could share one bin without exceeding capacity. The configuration LP permits nonnegative fractional weights on such configurations, subject to covering the items.

These fractional weights are not literal fractions of physical bins. Rounding them to whole bins may require extra bins. The disputed modified integer round-up conjecture concerns whether a universal constant controls that extra number.

## What is known

The protected Family 118 catalogue lists one manuscript, *Additive hardness and unbounded configuration gaps in bin packing*.

The pinned publisher overview claims that the integer optimum $\mathrm{OPT}(I)$ and the configuration LP optimum $\mathrm{LP}(I)$ satisfy **unbounded additive separation**: for every constant $C$ there are bin-packing instances $I$ with

$$
\mathrm{OPT}(I)-\mathrm{LP}(I)>C.
$$

This is an additive claim, not the assertion of an unbounded multiplicative ratio. The overview presents the result as a counterexample to the modified integer round-up conjecture of Scheithauer and Terno.

Separately, the overview claims that, for **every fixed additive constant**, approximating the integral optimum within that many bins is **NP-hard**, even if **each item is larger than $1/6$**. Under this promise, every bin can accommodate at most five items because six such items would sum to more than one. The hardness statement and the LP-gap counterexample must not be conflated without checking the reduction and instance-family hypotheses.
