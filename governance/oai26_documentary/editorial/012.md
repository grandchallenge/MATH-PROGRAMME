# OAI-26 family 012: largest prime factors of consecutive integers

## The question in plain language

Every positive integer factors into primes. Its *largest prime factor* records the largest prime appearing in that factorization. Two consecutive integers have no common prime divisor, but this does not automatically mean their largest prime factors behave independently.

The Erdős–Pomerance joint Dickman conjecture concerns the sizes of the largest prime factors of consecutive integers. The pinned OAI-26 overview claims that a September 24, 2026 manuscript establishes their asymptotic independence in ordinary natural density. It also claims that exactly half of positive integers, in the density limit, have a smaller largest prime factor than their successor.

## A small example

The integers 14 and 15 are consecutive. Their largest prime factors are 7 and 5, respectively. For 20 and 21, they are 5 and 7. Individual examples fluctuate, and counting a modest initial interval cannot establish an asymptotic law.

Write the largest prime factor of an integer $n>1$ as $P^{+}(n)$. A natural normalized size is

$$
\frac{\log P^{+}(n)}{\log n}.
$$

The Dickman distribution describes the limiting behavior of smooth numbers, integers whose prime factors are all relatively small. A *joint* Dickman law for $n$ and $n+1$ asks whether the two normalized measurements behave, asymptotically, like two independent samples from that limiting law.

Independence here means a factorization of limiting joint probabilities, not that neighboring integers are arithmetically unrelated.

## What is known

The pinned Family 012 catalogue identifies one manuscript, *The joint Dickman law for consecutive integers* (September 24, 2026). The publisher's source overview attributes two conclusions to it:

- The logarithmic sizes of the largest prime factors of $n$ and $n+1$ converge jointly to independent Dickman-type limits, when integers are counted with ordinary natural density.
- The set of integers satisfying $P^{+}(n)<P^{+}(n+1)$ has natural density one half.

The distinction between ordinary density and logarithmic averages is material. A logarithmically weighted average can be easier to control and is not interchangeable with an ordinary count divided by $X$.

The comparison probability of one half is consistent with an independent, identically distributed continuous limiting pair, where equality has limiting probability zero. However, transferring weak convergence to this strict inequality requires checking that the limiting distribution has no mass on the diagonal and that the normalizations and finite-sample comparison align.

These are the **indexed manuscript claims**. The exact theorem statements, quantified convergence, treatment of $n=1$, and any effective error terms require direct examination of the pinned PDF.
