# OAI-26 family 003: Zero-free half-planes and exceptional real zeros

## The question in plain language

The zeros of the Riemann zeta function encode fluctuations in the distribution of prime numbers. A zero is a complex argument at which the function vanishes. Its real part matters: zeros farther to the right can contribute larger terms to prime-counting error formulas.

The September 30 manuscript claims a fixed empty region to the right of $7/8$, at every height, not just up to a computational cutoff. It makes the same claim for Dirichlet L-functions (which distinguish residue classes of primes) and finite-order Hecke L-functions over the Eisenstein field (which distinguish arithmetic classes of ideals). GCL has successfully checked all three corresponding original Lean theorems, within their formal statements. This is not the Riemann hypothesis, which locates every nontrivial zeta zero on the line with real part $1/2$.

The October 1 companion asks a different question: can an exceptional real zero get arbitrarily close to one relative to the logarithm of its conductor? Its formal theorem has also passed GCL's original-source checks.

## A small example

For real $s=2$, the familiar series gives a nonzero value:

$$
\zeta(2)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}>0.
$$

That point lies in the classical region of absolute convergence. The challenge is to exclude zeros even where the original series cannot be used directly, and uniformly at all imaginary heights. One example, however exact, cannot establish such a statement.

For the exceptional-zero result, a hypothetical real zero at $\beta=1-1/(\log q)^2$ would have $(1-\beta)\log q=1/\log q$. Along conductors tending to infinity, this quantity tends to zero. A uniform positive lower bound rules out such a sequence (not necessarily every individual zero in the interval).

## What is known

### The quasi-Riemann theorem

The September 30 source, Theorem 1.1, asserts nonvanishing throughout $\Re(s)>7/8$ for every finite-order Hecke L-function over $F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$ and every Dirichlet L-function, including zeta, allowing the principal pole at one. The open boundary matters: it does not exclude zeros on the line $7/8$. The October 5 manuscript gives the weaker $11/12$ boundary; the stronger conclusion implies that weaker conclusion, but not independent verification of the October 5 argument.

The Euler product gives classical nonvanishing for $\Re(s)>1$. Classical conductor- and height-dependent zero-free regions approach one; a fixed uniform half-plane is a qualitatively different assertion. The paper's key innovation is a comparison of two representations of a carefully designed sum, not a larger numerical search for zeros.

**Proof architecture (source explanation).** The paper starts with the supremum of the real parts of zeros across primitive finite-order Hecke characters. Assuming it exceeds the target boundary, it seeks a contradiction by continuing the reciprocal L-functions beyond that supremum. A completed cubic-theta sum has two representations: reflection controls its size, while Poisson summation exposes a principal contribution containing the reciprocal of the target L-function. Bounds on the remaining rows and the direct sum enable analytic continuation across the putative boundary.

In the balanced first stage, quadratic and planar additive large-sieve bounds control the reflected sum; a sextic character large sieve and a truncated reciprocal polynomial control the problematic Poisson rows. This gives the $11/12$ threshold. In the refined stage, selected primes compensate unwanted Euler terms, unequal averaging scales improve the power balance, and both reciprocal and ordinary Dirichlet polynomial moments constrain the number of problematic rows. The paper proves these moment bounds by separate recursive arguments with Poisson transformations and reflected energy. Principal residues and exceptional inducing-character families are handled separately. Uniform positive exponent margins yield the $7/8$ threshold.

The final transfer uses the ideal norm and quadratic factorization: the norm-pulled Hecke series factors into the Dirichlet series for a character and its twist by the quadratic character of the Eisenstein field, apart from finitely many local factors. In the positive half-plane the omitted factors cannot create zeros. The principal pole is treated separately. This explains why the Hecke-family proof matters even to the zeta conclusion.

### The uniform Landau–Siegel zero gap

The October 1 manuscript asserts that there is an absolute constant $c>0$ such that for each primitive nonprincipal real Dirichlet character of conductor $q\ge3$ and each real zero $0<\beta<1$,

$$
(1-\beta)\log q\ge c.
$$

It does not supply an explicit numerical value of the constant. Its proof is separate: a hypothetical zero too close to one forces a shortage of primes on which the character is positive. The other primes supply divisibility for a nonzero interpolation determinant built from algebraic conjugates in a biquadratic field. A weighted monomial selection makes Frobenius substitutions reduce row weight and extract prime powers. The source compares the resulting lower divisibility bound with an upper archimedean size bound; the natural degree scale $U=N^{4/3}$ gives $\log N=3\log U/4$, a strict margin. Uniform interpolation and parameter choices make the two bounds incompatible. This is a number-theoretic contradiction, not a count of observed zeros.
