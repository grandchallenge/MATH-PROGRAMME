## The question in plain language

Some familiar infinite sums have values whose arithmetic nature is surprisingly hard to determine. A number is *rational* if it equals a ratio of two integers with nonzero denominator; otherwise it is *irrational*.

Catalan's constant is a positive number built by alternately adding and subtracting reciprocals of odd squares. The OAI-26 Family 005 manuscript, dated September 24, 2026, claims to prove that this constant is irrational. This is an unusually strong assertion about a long-standing problem, and the existence of a manuscript claiming it is not evidence that the argument has been independently accepted.

## A small example

The defining series is

$$
G=1-\frac{1}{3^2}+\frac{1}{5^2}-\frac{1}{7^2}+\cdots
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)^2}.
$$

The alternating-series test gives a simple numerical bound: after truncating at index $N$, the absolute error is at most the magnitude of the next omitted term. So the first few terms yield increasingly accurate rational approximations.

But arbitrarily accurate rational approximations do *not* establish irrationality. Every real number, rational or irrational, admits such approximations. An irrationality proof needs an additional structural argument showing that no fixed fraction can equal the entire infinite sum.

For contrast, the irrationality of the square root of two can be proved by a divisibility contradiction. The same elementary contradiction does not automatically apply to an alternating infinite sum.

The arithmetic problem should not be conflated with being able to compute many digits of the constant, with convergence of the defining series, or with proving that the number is transcendental. Irrationality means only that the value is not a fraction of integers; transcendence would assert more.
