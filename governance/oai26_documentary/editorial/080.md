# OAI-26 family 080: the Sobolev endpoint for Schrödinger convergence

**Editorial status: DRAFT_UNACCEPTED. NO_CERTIFICATION.** This article documents claims from two pinned manuscripts and does not independently verify them.

## The question in plain language

The free Schrödinger equation describes how an initial wave evolves. At time zero its solution should recover the initial function, but for rough initial data it is difficult to show convergence at almost every spatial point as time approaches zero. Carleson's Schrödinger convergence problem asks exactly how much Sobolev smoothness is needed.

The pinned OAI-26 overview claims convergence at the sharp threshold itself, not just for smoother inputs, in every spatial dimension at least two.

## A small example

Write the free evolution of initial data $f$ as $e^{it\Delta}f$. The mathematical question is whether

$$
e^{it\Delta}f(x)\longrightarrow f(x)
\quad\text{as }t\downarrow0
$$

for almost every point $x$. Sobolev space $H^s$ describes functions with $s$ derivatives in an averaged square-integrable sense, including fractional values of $s$.

The indexed critical exponent in dimension $n$ is

$$
s=\frac{n}{2(n+1)}.
$$

For $n=2$, it equals $1/3$. A theorem for all $s>1/3$ would not settle whether convergence holds at $s=1/3$ itself; that equality case is the claimed advance.

## What is known

The source-locked catalogue identifies two manuscripts, both dated September 24, 2026: *Endpoint convergence for the planar Schrodinger equation* and *Endpoint pointwise convergence for the Schrodinger equation in higher dimensions*.

The publisher overview claims that for **every integer dimension $n\ge2$** and **every initial datum** in the critical Sobolev space, the free Schrödinger evolution converges to the datum **almost everywhere as positive time tends to zero**:

$$
f\in H^{n/(2(n+1))}(\mathbb R^n)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{it\Delta}f(x)\to f(x)\ \text{a.e. as }t\downarrow0.
$$

The scope combines a planar endpoint and higher-dimensional endpoint results. Almost-everywhere convergence is not uniform pointwise convergence or convergence at every point, and a positive-time limit is not automatically a statement about arbitrary non-tangential approaches. Exact Fourier conventions and maximal estimates require checking the papers.
