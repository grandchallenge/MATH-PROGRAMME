# OAI-26 family 011: Prime-factor statistics of prime predecessors

**Editorial status: DRAFT_UNACCEPTED. Mathematical status: NO_CERTIFICATION.** The three source manuscripts make ambitious claims about the factorization of $p-1$ for primes $p$; this page documents those claims, not independently verified proofs.

## The question in plain language

For a prime such as $p=31$, the predecessor $p-1=30=2\cdot3\cdot5$ has three distinct prime factors. For $p=17$, the predecessor is $16=2^4$, with only one distinct prime factor but four factors counted with multiplicity. How do these prime factors behave when we look across increasingly many primes?

Family 011 contains three distinct questions: whether many primes have predecessors made only of relatively small prime factors; whether the *sizes* of all prime factors follow a limiting statistical law; and whether some predecessors are squarefree and contain an *even* number of prime factors. The published sources claim striking answers, but the questions should not be treated as equivalent.

## A small example

A positive integer is called *smooth* relative to a bound $y$ if none of its prime factors exceeds $y$. The predecessor $30$ is $5$-smooth; $16$ is $2$-smooth. Smoothness says how **large** the factors can be. Parity says whether the number of factors is odd or even. It does not follow from smoothness.

The Möbius function $\mu(n)$ equals $(-1)^r$ when $n$ is squarefree with $r$ distinct prime factors, and equals zero when $n$ has a squared prime factor. Thus $\mu(p-1)=1$ is exactly the assertion that $p-1$ is squarefree and has an even number of prime factors. These elementary examples do not establish that infinitely many primes satisfy the condition.

## What is known

**Smooth shifted primes and totient fibers.** The September 24 paper *Weighted dilation graphs, smooth shifted primes and totient fibers*, Theorem 1.2, claims that for each fixed $0<\delta<1/4$, at least $x^{1-o(1)}$ primes in $2x<p\le5x$ have largest prime factor of $p-1$ at most $x^\delta$. By choosing a smaller exponent the source extends the conclusion to every fixed positive $\delta$; it does not claim uniformity as $\delta$ tends to zero. Its Theorem 1.1 claims that for every $\varepsilon>0$, infinitely many positive integers $n$ have more than $n^{1-\varepsilon}$ preimages under Euler's totient function. The second assertion is linked to products of primes with smooth predecessors, but it is a separate theorem.

**A distribution for all factor sizes.** The September 24 paper *The Poisson–Dirichlet law for prime predecessors* claims that for a uniformly selected prime $3\le p\le x$, the decreasing list of prime factors of $p-1$, *with multiplicity*, has normalized logarithmic sizes converging in every finite joint distribution to the Poisson–Dirichlet law with parameter one. A limiting **joint distribution** of ranked sizes is more specific than merely obtaining many smooth predecessors. The source attributes the earlier conjecture to Ford, Konyagin, and Luca.

**Even factor-count parity.** The September 17 paper *Prime Predecessors with an Even Number of Prime Factors*, Theorem 1.1, claims infinitely many primes $p$ have $\mu(p-1)=1$. It explicitly requires squarefreeness, so the counting-with-multiplicity and distinct-factor conventions agree on the constructed predecessors. Its introduction invokes dilation-graph and weighted-sieve results from the smoothness companion but says the parity conclusion requires further *signed* correlations, a prime slot, and a Type II distribution argument. The source does not derive parity from smoothness alone.

The three theorems concern the same arithmetic object, $p-1$, but impose genuinely different conditions and use different counting conventions and analytic steps.
