# OAI-26 family 120: almost-linear-time exact graph matching

**Editorial status: DRAFT_UNACCEPTED. NO_CERTIFICATION.** This documentary article describes a claim in the pinned publisher overview, not an independently verified algorithm.

## The question in plain language

A matching in a graph is a collection of edges with no shared endpoints. A *maximum-cardinality matching* contains as many edges as possible. Unlike simply finding a large matching, computing an exact maximum must rule out every possible improvement.

Family 120 reports a randomized algorithm purportedly computing an exact maximum matching in almost linear time even for general, nonbipartite graphs. The reported method also handles prescribed-degree spanning subgraphs.

## A small example

Suppose a graph is a path of four vertices. Its two outer edges form a matching of size two, which is maximum. A greedy method might first choose the middle edge and produce only one matched pair. In general graphs, odd cycles make exact matching harder to analyze than in bipartite graphs.

A prescribed-degree factor asks for a spanning subgraph in which each vertex has an assigned number of incident selected edges. Ordinary matching corresponds to degree at most one, whereas factor problems can demand a range of higher degrees. These outputs must not be confused.

## What is known

The protected Family 120 catalogue identifies one manuscript, *Almost-Linear-Time Maximum-Cardinality Matching in General Graphs*.

The pinned publisher overview claims that every **simple undirected graph** with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges admits a randomized algorithm producing an **exact maximum-cardinality matching** in

$$
(n+m)^{1+o(1)}
$$

**word operations**, with success probability at least $2/3$. It explicitly says that this running-time bound holds **on every computation path**, rather than merely in expectation.

The overview additionally claims the same asymptotic time and success guarantees for producing a **spanning subgraph with prescribed admissible vertex degrees**, or deciding that none exists. The word “admissible” needs its exact definition from the primary paper, especially when a degree prescription is infeasible.

This is a randomized exact-output algorithm, not a deterministic algorithm or a randomized approximation scheme. Its word-operation bound is not automatically a bit-complexity guarantee without an explicit machine-word model.
